OrdersThe brief

The owner gave the committee five base directives and asked it to be as creative as possible inside them.

  1. 1
    Historically accurate set between 1895 and 1925.
  2. 2
    A life sim / RPG of a spy the career, business and private life of a freelance agent as one interlocking resource system (Reilly, Ace of Spies).
  3. 3
    Incredibly deep systems travel (Bradshaw's Guide, 80 Days), deception / conversation / coding, items / artifacts, economy / business, politics / warfare.
  4. 4
    Pressure time-sensitive events in a world that moves without the player, and hidden movement: the player is hunted, as in Scotland Yard.
  5. 5
    An engine efficient and scalable, for dynamic events, decisions, actions and dependencies.

The prohibition

No proposal may be about persons, story, graphics or style. Every idea must be gameplay and systems mechanics, with an argument for why the game would be captivating and addictive to play. Historical institutions, technologies and macro-events may be used as inputs to mechanics; characters, plots, scenes and art direction may not.

C07 · whole-game concept · lead author P01 (Core Loop & Systems Designer)

The Departure Is the Turn

The world is a discrete-event simulation faced by the timetable and the diary; decision points are departures, and every verb sits in the slack between them.

Borda 188/200mean 8.60first-place votes 5ranked between 1st and 5th

One-line pitch

A spy's thirty-year life played as an itinerary: the world is a discrete-event simulation faced by the timetable and the diary, every decision point is a departure, and every verb — meet, cable, forge, trade, bribe, register — takes hours that must fit into the slack before a train that leaves without you.

The hook — what is new

There is no abstract turn and no free time. The world advances through one queue of timestamped events and choices are made in the gaps between scheduled departures, so the connection — arriving to leaving, when you are stationary, visible and spending — is the atomic unit of tension. You plan on the subgraph you know from owned, dated guides and porters, and it goes stale as the network changes. Because the hunters ride and read the same timetable, time pressure and the hunt are the same object as travel.

Core loop

  • Every 30 seconds: Decide what fits before the departure you have provisionally booked — a bank drawing in business hours, the poste restante, a meeting, a cable, or waiting — and choose class, lodging and instrument, each priced in money, hours, health and trace.
  • Every 10 minutes: Assemble a day of time-stamped slots: mail deliveries, bank hours, a 14:05 express, the hotel register that reaches the police tomorrow. A tight connection risks a seasonal delay; a loose one buys dead hours of exposure and expense. Interrupts — a telegram, a late train, a strike rumour — reopen the plan.
  • Every session (1–2 hours): One commission carried across two or three cities on a chain of connections — gauge break, frontier hall, night boat. Every pause sits within sight of a departure, so sessions end aboard a night train with the world and the hunt advancing until next load.
  • Over a campaign (years of game time): The network thickens and accelerates — tunnels, through services, motor cabs, airships, airlines — while borders harden (1914), currencies break (1917, 1923) and your files thicken. Routes are learned, re-bought, sold and forgotten; the same verbs grow faster and riskier; the diary becomes a life.

The systems and how they interlock

  • Travel (network, schedules, fares, classes, traces): A date-versioned, multi-modal master graph of services built from monthly guides. The player routes on a known-edge overlay — learned date, source, confidence — while the simulator executes the truth and emits ghost-connection events when an edition has gone stale. A named berth writes a high-fidelity, persistent record; a third-class ticket reveals only mode and direction. Travel necessarily spends time, money and evidence, so every other system hangs from it.
  • Deception / conversation / coding (C04, C12, C20): Two legends stay apart only while no connection could have carried one body between their recorded appearances — the alibi test is a fastest-path query. Conversations are finer timestamps on the same global queue — never a second scheduler, so total ordering holds across conversation and pursuit; a cipher takes hours by hand or minutes by bureau; a cable leaves an office copy. Every assertion is a record, with an authorship field, in the store the hunters read.
  • Items / artifacts (C06, C19): Papers, codebooks, luggage and crates hold itineraries of their own on the same queue — registered luggage follows the ticket, a crate moves at goods speed. Documents are tested in the frontier hall, a scheduled dwell of fifteen to ninety minutes at a gauge break, and their stamps are dated against the legend's itinerary.
  • Economy / business (C08, C18, C15): Money is instruments with hours and places — a letter of credit draws only at listed correspondents in banking hours; gold is anonymous and seizable. Rent days and maturities are queue events; burn is continuous and income lumpy, so the ledger forces you onto the network. Commissions are located, time-windowed offers made mutually exclusive by travel time: accepting Baku forecloses Tangier.
  • Politics / warfare (C10, C09, C03): The world calendar writes rows into one dated parameter layer the timetable reads: passport requirements per edge, registration coverage, police liaison, requisition and suspension of services. Crises climb ladders with literal deadlines; at general mobilisation military flows claim the civilian graph. Politics is felt as which trains run, which frontiers ask for papers and who may read the register you signed.
  • Time pressure & the world clock: Native. Departures leave without you; windows close; the Meldezettel reaches the police within about a day; a frontier post receives its circular only in office hours and where liaison exists. Three cadences beat against each other — the day of slots, the institutional rhythm of registers and circulars, the macro calendar of crises and seasons.
  • The hunt (hidden movement, evidence, hunters; C17, C05, C13, C16): Hunters never see you; they read records arriving on institutional clocks, hold weighted hypotheses advancing along the timetable you ride, cannot teleport, and cordon a city only where their men can be in time. Their notices move by cable, their bodies by train; the night train outruns tomorrow's newspaper but not tonight's wire. Your own trail is always visible as a self-forecast from your own acts; what they have concluded is not.
  • The life (ageing, health, households, reputation, legends; C11, C04, C08): The diary is also the household's calendar — rent, remittances, club nights — and each kept appointment is a witness record. Health is spent by third class and long legs and recovered in weeks of world time that neither clock nor hunt pauses; ageing lengthens every timer; skills grow by use and rust by disuse; a year-end audit batches what accrued and stamps the coming year's known closures onto the calendar.

A worked example of play

Berlin, 18 October 1905 (New Style). A service's want (C18) pays for copied documents delivered to a handler in St Petersburg within six days; a three-month bill drawn under the same legend matures there on the seventh (C08). The Nord-Express, a first-class train de luxe, runs twice a week — tonight is one of its days — while the ordinary D-Zug to Eydtkuhnen runs daily: running days are as real a constraint as departure hours. The bank closes at four, so the letter of credit is drawn first; the hotel's Meldezettel reaches the Polizeipräsidium tomorrow (C17), after the train has gone. The planner offers the Nord-Express berth — fast, rested, under the legend's name — or second class on the D-Zug, anonymous, a night sitting up: one cost vector (C02). The known-world overlay marks the Wirballen connection at high confidence from the October edition bought that morning; September's showed a withdrawn Sunday working. The evening edition carries strike rumours from the Russian lines, in Gregorian dates — the disruption layer's forecast — so the plan buys a day of buffer and a fallback: a Stettin–Petersburg steamer edge learned from a shipping agent's cable, still open before the ice. At the frontier everyone changes trains; the passport hall records the name presented and exchanges the passport for a residence permit, so leaving will need an exit permit — the leg is gated at both ends. The self-forecast (C13) shows the Berlin description reaching the post only if the Prussian police have cabled, and only in office hours. Then the scheduled Tier-0 event fires: over 20–26 October the railway strike spreads across Russia — 7–13 October in the Julian dates of Petersburg's papers and registers, the thirteen-day gap that is the calendar tell — and closes the Russian graph edge by edge. The sleeper is stranded; the steamer sails. The player who bought the buffer reaches Petersburg by sea, meets the bill and finds the handler's window shut by a day — the lapsed offer reports a rival delivered. The player who did not sees the bill protested and gazetted, and that name's heat rise in a city they are locked inside.

Why it is captivating and addictive

A provisionally booked departure is an open loop: until the errands that justify it are done, the booking stays mentally active (Zeigarnik), and because the board is always visible, effort rises as the departure nears (goal gradient), so the player rarely pauses. Near-misses — the connection made or lost by eleven minutes — motivate only because the margin was the player's own slack: delay odds are shown before commitment, so every miss is attributable; an unforecast miss would be frustration, not a hook. Mastery lives in the head — slack, buffers and the known network are skills the veteran feels, not stats — and it has a loss half: editions go stale on the period's own cadence (1 May, 1 October, the wartime suspension of the Guide), so route knowledge rots on a schedule and re-engagement is honest and dated. Approach–avoidance: the sleeper you want and the register you fear are one purchase. Information gap: the evening edition, the stale guide and the frontier's unknown demands must be closed. The time jump is itself anticipation: the diary shows what will pass before time advances, and a sealed leg's arrival dumps the accumulated news as a reveal. Emergent narrative: alignments of the three cadences — a deadline, a frontier closure and a cordon in one week — are set pieces nobody wrote. The stopping points are the hotel register (costed, not penalised), the night train (the designed end: the 23:40 reaches Berlin before the bourse opens, so the player stops aboard it, on a cliffhanger the schedule wrote), the arrival after a sealed leg (a reveal) and the year-end audit, which players pass through because the coming year's known closures stamped onto the calendar are anticipation, not obligation. "One more leg" is tied to a visible departure, never to a timer.

Historical grounding

Accuracy is the rules. The master graph is built from period guides — Bradshaw's monthly Continental Guide, the Reichskursbuch, the Indicateur Chaix — whose editions change at seasonal changeovers (typically 1 May and 1 October). In wartime only Bradshaw's Continental Guide ceased; the Reichskursbuch and the Chaix printed reduced Kriegsfahrplan and horaire de guerre services, so wartime edge data come from the national guides — the network thins, it does not go unpublished. Times pass through local regimes: Russian railways run on St Petersburg time, Russian registers keep the Julian calendar until 31 January 1918, France keeps Paris time until 1911 — a forged date in the wrong calendar is a tell. At the gauge breaks (Eydtkuhnen/Wirballen, Alexandrowo; Irun, Port Bou) passengers change trains in a hall of passport desks, customs and a telegraph office. Registration is a row per jurisdiction and date: Meldezettel in Germany and Austria, innkeepers' registers in France, passport desks in Russian hotels; in Britain nobody is registered before August 1914 (aliens from 5 August 1914, British subjects from the National Registration Act 1915, general hotel registers from 1920). The network is the progression: Simplon 1906, Lötschberg 1913; Trans-Siberian through running 1903–04, the Baikal gap closed 1904, regular Circum-Baikal service 1905, the Amur line 1916; DELAG airships 1910–14, the London–Paris airline 1919; the Orient Express suspended 1914, the Simplon-Orient Express 1919. Disruption is dated: Baltic ice closes the Petersburg approach while Libau stays open; strikes in Russia (October 1905), France (1910) and Britain (1911, 1919); civilian trains suspended in Germany and France in early August 1914; the wartime Haparanda–Torneå route to Russia. Passport-free Western Europe before 1914, Russian and Ottoman passport regimes, universal controls from 1914 and the League's 1920 booklet are edge attributes with validity windows.

Engine: dynamic events, decisions, actions, dependencies

One global priority queue of timestamped events with total ordering (time, priority, insertion id). Trains, mails, bank hours, register deliveries, maturities, liaison dates, crisis rungs, hunter moves and the player's intentions are all events; entities subscribe to event types; handlers are data. Every stochastic draw is seeded, so a save is seed plus input log plus year-boundary snapshots (C11): replay is exact from any snapshot without replaying thirty years — which makes C16's autopsy and C10's inquest computable and is the debugging tool. Timetables are monthly tables (edge, calendar bitmap, departure, arrival, class, fare, supplement) — the only tables versioned per edition; everything else versions by date rows. Routing is connection-scan over the time-expanded graph; earliest-arrival profiles are computed on demand and memoised per (origin, hour bucket, period) on the hierarchical graph, so each hunter propagation step costs microseconds from cached profiles — a full node × hour × node precomputation would run to hundreds of megabytes per monthly edition. Level-of-detail scheduling runs the player's city and legs in flight at minute granularity and the rest at day granularity: a quiet winter passes in minutes, a closing cordon takes an hour. The known-world overlay is a sparse per-agent set of {edge, learned date, source, confidence}; the planner routes on it, the simulator executes the truth, and hunters route, propagate hypotheses (C05) and test alibis (C04) on their own overlays, never on ground truth. Node dwell — the connection — emits records consumed by C17, C02 (exposure) and C08 (expense). The communications graph (C13) is a second edge table of latencies, office hours and jurisdiction gating over which records reach their readers — C17's lag model. The dated parameter layer (C10) supplies edge requirements, registration coverage, requisition and liaison as rows keyed by (edge or jurisdiction, date, tier); a new decade adds rows, not code. The other nineteen candidates attach as event types and handlers on this queue; none runs without it.

Progression and endings (as systems)

What grows: the known network (edges learned, confidence raised, corridors saleable), slack mastery in the player's head, each legend's record-set and credit, skills exercised. What decays: editions at every changeover; unused skills toward a floor; unattended legends; health spent by cheap travel and recovered in world time. What accumulates: records in institutional ledgers, debts with maturities, dossiers per name, a file the alliance calendar may one day copy to a new reader. Endings are system states reached on the timetable (C11): retirement is reaching a non-extraditing haven with wealth in a currency that still converts before 1914, 1917 or 1923 slams a door; capture is a cordon closing on a city whose next departure you cannot make; ruin is a protested bill gazetted under the name you wear; succession keeps the world running and hands the next agent every debt and file; routes and codes persist only in the player's head.

Risks and mitigations

  • "A skeleton, not a motive engine" (P07; echoed by P05, P06, P10). True by design: C07 is the clock; the motive engines are arranged around it — C18's windowed, exclusive commissions and C08's burn and maturities are the reasons to leave the hotel, C01's reconciliation dates the reckonings. Rule: every city-day has guaranteed claimants for its slack — C18's board refreshes with each edition and C08's rent days and maturities fall on the calendar — because an empty slot is dead time, the one state in which this design loses the player.
  • Spreadsheet feel (P01.01's own risk). Strong defaults — "next sensible departure", automatic edition purchase at stations, a porter — make planning depth opt-in; the diary, not a Gantt chart, is the face.
  • Punishing randomness (P04.02). Delay distributions are shown before commitment; a forecast channel (editions, agents' cables, insiders) always exists, so buffer is priced.
  • Skipping one's life (P02.01). The diary shows what will pass before time jumps; interrupts stop the jump; conversation and pursuit are finer-grained events on the same queue.
  • Off-screen death (P09.01). Deferred leg resolution never kills without warnings visible before boarding; the hotel pause stays cheap enough to be a real choice.
  • Unfair ghost connections (P04.01, P03.07). Confidence cues on every known edge; staleness bites only at changeovers and wartime suspensions; recovery options always exist.
  • Dead time on long legs (P04.10). A sealed leg is a queue event carrying an action budget — ciphers, forgery, letters, conversation — and news arrives only at ports or by wireless, so arrival is a reveal.
  • Data volume and accuracy. Model the trunk network exactly from period guides and abstract minor lines to regional defaults; dates are table rows, cheap to audit and fix.

One-line pitch

Several intelligence services and commercial principals pay you at once, each holding its own model of who you are and what you know; the game is to retire before those models are reconciled.

The hook — what is new

Every paymaster is a faction with a belief store: facts about you, each tagged with provenance and confidence, built only from what you delivered, what it observed and what its other sources corroborate. Your product is the report, composed from tokens you actually hold plus fabrications, each fabrication booked as a consistency debt that falls due on a real date. Stores are compared only at dated reconciliation events — each service's audits and the liaison channels the diplomatic calendar opens in 1904, 1907, 1914 and 1917 — so the hunt is born from your own inconsistencies on a schedule you can read in the newspapers. And the loop closes on itself: every success credited to a service raises its annual vote, funding both the prices it pays you and its counter-intelligence capacity — your deliveries pay for the hunters who will one day read you.

Core loop

  • Every 30 seconds: In the slack between departures on C07's clock: read a requirement, decide which fragment goes to which service and which gets chickenfeed, pick the cipher that service reads (C20), and compose a report from held tokens — C17 records you own or claims you invent — with each outstanding lie's maturity date beside the composer.
  • Every 10 minutes: A city day: meet a handler (the meeting writes a record under the legend that attends), deliver, and see the delivery scored for novelty and corroboration after a verification lag; take payment in a name-bound instrument (C08); service a lie that matures this week, or roll it over and let the principal compound.
  • Every session (1–2 hours): One requirement cycle across two or three capitals: acquire a document, carry it through a frontier whose regime the date sets (C06), deliver it under one legend and a marked copy under another, then survive an audit whose questions leak what the store believes (C12, C16). Sessions end on a night train with a reckoning in sight.
  • Over a campaign (years of game time): The alliance calendar schedules the reckonings: 1904 and 1907 open channels between employers; October 1909 adds a British buyer and a British hunter; August 1914 fuses Entente counter-intelligence and arms enemy services with wartime powers; March 1917 abolishes a Russian service and spills part of its files; 1923 links Europe's police by circular — liaison edges with delay, never a merged store. Budgets grow with your credited successes until a store flips to "double", pay stops and the hunt begins — unless you have retired the legend or the life (C11).

The systems and how they interlock

  • Travel (network, schedules, fares, classes, traces): Requirements are located and time-windowed, handlers sit in specific capitals and liaison hubs move with the calendar — Paris and London before the war, Rotterdam, Copenhagen, Berne and Madrid inside it — so the three-service week is a routing problem on C07's timetable. Every meeting writes a C17 record under the legend that travelled; carried documents are examined at the change of train at the Russian gauge break and at every crossing once passports return in August 1914.
  • Deception / conversation / coding: Reports are token-based, never free text. Each service has its own cipher and legend; a reused keyword is a signature that can link two legends. A lie is a liability with a principal (distance from truth), a holder and a maturity (the world-clock event that tests it): service it, roll it over or buy it back in rapport. Handlers' questions, played as C12's claims and calls, are the only direct window on a store.
  • Items / artifacts: Deliverables are documents with provenance (C06): originals, photographed copies carrying distinguishing marks, forgeries that pass until cross-checked. Each class has a per-faction value curve (C18) rising toward its crisis and collapsing when superseded or published — construction tables in the naval race, deployment timetables in the Balkan Wars, every secret treaty on its publication in November 1917. Selling twice pays twice and leaves two marked copies in two stores.
  • Economy / business: Retainers, bonuses and audited expenses are paid in instruments drawn under a legend's name (C08); a commission house is a first-class paymaster whose contracts excuse your presence and whose two books launder service money into trade income (C15). Payment follows corroboration: a fabricated delivery is paid provisionally and reverses as credit loss and suspicion. Each service's annual vote is a Tier 1 output of the Tier 2 outcomes credited to it, scaling its prices and its watchers and cables (C05).
  • Politics / warfare: Services and ministries are factions whose requirement generators read their worries from the world state — naval construction 1906–1912, deployment plans 1912–1914, neutral shipping and censorship from 1915 — and whose goals are confined to Tiers 1 and 2 (C10): no service can prevent a war; every service wants the contracts and the credit inside one. Liaison edges open partially and late when states align (C03), close when they fall out and pass incomplete files to successor regimes; war mode (C09) changes what is bought and who hunts.
  • Time pressure & the world clock: Three rhythms beat against each other: C07's day clock of departures and bank hours, each service's audit cadence, and the liaison calendar whose dates are Tier 0 and therefore foreseeable. Requirements expire with their crises; a lie's maturity and the yearly vote are scheduled events. The reckoning is in the papers before it is in your ledger.
  • The hunt (hidden movement, evidence, hunters): The hunter is the patron. A flipped store becomes a C05 hunter whose dossier is everything you ever told it, funded by the budget your deliveries built. Reconciliation between two services that know you under two names is itself a linkage event (C04) when the stores share a signature. Hunters read only C17 records, so capture is auditable in C16's autopsy: you see which report hanged you.
  • The life (ageing, health, households, reputation, legends): Each service relationship is bound to a legend with its own household, accounts, cipher and burn (C04, C08); retainers are the life's income and lapse when a service's worries move on. Thirty years accumulate stores that never forget while ageing (C11) lengthens every recovery. Retirement is the ending the loop is built toward: a stable currency, a non-extraditing haven and a name no store has flipped.

A worked example of play

March 1913, the First Balkan War in its last phase. The player holds a retainer from the Foreign Section of the British Secret Service Bureau under one legend, one from the French Deuxième Bureau under another, and a commission agreement with a Belgian société anonyme brokering Balkan artillery orders under a third. In Vienna, through a cultivated railway clerk, he obtains a fragment of Austro-Hungarian deployment tables, a document whose value curve has climbed since October 1912 and will break when the war it concerns ends. He sells the original to the French and a photographed copy, carrying the original's marginal stamp, to the British — having told the British he had no Vienna access, while his hotel's Meldezettel under the British-facing legend reached the Polizeidirektion within a day. Late May 1913 brings a supersession event: a compromise of Austro-Hungarian plans becomes public, the Evidenzbureau revises them, every held copy's value falls to zero, and both services now hold the same report from two sources; the marked copy raises suspicion in both stores. The quarterly British audit asks about Vienna; the lie matures; he rolls it over with a supporting lie about a courier. His credited 1913 successes raise the Bureau's 1914 vote, which pays for an officer to check its freelancers' hotel registers. In August 1914 the Entente channel fuses the two stores: the marked copy and a Playfair keyword reused across both ciphers link the two legends; the British store flips, pay stops, a hunter takes the file with the powers the legend's papers select — the Aliens Restriction Act of 5 August if the British-facing legend is foreign, the Defence of the Realm Act of 8 August if it is British-papered — and from the autumn of 1914 the Belgian firm's books come under German sequestration in occupied Brussels. The remaining moves are the system's: reach Rotterdam and buy back the debt with a corroborated truth, or retire both legends and live on the third.

Why it is captivating and addictive

The engine is sustained dramatic irony: the player always knows more than any one faction and must keep each faction's picture coherent — the cognitive load of spinning plates whose collisions are foreseeable but never fully preventable. The near-misses are under the player's control: a leak that almost crossed a threshold came from a lie the player chose, so it motivates mastery rather than gambling. Each added master is a self-set difficulty slider raising income and tension together, and the budget loop means every credited success funds the hunters who will read you, so difficulty rises by the player's own achievement, never by rubber-banding — persisting consequence by construction. Dread and relief alternate honestly: dread climbs toward audits whose cadence is learnable (the example's quarterly British audit) and toward liaison dates that are Tier 0 — known dates with unknown content, scheduled uncertainty rather than diffuse dread — and relief comes when a report is paid after its verification lag, a delayed, legible event rather than a click, honest because the lag is institutional; loss aversion attaches to trust built over years with a patron. The maturing-lie ledger and the dated reckonings are open loops: Zeigarnik tension without an authored cliffhanger, because the calendar that threatens you is history. Mastery lives in the player's head — which services talk, when, and what each checks — and the emergent narrative is betrayal by one's own inconsistencies, so every exposure is a story the player wrote. The natural stopping points are the relief after a paid delivery and the night before an audit; players push through because the next reckoning is visible, the lie that matures tomorrow is theirs to service, and C07's night train turns stopping into anticipation.

Historical grounding

The career is the touchstone's own: freelance and semi-official agents paid per report by several services, often doubling as commission agents, drawing retainers and audited expenses from annual secret-service votes — the British Vote a fixed parliamentary sum of about £50,000 a year before the war, the Bureau beginning on roughly £7,000 — through payment channels that are themselves records with readers: the Bureau's audited vouchers, Abteilung IIIb's postal orders via Dutch intermediaries, the Okhrana's cash from Paris. The buyers are the period's institutions: before October 1909 Britain's Admiralty and War Office intelligence departments, then the Secret Service Bureau with its Home and Foreign sections; the Deuxième Bureau; the Evidenzbureau; Abteilung IIIb; the Russian General Staff's intelligence and the Okhrana's Paris bureau, which worked closely with the French police until the Okhrana's abolition in March 1917, when its Petrograd files were partly burned and partly published. The reckonings are real dates: the Entente Cordiale of 8 April 1904 and the Anglo-Russian Convention of 31 August 1907, along which cooperation grew partially and late; Entente intelligence liaison from August 1914; the Aliens Restriction Act of 5 August and the Defence of the Realm Act of 8 August 1914 as the hunters' new powers; the Vienna police commission of 1923. What is bought changes inside the rules: dreadnought tables during the 1906–1912 race and the 1909 scare, deployment timetables during the Balkan Wars, shipping schedules from 1915, and nothing for a secret treaty once the Bolsheviks publish it in November 1917. Travel obeys the errata: passengers change trains at the Russian gauge break; Western Europe is passport-free until the decrees of late July and August 1914; nobody is registered in Britain before August 1914, so London is where a legend is invented and Vienna, with its Meldezettel, where one dies. Money is gold-standard until 1914; a retainer in marks is worthless by 1923.

Engine: dynamic events, decisions, actions, dependencies

The faction is one entity type: a belief store, a budget, a requirements generator, an audit cadence and liaison edges. The store is a C17 view — tuples of (subject, predicate, value, confidence, source, time, authorship, readers) — holding only facts about entities of interest: the player's identities, documents and claims. A world record's readers are derived at query time from its source institution and C03's cooperation matrix on that date, so the 1914 fusion and the 1917 spill open old records to readers that did not exist when they were emitted. A report appends claim tokens with authorship marked and confidence set by trust; world records (a hotel slip, a cable copy) enter the same store through C17's lag-and-reader model. Consistency debt lives on C07's one global queue, never a queue of its own: each claim's test event is an entry keyed by its world-clock trigger, so total ordering and exact replay hold; when it fires, truth-distance against the world log above a threshold emits suspicion scaled by the product of the two confidences. Reconciliation runs only at events — audits, and liaison edges opened by Tier 0 alliance rows in C10's parameter layer — iterating shared subjects between two stores, so cost is linear in shared facts and events number hundreds to low thousands per campaign (audit cadence × ten to fifteen factions × thirty years, plus liaison openings) — trivial; between events the stores are inert. Matching signatures (copy marks, cipher keywords, handwriting) across two reconciled stores emit a linkage candidate to C04. The budget loop is a yearly event per faction: vote = f(credited Tier 2 outcomes, scare level), allocating requirement prices and C05's watcher and cable budgets; a flipped store instantiates a C05 hunter whose dossier is the store itself. Everything is deterministic from seed plus input log on C07's global queue, so C16's autopsy shows the exact report, audit and liaison date behind a flip. Services are authored as data rows — requirement templates keyed to world-state predicates, audit cadences, cipher classes, liaison edges with open date, delay and fidelity, value curves per document class — so a new service is a row and some edges, and the expensive reasoning, hunter inference, stays in C05 and budget-bounded.

Progression and endings (as systems)

What grows: live masters, trust and retainer level per service, the prices a rising vote pays, the network of handlers and cover addresses, and the player's knowledge of who talks to whom and when. What decays: trust without deliveries, a service's need for you as its worries move, every document's value along its curve. What accumulates and never decays: the stores — everything you ever told each service — rolled-over consistency debt, credited successes turned into hunter budgets, and signatures shared across legends. The end states are C11's, reached mechanically: retirement when an unflipped legend holds wealth in a stable currency in a non-extraditing haven, the valuation visible beforehand (roubles in 1916 are a readable mistake); a flip followed by capture, survivable — expulsion before 1914, a court-martial inside the war — and explained in the autopsy; turning, when the service that caught you runs you as its double from the hunter's chair (C05's inverse mode); ruin when retainers lapse faster than the burn; death or succession, the heir inheriting households, debts and a two-subject file. The flip is a ramp, not a cliff: suspicion rises through audits and liaison events and is readable beforehand through C16's windows (audit questions, interrogation patterns, the press), so every flip is preceded by warnings the player could have acted on. Every year the loop asks: one more report, or stop while the ledgers are still unreconciled.

C04 · whole-game concept · lead author P10 (Life-Sim & Character Progression Designer)

The Legend Portfolio: Several Lives at Once

Legends are the unit of account: they keep running in other cities whether or not you wear them, and the hunt advances only by linking two names.

Borda 181/200mean 8.30first-place votes 2ranked between 1st and 4th

One-line pitch

Legends are the unit of account: each cover identity owns its own flat, bank account, debts, standing and police file, keeps running in its city whether or not you are wearing it, and the hunt advances only by linking two names.

The hook — what is new

The player is never the unit the game accounts for; the name is. A legend is an entity owning a fact-graph of claims whose weights decay unless fed by acts under that name, plus a residence, accounts, obligations, a monthly burn, a standing per circle and one dossier per hunting service. Hunters never see a body, only records under names, so pursuit is a linkage problem rather than a chase, and the canonical link is decidable: two names whose records could have been produced by one body on the timetable the hunter knows (C07) are a link candidate, while records that no connection known to that hunter could reconcile keep the names apart. Failure is therefore granular. You lose a name, not the game.

Core loop

  • Every 30 seconds: Choose which name acts, and how: sign the hotel slip as A or B; pay from A's letter of credit or in anonymous gold; post from B's accommodation address or by hand. A linkage-risk indicator computed from your own records warns before you arrive as A on a ticket B bought.
  • Every 10 minutes: A day in one city under one name: feed its record-set (a bank drawing, a club lunch, a registration renewed), collect what piled up at its address, read the own-trail view of what this name has written lately, and decide whether it stays live, rests or retires.
  • Every session (1–2 hours): A circuit. Plan by timetable so that unattended lives do not go stale (rent on the quarter-day, unanswered mail, a lapsed registration) and so that the last record of one name and the first record of the next are provably incompatible for a single body. Arrive and face what the simulation materialises, at least one opportunity for every obligation: an offer at the club or an introduction at a fed address beside the fines and the creditor's letter, because arrivals are this design's reveals.
  • Over a campaign (years of game time): Legends appreciate (record-set depth, credit, standing) and accumulate exposure (dossier size, links); August 1914 makes nationality a life-or-death attribute and February 1915 binds one face to every British name it is issued under; names are burnt, retired, sold or linked, the files thicken, and the campaign ends when one clean name reaches a haven (C11).

The systems and how they interlock

  • Travel (network, schedules, fares, classes, traces): the circuit is a timetable plan on C07's scheduler. Verification latency is routing: a Berlin employer answers a hunter's inquiry in a day, an overseas registry in weeks, so a claim is safe for as long as the mails take. The timetable is also the alibi: C14's habit profiles and a fastest-path query between two presence records, run on the hunter's known graph, decide whether two names can be one body. Class must match the legend (C02); frontiers test the presented name's papers (C06).
  • Deception / conversation / coding: every assertion in a room (C12), on a hotel form or in a report (C01) is a claim written to the acting legend's fact-graph; claims and world records are one C17 tuple differing only in authorship. The legend ledger lists each name's claims, holders, verification cost and corroboration. Each name reports in its own cipher (C20); a captured cipher is a link.
  • Items / artifacts: papers, visiting cards, letters of introduction, monogrammed luggage, tailors' labels and typewriters belong to a name and can betray it: one hand in two registers, one trunk under two names, one typeface in two correspondences. Folded options: a seizable notebook as the only private record in hard mode (P05.12), and a bounded donor identity, a vanished person's record-set plus a small hot set of acquaintances (P05.11).
  • Economy / business: one ledger per legend (C08). Each name has a class with a monthly burn and a credibility that tracks spend against class; underfunded names decay, overfunded ones attract thieves. Credit is sized to record-set depth, so a thin name cannot borrow enough to make burning it pay, and a burned name's creditor becomes a budgeted private hunter seeking the link to your other names. Households anchor a legend: rent, staff with discretion and loyalty values, safe house, mail drop, cache, and a talk rate that writes witness records; a firm (C15) is a name's visible means of support.
  • Politics / warfare: nationality is a legend attribute read by the regime layer (C10): internment of enemy-alien men of military age, call-up papers, property under custodians from 1914, expropriation from 1917. Liaison is a linkage source: when allied services compare files at a dated reconciliation (C01, C03) and know you under different names, the comparison itself can join them; 1917 spills a fallen service's archive.
  • Time pressure & the world clock: records age and witnesses forget, so links decay; verification clocks run only while someone checks; rent and bills fall on calendars; registration slips reach the police on institutional lags; the document regime flips on fixed dates (31 July and August 1914, February and November 1915, 1920).
  • The hunt (hidden movement, evidence, hunters): each service holds one dossier per name as a filtered view of C17, and C05's hypotheses propagate per name. Linkage events: a witness who has seen both faces, a draft endorsed from one name to another, a photograph matched across files, gossip crossing a bridging institution where one person sits in two circles, a liaison reconciliation, or presence records the timetable can reconcile. Burning a legend zeroes its heat but forfeits its accounts, contracts, households and friendships and hands every asserted fact to the hunters; destroying the one linking record can sever a link instead.
  • The life (ageing, health, households, reputation, legends): standing is held per name per circle (a mess, a bourse, an émigré colony), decays toward neutral with time and distance, and gossip crosses bridges at a rate, so a scandal under one name surfaces elsewhere weeks later. Names do not age; the one body behind them does (C11): unchanging portrait-parlé fields (height, ears, scars) and wounds (C02) leak across names into every dossier at once. Retirement (C11) needs a name clean enough to live under.

A worked example of play

Spring 1914. Name A is a British-papered textile traveller: a London accommodation address (nobody is registered in Britain before August 1914), a bank account with a letter of indication, a club, weekday second-class trips to manufacturing towns. Name B is a mining engineer on a second British passport from the lax pre-war declaration system, with a polizeiliche Anmeldung filed at the precinct and indexed at the Einwohnermeldeamt, a flat, a housekeeper, a Berlin account and standing in a technical society; the Berlin police hold a thin dossier on B since an inquiry agency found no such engineer in any directory. A must draw its letter of credit in London on Wednesday; B was recorded at its Berlin bank on Tuesday afternoon. The fastest Berlin–London connection via the Hook of Holland night boat takes about a day, so the records are incompatible for one body and the engine keeps the names apart; the housekeeper also pays B's quarter-day rent in cash, a dated receipt placing B in Berlin again, at the price that she now knows the pattern and her loyalty becomes a target. In late July the papers and cable volumes signal the ladder (C09); before the civilian timetable collapses the player must retire B cleanly, filing its Abmeldung with a forwarding address and closing flat and account over days the hunt does not pause (a name that merely vanishes leaves a police query instead), or burn it, handing the Berlin police B's specimen signature, its Anmeldung and the housekeeper's testimony that B's mail went to a London address, now a link candidate to A. From the autumn of 1914, B's nationality puts its flat and account under compulsory administration as enemy property and its wearer liable to internment, as British civilian men were from November 1914; from February 1915 renewing either passport binds the player's photograph to both names in one archive (C03), a link waiting for a review.

Why it is captivating and addictive

The hook is honest in P09's sense: every pressure is an institution's real clock, never a manufactured timer. Endowment and the IKEA effect do the work: a legend built over months of registrations, references and trades is a genuine loss when burnt, one the hunt can inflict without ending the game, and choosing which name dies is rich in anticipated regret. Plate-spinning gives sustained engagement with legible failure: three to five lives, each with a rent day, a maturing claim and a lapsing registration, produce deadline planning on the period's calendar: a quarter-day in Petersburg is an in-game constraint to route around, not a summons. The double life is a fantasy made mechanical: trusted in one circle and dangerous in another under different names, juggling the player can see. Dread of linkage is an information gap, since only C16's windows say which records a service has read. A new legend offers the clean-slate appeal that makes roguelikes compulsive, inside one continuous career. Risk is previewed before commitment: the linkage-risk indicator shows, before you arrive as A on B's ticket, what a service holding both records could join, so every link is attributable and every escape earned; visible odds before the act make dread agency, not anxiety. Natural stopping points are arrivals, when a city's mail materialises, the end of a circuit, and the burning of a legend; players push through because a departure is already in sight (C07), a verification clock is running on yesterday's claim, and an unattended life decays on a slow, legible, delegable scale: rent on quarter-days, mail unanswered for weeks, a registration lapsing on a known date, all shown in the ledger and delegable to staff who leak, a planning constraint rather than invisible rot. Above all, failure is granular: a disaster ends a name, not the career, and a new legend's fresh start keeps the player playing after the worst night of the campaign.

Historical grounding

Accuracy is the rule set. Where a name can be invented is a property of the registration regime: Britain is where people are invented, Germany where inventions die. German and Austrian Meldewesen send a slip to the police within a day and keep resident cards; French innkeepers keep police registers; Russian hotels carry the passport to the police desk and yard-keepers report to the precinct; in Britain nobody is registered before August 1914 (aliens from the Aliens Restriction Order of 5 August 1914, British subjects from the National Registration Act 1915, general hotel registers from the Aliens Order 1920), so a British legend can be invented until 1915 and a foreign one only until August 1914. Hunters audit legends with the period's tools: inquiry agencies, professional and consular registers queried by post or cable, portrait-parlé cards, fingerprint files from 1901, typeface comparison, laundry marks and tailors' labels. The document regime changes inside the rules: passport-free Western Europe against Russian and Ottoman visas before 1914; German passports by Bundesrat decree of 31 July 1914 and French ones in the first days of August, so the German edges flip a few days before the French; British photographs from February 1915 and compulsory passports from November 1915; the 1920 League booklet. Internment is staged (selective from August 1914, general in Germany from November 1914 and in Britain from May 1915), enemy property passes to custodians, and 1917–18 expropriates every Russian account and household (banks nationalised and safes opened from December 1917, housing requisitioned through 1918). Per the Charter errata, alibi arithmetic uses a change of train at the gauge break and Trans-Siberian through running from 1903–04.

Engine: dynamic events, decisions, actions, dependencies

The identity is an entity with components: a fact-graph of claims (C17 tuples with weight and timestamp), residences (household entities with staff, cache and talk rate), instruments and obligations (C08), registrations, a nationality, a standing vector per circle, papers (C06 items) and a derived heat per service. Every act emits identity-tagged records into the one append-only store; a dossier is an indexed filter over it, so hunters pay nothing to keep files. Linkage is a rule pass run per service, over that service's delivered view only, when a new record reaches it: a link candidate exists only for a service holding both records, never over ground truth, which would break C17's invariant. Rules: shared handwriting or typeface ids, shared object ids (a trunk, an endorsement chain), witness co-presence, photograph matches, circle-overlap propagation, liaison reconciliation, and timetable compatibility as a fastest-path query on that hunter's known graph (C07), with earliest-arrival profiles cached per origin, hour bucket and timetable period, since compatibility depends on the first record's time, not the city pair alone; the player's indicator runs the same query on the public timetable as the worst case. Unattended identities run at low level of detail: their scheduled records (rent receipts, renewals, lapsed registrations) are emitted on time into the store, so a hunter who queries them finds them (the worked example's cash receipt exists the day it is paid); only texture, which callers and what letters, is deferred and materialised deterministically from the seed on arrival, so cost follows attention, not the number of lives. Gossip crossings between circles are scheduled events on the one queue and standing decay is evaluated lazily on query; nothing in this spine runs per tick. Verification clocks are lazy, spawned only when a hunter checks a claim, with latency from the communications and travel graphs. Burning cascades through a dependency graph (contacts, accounts and households bind to the name), and every link event carries provenance, so C16's autopsy replays exactly which record joined two names. Content is declarative: fact types, link rules, registration coverage and lag per jurisdiction and date (rows of C10's parameter layer), class burn tables, institution templates and donor record-set generators. Determinism is C07's: seed plus input log replays the portfolio exactly.

Progression and endings (as systems)

What grows: record-set depth, credit, standing, households, and the player's knowledge of each country's disclosure texture. What decays: unfed claim weights, standing toward neutral, links as witnesses forget, registrations as they lapse. What never decays: dossiers, archives, the body's description fields. A legend has its own end states: rested (cheap, decaying), retired cleanly (slow, expensive), sold (a clean legend is an item with provenance), burnt (instant; every fact becomes evidence) or linked (its dossier merges into another's). The life's end states are C11's, each a query over the portfolio: retirement holds only for a name whose heat is below threshold in every service with reach into the chosen haven and whose assets sit in a stable currency; capture is a dossier that reconstructed the body behind the names; succession passes standings held by names an organisation knew.

Risks and mitigations

  • Cognitive load. Cap live identities at three to five; onboard with one life and add a second only when the first has a record-set worth protecting; the legend ledger must be the best interface in the game.
  • Upkeep as chores. Every arrival materialises at least one opportunity per obligation (callers, offers, introductions at a fed address); maintenance is delegable to staff who leak; bookkeeping aggregates per name.
  • Legend hoarding and borrow-then-burn. Burn by class, reactivation events for dormant names, credit sized to record-set depth, creditors who become hunters.
  • Hunter omniscience or arbitrariness. Links come only from records, reachability is noisy, every capture has an autopsy, and pre-photograph face matching must stay a filing operation weaker than a photograph.
  • Pre-1914 may feel easy. Russia and Ottoman lands are high-paper regions; archives make early carelessness expensive later (C03).
  • The panel ranked the clock first. Rightly: this concept assumes C07 as the scheduler and C17 as the record store and attaches to both without modification.
  • Taste. Donor identities stay institutional record-sets, never authored lives.

The Paper Hunter: One Evidence Engine

Never observed, only recorded: every act writes a record into an institutional ledger with coverage, lag and fidelity; hunters read only what reached them; every other hunt candidate reads this store.

Borda 155/200mean 7.80first-place votes 0ranked between 2nd and 10th

One-line pitch

You are never observed, only recorded: every act of a spy's life writes a record into some institution's book — hotel slip, berth sheet, cable copy, bank counterfoil, frontier stamp — and the services hunting you read only what reached their desks, so the game is deciding what the paper may say.

The hook — what is new

Pursuit games show the hunter a position; this one shows the hunter a filing cabinet. Every record is one tuple — subject, predicate, value, confidence, source institution, time, authorship — and who may read it is not stored but derived, on the day of asking, from C03's cooperation matrix and the archive's survival; a service's dossier on you is only the records that reached it through its own jurisdiction and liaison channels. Lies, reports and forged fields are the same tuple with a different authorship, so deception and the hunt are one system; every deduction is derived from the log, so every capture can be replayed and learned from. The craft is bookkeeping under fear: which name signs, which book it lands in, how long it lives there, who will open it.

Core loop

  • Every 30 seconds: The convenient act writes a record; the awkward one writes none. First class and a grand hotel put your name on a berth sheet and a police slip; third class and gold coin write nothing and cost sleep. A cable leaves a copy at the office for months; a letter takes three days and, in pre-war Western Europe, leaves nothing — in Russia the black cabinets open watch-listed mail, and from August 1914 every belligerent's censor opens, stamps and logs it, so a letter's silence is a row of the dated layer, not a constant. Beside every fare the planner shows a self-forecast — from your own acts and the published lags, never from the hunters' files — of where each record will be, and when.
  • Every 10 minutes: A leg is planned against the hunters' probable knowledge: the Munich slip reaches the Polizeidirektion tomorrow; the Zurich drawing sits behind a warrant; the trunk still wears the Vienna hotel's label. Thin the trail (a bribed clerk, a stolen register page — records in themselves), choose which legend signs the next book, or lay a decoy: a through-ticket never used, an accomplice signing a distant register, a cable from a station you leave by cab.
  • Every session (1–2 hours): A commission across two or three cities on C07's timetable; a reveal rhythm you can hear — registers nightly, gazettes weekly, circulars monthly; a near-miss when a description lands one node behind you; perhaps a dossier burgled and a page removed; perhaps an arrest, and the autopsy that shows which record did it.
  • Over a campaign (years of game time): The log never shrinks. Dossiers copy along alliance edges in 1907 and 1914, pass with losses to successor regimes in 1917, reopen under archive reviews; registration regimes thicken from 1914 to 1920, so the same verbs write more paper each year; early carelessness is still filed when you are fifty. A veteran's dossier is thin because the veteran learned which books to stay out of.

The systems and how they interlock

  • Travel (network, schedules, fares, classes, traces): Every mode leaves a different mark (P04.05): a named sleeping-car berth is high-fidelity and persistent; a third-class day ticket reveals mode and direction only; a steamer files a passenger list, but Britain's Aliens Act 1905 lists were required only of immigrant ships carrying more than twenty alien steerage passengers, so a Harwich packet filed none and before 1914 the Hook route writes nothing at either end; a cab is remembered for a day. A missed connection (C07) forces an overnight stop and a register. Frontier requirements are rows of C10's dated layer: none in Western Europe before 1914, passports and visas in Russia and Ottoman lands, everywhere afterwards. A record's lag is a delivery on the same clock as the trains, scheduled for subscribed readers and computed on query for everyone else, and the hunters ride the same graph (C05).
  • Deception / conversation / coding: Which legend signs is the deception verb (C04); dossiers are kept per name, so the hunt advances only by linking two — a witness who saw both faces, one hand in two registers, an endorsed draft, one trunk under two names, a shared cable address, a timetable that could not have produced both records, two services comparing files at liaison. An Assert in conversation (C12), a report token (C01) and a forged passport field (C06) are records with authorship set to you; a lie's truth-distance is its gap from the world log, which the clock will test. Cables are copied and letters censored (C20): both are records with readers.
  • Items / artifacts: Provenance is the log filtered by item id — issued, bought, stolen, copied, crossed a frontier at a time, pawned, planted — and an inspector knows its institution's view widened by liaison (C06). A labelled trunk is a record on legs; a stolen dossier is an item whose pages are records, and removing one makes a deduction regress.
  • Economy / business: Money has a signature (C08): a letter of credit draws only at listed correspondents, by the signing legend, against a specimen; a telegraphic transfer leaves a cable copy; gold leaves nothing and weighs. Protested bills and gazetted bankruptcies are public records in the same store, which is how a burned legend's creditor becomes a budgeted private hunter. Decoys cost money and accomplices, who have trails. Hunters have budgets too: each ledger query is funded by a service's annual vote (C01), so your successes pay the clerks who will read you.
  • Politics / warfare: Readers are political. A record's reader set comes from the cooperation and extradition matrix (service × jurisdiction × offence class × date, C03): a Sûreté lead dies at the Belgian frontier unless Brussels agrees; London shelters offences framed as political under the Extradition Act 1870's exception while ordinary crimes are extraditable to Russia under the 1886 treaty, so how a service characterises your act decides who may read your file. War rewrites readers overnight: censors read current cables as routine, enemy archives close, allied ones open, a fallen regime's files pass on with a destruction fraction.
  • Time pressure & the world clock: Lag is pressure: a Berlin or Vienna slip reaches the police within about a day; a consular inquiry takes weeks; a cable copy sits for months; a review of retained copies is a rare event the war regime schedules. Scotland Yard's reveal cadence becomes institutional rhythm — hypotheses reweight when registers are collected, gazettes printed, circulars issued — never a visible countdown. The world clock mutates the tables: 31 July 1914 (German passports), 5 August 1914 (British alien registration), February and November 1915 (photographs; compulsory passports), 1920 (the League booklet; general hotel registers in Britain).
  • The hunt (hidden movement, evidence, hunters): C05 reads this store and nothing else: weighted hypotheses over each legend's place, time, mode and habit, propagated along the real timetable and reweighted only by delivered records; the hunter profiles you (C14's habit model), queries ledgers where weight concentrates, sits on fixed points, and cordons a city's real exits when weight collapses. A fabrication the timetable makes impossible is discarded and raises that service's scepticism against the evidence class, so decoys are an arms race. What you see is governed by C16: the own-trail view, always available and silent about what was read; and the windows onto inference — institutional cadence, the press, the stolen dossier, the interrogation room, and the autopsy that replays C05's reasoning over these records.
  • The life (ageing, health, households, reputation, legends): The life writes paper. A sanatorium hides you and registers you at once; a limp or a scar is a portrait-parlé field that never changes; servants are witnesses with a talk rate; standing per circle crosses bridging institutions as gossip and can link names (C04). Age thins the face in the 1905 photograph but not the height on the card. Capture never ends the life (C11): remand, expulsion, exchange or a sentence that spends years, and the file follows a successor as a two-subject dossier.

A worked example of play

March 1912, pre-war rules. Under legend A — a British machine-tool traveller — the player copies a shipyard tender in Hamburg during a window posted by an arms firm (C18). The act writes nothing; the week around it writes plenty: a hotel registration form (high fidelity; with the Hamburg police within a day; and, because the document is naval, readable by the Admiralty Staff's intelligence section working with the Hamburg political police — readers follow the document's subject), a commercial-code cable to a London cable address from the Hauptbahnhof office (copy retained for months, readable under warrant), a second-class berth on the night boat-train to the Hook of Holland (berth sheet under A), and a first-class landing at Harwich that no Aliens Act list records. In London nobody registers A: in 1912 Britain writes nothing. Ten days later the theft is noticed (a Tier 2 event); the naval section's dossier holds a porter's fading memory and, after a budgeted query of the Hamburg registers, A's name and the hotel's luggage label. Hypotheses propagate along the March timetable to the Hook, Antwerp and Copenhagen; a second query returns the berth sheet and weight collapses onto the Harwich route. A description reaches London by consular channel, but with no register to query and no address it yields a note; the dossier is shelved. The player's own-trail view shows the danger they made themselves: the London cable address is also used by legend B, a Swiss national, for bank cables — one tag under two names (C04). They retire the address and move B to letters. On 5 August 1914 every alien must register: A, British, writes nothing until the 1915 Act; B presents papers at a police station and is issued an alien's registration certificate, to which the wartime Aliens Restriction Orders (consolidated in 1916) add a photograph; the February 1915 photograph rule belongs to British passports, A's document, not B's. In 1916 a rare wartime review of retained copies, run by a British service now curious about A for its own reasons (C01), surfaces the 1912 form: A's name above the address B's 1913 bank cables carried. The autopsy, when it comes, shows that page, the two names it joined, and the letter that would have kept them apart.

Why it is captivating and addictive

The argument is honesty, not stimulation. Attributable consequence: hunters act only on records, so the player always knows which comfort betrayed them, and fairness attribution with controllability is the strongest predictor of persisting after loss: the autopsy shows not only the record that caused the capture but the branch point — the letter that would have kept the names apart — so the player leaves with "I could have", not only "I see why" (P09: "every deduction derivable from one log makes forty hours of pursuit auditable"). Loss aversion over something built and visible: the possession is the own-trail view — a thin, ageing trail the player can see — and every register spends it; the dossier itself is only inferred through windows, so the loss the player feels is of what they can see. An information gap without a minimap: what the hunters concluded is visible only through costly windows, so the evening edition is sought voluntarily and relief when it is silent is real; dread is resolved only by priced acts — an edition, a bought clerk, a stolen page — so each resolution is an event on an institutional cadence, and the cycle of tension and relief runs for forty hours without going chronic. Zeigarnik tension on a period clock: a slip in transit to the Polizeidirektion is an open loop between sessions, and the own-trail view's lag timers turn that anxiety into anticipation — each record's arrival is a known future event, so the player waits for a date rather than fearing a fog; this is the strongest honest hook the design owns. Theory of mind: feeding the hunter a through-ticket you never used and watching a cordon form on the wrong city is the richest agency in the pool, kept an arms race by a scepticism learner whose state is readable through a C16 window — discarded fabrications appear in the autopsy — so the hunter adapts in the open rather than cheating. Mastery in the head: knowing a German inn's Fremdenbuch is collected nightly while a British boarding house keeps none. The stopping points are the hotel register (saving costs cover) and the quiet week before a gazette; players push through because a record is in transit, an edition is hours away, and the night train that outruns tomorrow's slip is on the board.

Historical grounding

The parameters are the period's procedures. Registration: German and Austrian hotels delivered a Meldezettel or Fremdenzettel to the police, in big cities within about a day; French innkeepers kept registers for police inspection; Russian hotels carried the passport to the police desk; Britain registered nobody before August 1914 — aliens from the Aliens Restriction Act and Order of 5 August 1914, British subjects under the National Registration Act 1915, general hotel registers only under the Aliens Order 1920 — so a British legend can be invented until 1915, a foreign one only until August 1914. Travel paper: named berths, printed passenger lists, Aliens Act 1905 lists only for immigrant ships with more than twenty alien steerage passengers, customs declarations; a change of train, not a bogie exchange, at the Russian gauge break; the Trans-Siberian through from 1903–04. Communications: telegraph offices retained copies, obtainable under warrant; wartime censors read current traffic routinely and retained copies only in rare reviews. Documents: passport-free Western and Central Europe against Russian and Ottoman regimes; the German decree of 31 July 1914 and French measures of early August; British photographs from February 1915 and compulsory passports from November 1915; the League's 1920 booklet. Identification: Bertillonage and the portrait parlé, Scotland Yard's Fingerprint Bureau from 1901, Police Gazette and Steckbrief circulars, half-tone photographs in dailies from the 1900s. Readers: the Okhrana's Paris bureau beside the Sûreté; the Rome 1898 and St Petersburg 1904 protocols; the Extradition Act 1870's political-offence exception beside the Anglo-Russian treaty of 24 November 1886; the Okhrana's abolition in March 1917 with partial archive survival; the Vienna commission of 1923. Clocks: Russian railways on St Petersburg time and the Julian calendar to 31 January 1918; Paris time in France to 1911; Ottoman clocks from sunset — every displayed time passes through a local regime, and a forged paper in the wrong calendar is a tell.

Engine: dynamic events, decisions, actions, dependencies

Data model: one append-only record store of canonical tuples; legends, institutions, services, places, items and messages are keys into it, and every other candidate's store — C01's belief stores, C03's files, C04's fact-graphs, C06's inspector facts, C12's NPC beliefs, C14's witness log, C19's cargo, C08's gazettes — is a filtered view, never a copy. The tuple carries its source institution, not a reader list: readers are derived at query time from C03's matrix and archive survival on that date, which is what lets the 1914 fusion and the worked example's 1916 review open 1912 records to readers that did not exist when they were written. Emission: each act type carries a data-driven rule keyed by jurisdiction and date (coverage, lag distribution, fidelity, persistence, source institution), the dated rows living in C10's parameter layer; emission schedules delivery events only for subscribed readers — active hunters, bourses, censors on gated channels — on C07's global priority queue, so a slip arrives under the same total ordering as a train, while every other reader evaluates arrival lazily on query from the same lag rule (C13's form), so broadcast records such as circulars and gazettes never post thousands of events each. Reasoning: a dossier is an indexed filter, essentially free (P02.05); C05's hunters propagate bounded, weighted hypotheses on a hierarchical graph that refines only where weight concentrates, reweight at institutional cadence rather than per tick, and spend a budget on queries; bitset reachability keeps dozens of hunters at microseconds (P02.08); link detection is a declarative pass over co-occurring tags; C14's mining runs at ticks, cached; a per-service scepticism learner weights evidence classes. Lazy materialisation applies to the background population only: records tagged to the player and other entities of interest are always written, so the own-trail view and self-forecast read them before any hunter; ledgers nobody queries remain counts — books, not clerks. Determinism: each lag draw is seeded by hashing (seed, record id, reader id, edge id) rather than taken from a sequential stream, so lazy and eager evaluation agree and replay is order-independent; with total ordering a save is seed plus input log and replay exact, which lets C16's autopsy replay inference with provenance (record id → reweight → action) — also the designers' balancing instrument. Persistence: archive nodes store event ids; macro-events schedule reviews with per-archive destruction fractions; the record is never pruned, compaction touching derived state only (A-P02-1). Authoring is declarative: emission tables, coverage tables, liaison matrices, cadences, link detectors, scepticism priors. Cost is linear in acts for the records written and in subscribed readers for the deliveries scheduled — not acts × readers, because unsubscribed readers evaluate on query.

Progression and endings (as systems)

What grows: the log; each legend's record-set depth, which is also its credibility and credit limit; the hunters' dossiers; each service's scepticism; the player's competence, visible as a thinner dossier. What decays: witness memory, hypothesis weight without fresh records, hunter budgets without results, dormant dossiers — shelved, not deleted, waking on a linkage or a world-clock change. What accumulates: links between names, alliance copies, archive reviews. Endings are read from the record: capture yields the autopsy and continues the life (interrogation, remand, expulsion, exchange, a sentence that spends years); a burned legend ends a name, not the game; retirement holds only for a name no dossier contains, so file thickness is the retirement score (C11); turning hunter is C05's inverse mode; succession hands on a two-subject file.

C13 · whole-game concept · lead author P04 (Travel & Logistics Designer)

Faster Than the Wire: The Information Front

Your body moves at train speed, news of you at cable speed: race your own description, and trade in the gap before the bourse hears.

Borda 145/200mean 7.50first-place votes 0ranked between 2nd and 14th

One-line pitch

A spy life-sim with two maps, the railway you ride and the wire that carries news of you, where every act is a race between your body and your description, and knowing a thing an hour before the bourse is both your income and the evidence against you.

The hook — what is new

The period had two networks running at different speeds, and this game simulates both as graphs with real latencies. Every fact about an identity or an event is an entity travelling at its medium's speed: minutes between open telegraph offices, a night by mail train, five days by liner, an edition cycle through the agencies, a week for a photograph by post; the player's body travels on the timetable. The hunt, the newspaper, the bourse and the player's forecast of where their own description has reached are one propagation model read from different seats, so dread and opportunity are the same quantity, latency, and the player always sees their own front but never the hunters' minds.

Core loop

  • Every 30 seconds: choose how an act travels (in person, courier, letter, cable in commercial code) and read the planner's self-forecast beside the fare; buy or skip the edition; decide whether a headline without details is enough to act on.
  • Every 10 minutes: a day of edition times, bourse sessions, office hours and mail collections: a fact acquired at 16:00 opens a window that closes when the agency wire lands at the bourse tomorrow; a visible act writes records that reach their readers hours or days later; a leg is built so the body arrives where the description has not; a register, frontier post or bank counter is a checkpoint against what has arrived there.
  • Every session (1–2 hours): one commission generates two fronts, the secret's news and your own description, moving at different speeds through different channels; you route body, money and messages through the seams (offices that close at night, police who do not talk across a frontier, the sea beyond wireless range); the climax is a checkpoint the forecast rates at 40 per cent, a number that decomposes into channels, office hours and liaison gates you can still act on (alight a station earlier, sign the other legend), and tomorrow's edition tells you whether you were right.
  • Over a campaign (years of game time): the communications graph rewires on the world clock: cable redundancy after 1902, wireless pushing the wire to sea from the mid-1900s, censors and cut cables from August 1914, Russia going dark in 1917; mastery is knowing the latencies; your archive of office copies never shrinks; the prescience accumulator judges how often you were right too early.

The systems and how they interlock

Describe each as rules/resources/states and, crucially, how it feeds the others:

  • Travel (network, schedules, fares, classes, traces): the transport graph is C07's time-expanded timetable; a leg costs hours, a fare and a class, and class sets trace fidelity (C02). C13 adds a second graph over the same places: telegraph and post offices, coast stations, agencies, bourses, banks, consulates and police posts, joined by channel edges with a latency distribution, office hours, capacity, a jurisdiction gate keyed by traffic class and a copy rule. Routing is a latency play: a night express beats tomorrow's edition but not tonight's wire; a wire needs open offices at both ends, and official traffic (police circulars, liaison requests) also needs a liaison agreement across a frontier (C03), whereas private and commercial telegrams crossed every frontier under the International Telegraph Convention, gated only by offices, tariffs, the 1903 code-word regulations and, from August 1914, censors; a sea leg beyond wireless range is dark; in reverse, you route so that your letters of introduction precede you.
  • Deception / conversation / coding: a wanted notice is a cable, a portrait-parlé description travelling as a fact with a confidence value, the photograph following by post days later, so the cheapest defence is to change the thing described (C04: switch legend where only the old name has arrived). Commercial code compresses at so much a word, private cipher conceals but attracts censors (C20), and every cable leaves an office copy whose readers list (C17) grows under warrant. Planted news is a provisional fact that reverts when contradicted; a poisoned liaison request is a fact with false authorship; room talk (C12) spreads as rumour at post speed across C04's gossip bridges.
  • Items / artifacts: a telegram exists three times, handed-in form, office copy and delivered copy, each with its own readers; editions are dated objects whose value dies with the next edition; code books, a broker's book, ticker tape and a registration slip are items that are also records; goods in luggage and crates (C19) carry city prices that are themselves facts in transit.
  • Economy / business: the news-latency market. Each bourse (London, Paris, Berlin, Vienna, St Petersburg, New York; Constantinople for Ottoman paper) holds a fact set and reprices an instrument at its next session only when the relevant fact has arrived; a fact known before the agency wire opens a window of hours. Profit is bounded emergently, not capped: depth (a position above a fraction of daily turnover moves the price against you and writes a notable-dealing record), evidence (every trade is a timestamped C17 record correlable with later news) and prescience (C10). City price vectors shaped by tariff walls and season run the luggage trade (C19); market income pays C08's burn, is laundered through C15's books when large, and never replaces commissions.
  • Politics / warfare: the diplomatic calendar decides which police wires cross which frontiers (C03's matrix gates the edge for official traffic only); regime rows in C10's parameter layer switch censorship on, cut cables and reroute traffic through neutral copy points; crises (C09) swell cable volume so queues lengthen exactly when windows are richest. C10's prescience rule: moves correlated with an upcoming Tier 0 event, rather than with a fact whose acquisition is on record, raise a per-faction prescience score; thresholds trigger inquiry, frozen accounts or a recruitment approach; cover reasons discount it; Tier 1 jitter keeps the bet a bet.
  • Time pressure & the world clock: every element is a scheduled event in C07's queue: edition times, bourse sessions, office hours, mail trains, the arrival of each fact at each subscribed reader, other readers being evaluated lazily when they query. The world emits news with a propagation profile per event type, so the player always acts on information hours to weeks old; the lag is the pressure, and "wait for the evening edition" is a slot decision like any other.
  • The hunt (hidden movement, evidence, hunters): C13 is the wire half of C05: a notice reaches a post when its fact arrives; hunters reweight hypotheses only on records that reached their institution and move watchers by train; checkpoints query local knowledge only. The player's instruments are the self-forecast, computed from their own act log and published channel parameters and never from faction stores, and C16's windows (editions, agony columns, the interrogation room, the autopsy). Tactics: outrun the front, slow it (a bribed clerk, a frontier crossed before the relay, a wartime cut), change the described thing, or feed it a false sighting that C17's scepticism learner may discount.
  • The life (ageing, health, households, reputation, legends): the life rides the same wires: references and remittances travel by post and telegraphic transfer at the same latencies, so a legend's record-set must arrive before its body; standing (C04) spreads by gossip and reaches a city before you; a telegraphic transfer (C08) is instant and copied; households read the editions too; staying ahead of the wire costs sleep on C02's dial; office copies left in 1903 wait for C03's archive reviews in 1919.

A worked example of play

Constantinople, the second week of October 1908: Bulgaria's declaration of independence and the annexation of Bosnia have frozen police liaison between the Porte and Vienna. A want (C18): a Vienna bank will pay for the Porte's decision on a railway concession tender before it is gazetted. The player obtains it at 16:00 on day 0 and two fronts start. The decision's front: gazette tomorrow morning, the agency correspondent's cable about 10:00 Constantinople time, Vienna by 11:00 local time (Constantinople's clocks run an hour ahead of Vienna's), evening editions, and the Vienna bourse repricing the concession company's shares at the day 1 session. The description's front begins only when the clerk's absence is noticed at 08:00 on day 1: an Ottoman circular reaches the Ottoman post on the Bulgarian frontier, Mustafa Pasha beyond Adrianople, by wire within two hours; nothing is asked of the Bulgarian police, independent since 5 October 1908; the request to Vienna travels by embassy, which C03's matrix prices at two days this month. At 17:00 on day 0 the player hands in a fourteen-word commercial-code cable to a Vienna broker; its office copy is a C17 record whose readers are the cable company and, under an Ottoman warrant weeks away, the police. The broker buys at the opening of the day 1 session, under the depth threshold, two hours before the agency wire reprices. The body boards the Orient Express that evening: Mustafa Pasha passes about 06:00 on day 1, four hours before the post is hot; Serbia has been asked nothing; Vienna is some forty hours out. The self-forecast puts the Vienna police's chance of holding the description on arrival at 15 per cent, rising past 60 for a second night, because a registration slip is read within a day and the embassy request lands on day 3. The player chooses: alight at Budapest, where the request arrives later still; take payment by telegraphic transfer (instant, copied) or a letter-of-credit drawing (a signature record); register the Vienna night under the second legend. On day 3 the Vienna evening edition carries an agency paragraph on a theft at the Porte with a description (C16's window); the prescience accumulator is untouched because the fact's acquisition is on record; the cable copy sleeps until a review (C03) reads it.

Why it is captivating and addictive

The tension/relief cycle is the checkpoint: register, frontier, counter, each a query against what has arrived, resolved by institutions on their own clocks, so near-misses are earned rather than rolled; the forecast's percentage is not a lottery with a label but a sum of channels, offices and liaison gates the player can still change, as the worked example shows. Dread is legible without omniscience: the self-forecast turns anxiety into planning while the hunters' real holdings stay behind C16's windows, which is what keeps the race honest. The forecast also delivers relief honestly: most of the time it reports that no reader holds you yet, so standing still is a decision rather than a gamble, and relief is earned by reading. Information-gap curiosity runs the news half: a headline without detail is a gap the mind must close, so the evening edition is irresistible; the market makes the gap pay in numbers, but market windows exist only for facts whose acquisition is on record, so they are scarce by construction and the fast numeric loop cannot outcompete the slow spy loops; the hunt makes the gap cost in sleep. Mastery lives in the head: that a provincial office closes at eight, that a liner is dark for three days, is competence in the self-determination sense. Meaningful choice is the speed-permanence trade on every message: the cable instant and copied, the letter slow and private, the courier a body with a timetable. Emergent narrative is the record itself: the autopsy shows the race's margin, the hour the description arrived against the hour you left, so a near-miss or a capture is replayable and a loss converts into mastery. Natural stopping points are the last edition and the bourse's close; players push through because a session ends aboard a train with the forecast showing where the description will be at arrival, and the window they are inside closes at a published hour. No variable-ratio schedule is used; every uncertainty is an institution's clock.

Historical grounding

Accuracy is the latency table. The submarine cable network was dense by 1895 and redundant after the All Red Line closed in 1902; a transatlantic word cost about a shilling and companies kept office copies, readable under warrant; commercial codes (Bentley's from 1906) compressed meaning for anyone holding the edition. News moved through the Reuters–Havas–Wolff ring, each relaying its territory, into multi-edition dailies in the capitals and a day later into the provinces; Exchange Telegraph tickers had fed London since 1872. Wireless enters on the world clock: coast stations and shipboard sets from the first years of the century, liner news bulletins from 1904, and in 1910 the celebrated case ran the other way: a liner's master recognised passengers from press descriptions and wirelessed ashore, and the pursuer crossed on a faster liner to board at Quebec, which the model reproduces as a reader at sea querying the shore while a body races by timetable; before wireless a ship at sea was five dark days. Post ran at night-mail speed with several deliveries a day and pneumatic tubes in Paris, Berlin and Vienna; there was no transatlantic telephony until 1927. Police knowledge travelled by telegraphed circular, posted description cards and fingerprint cards from 1901, through liaison that followed treaties (C03). The rules change inside the period: the November 1905 Russian postal-telegraph strike blacks out the wire; on 5 August 1914 the Channel cables to Germany are cut and German traffic to the Americas passes through neutral copy points; censors read cables and admit only deposited codes; a Press Bureau filters editions; most bourses close at the end of July 1914, London from 31 July to 4 January 1915; Russian editions date in the Julian calendar until 31 January 1918 (C17's clock rule); the intercepted cable of January 1917, published on 1 March, is the archetype of an office copy that fights back.

Engine: dynamic events, decisions, actions, dependencies

Two graphs over shared place nodes: C07's time-expanded timetable and a communications graph of institution nodes and channel edges, each edge a per-date parameter row (latency distribution, office hours, capacity, C03's jurisdiction gate keyed by traffic class, official wires gated and private cables ungated but censored from August 1914, censorship state, copy retention) in C10's single parameter layer, written by macro-events. A fact is C17's tuple (subject, predicate, value, confidence, source, time, authorship, readers), where readers are not stored but derived at query time from the source institution and C03's matrix on that date, which is what lets the worked example's office copy be read under a later Ottoman warrant; propagation is not a second mechanism but C17's lag-and-reader model computed on this graph: an act or event emits a fact with a propagation profile (which channel types carry it, with what degradation) and the scheduler posts arrival events into C07's priority queue only for subscribed readers (an active hunter, a bourse session, a watched frontier post); every other reader is evaluated lazily on query, hop-bounded, so cost scales with subscriptions and queries, not facts in flight, and C17 states the same subscribed-reader rule; facts exist only for subjects of interest; institutions never read ground truth. The self-forecast reuses the propagation code on the player's own act log with published parameters and no faction state, so it is cheap and cannot leak. Markets are functions: a bourse's price is a deterministic function of the facts that have arrived there plus a seeded mean-reverting term and a depth response, with no per-tick simulation; trade records are C17 rows with broker and bourse as readers, and the who-dealt-before-the-news rule runs only when a service investigates. Every latency draw is hash-seeded by (campaign seed, fact id, reader id, edge id) rather than taken from a sequential stream, so lazy evaluation cannot change draw order; with every arrival totally ordered in C07's queue, a save is seed plus input log and C16's autopsy replays the exact chain. Authoring is tabular: offices and hours, latencies by year, agency territories, edition times, censorship rows, propagation profiles per event type, structured headline templates; a decade adds rows. Scale: a few hundred institution nodes; thousands of subject-of-interest facts per campaign, each with a handful of arrivals; editions and police circulars are modelled not as one fact per copy but as periodic broadcast channels with circulation geometry (C16), without which the count would run to tens of thousands.

Progression and endings (as systems)

What grows: latency knowledge held in the player's head; a network of institution-bound assets with reservation prices and leak risk (telegraph clerks, agency stringers, brokers, a cable address) that can be bought, burned or turned; cover reasons for being early, which discount prescience. What decays: facts' confidence and readers' attention; a cold description stops being queried but is never deleted. What accumulates: office copies and trade records in archives that C03's reviews may reopen; the prescience score; the market's depth response to a known name. End states are C11's, reached through this layer: retirement holds when no live front reaches the haven's readers; capture is a checkpoint querying a fact that arrived first; ruin is a frozen account after a prescience inquiry or an exchange closed on an open position; turning is C05's inverse mode run from the wire's side; the second agent inherits archives that remember the first.

Risks and mitigations

  • Collapse into flight (P01, 9th: "narrows the life toward flight and arbitrage"; P05, P06 and P10 agree). Mitigation: most fronts are opportunities, and the same layer is the player's only window on the world, so most play is reading and using latency; the self-forecast usually reports that no reader holds you yet, so standing still is the common right answer; running costs health (C02) and households (C04).
  • The life is absent (P10, 14th: "after amendment its substrate is C17 and the life is absent"; P02: "a spine for the pressure half of the brief"). Honest answer: C13 is the pressure-and-opportunity spine and is pitched coupled to C04 and C11, whose legends, standings and remittances ride these wires; it does not replace them.
  • A stock minigame (P02.09's own risk). Bounds are emergent (depth, trades as evidence, prescience) and income is tied to C08's burn and C15's laundering.
  • Latency tuning: if the wire always wins the race is unwinnable; if offices never matter it is trivial. Office hours, liaison gates, crisis queues and the sea are the designed seams, and the MVP exists to tune the margins (P05.04: postal speeds near express speeds narrow races).
  • The minimap exploit (P01.06's first risk): closed by A-P08-1; hunter inference appears only through C16.
  • Replay foreknowledge (P03.08): Tier 0 dates are immovable, Tier 1 jitter and micro-events keep timing approximate, and C10 prices prescience as evidence.

  • Procedural news text: structured headlines and tables, never articles.

The Four Windows

You never read hunter inference — what the services have concluded — except through costly windows; your own trail is always visible, computed from your own records, and says nothing about what was read or inferred.

Full candidate text
Central hook
Hunter belief is exposed only through known consolidation rhythms with unknown content; what hunters publish and what you publish back; a list of inferred habits obtainable at risk; a replayable reconstruction of the evidence chain after capture; and — a window of the same kind — the interrogation room (C12), where question patterns leak the dossier. C17's exposure map, C02's forecast and C04's linkage indicator are own-record views, not windows.
How it plays
Daily: buy the editions (description, reward, later a photograph) and answer in kind: agony-column cipher, a planted sighting, a paid paragraph. Registers collect nightly, gazettes weekly, circulars monthly, tightening in wartime: a tempo of boldness and retreat. After an arrest: see the dossier as built — register, cable copy, the timetable deduction excluding other routes — then continue: interrogation, remand, expulsion or a sentence; the autopsy also surfaces approach events made on your dependants (C08) and replays C05's inference over C17's records.
Directive coverage
Hunt primary. Secondary: deception (planted sightings), coding (agony-column ciphers), economy (paragraphs, bail), politics (exchanges; censorship), travel (circulation areas), time (editions as rhythm), the life (sentences consume years).
Why captivating & addictive
Fairness attribution best predicts persistence after loss; the autopsy converts loss into mastery and reads as a story; dread sought daily; relief when the edition is silent.
Engine implications
Deterministic, seed-replayable simulation with provenance on every inference, also the engine's debugging tool; publications as broadcast events with circulation geometry and recall multipliers, relayed by agencies with delay; cadence from institutional parameters; procedural notice text; windows read faction stores only through these channels, and own-trail views never do.
Historical grounding
Police Gazette and Steckbrief traditions; half-tone photography in dailies from the 1900s; the agony column as covert channel; expulsion or short sentences as prewar agents' fate; wartime courts-martial.

Codes, Ciphers, Censors and Copies

Every message has readers: a cable's office copy outlives you, letters pass learning censors, and intercepts are ciphertexts you break by hand, book, bureau or roll.

Full candidate text
Central hook
An abstraction ladder (hand, book, bureau, roll) yields one object; censors score letter components with date-improving heuristics, so codes have half-lives; a captured book reads traffic until its owner rotates it when visible exploitation raises hidden suspicion.
How it plays
Composing: shave words (cables cost per word); commercial code anyone with the edition reads, or private cipher, admitted only from deposited lists after August 1914; route by courier for permanence over speed. Reading: frequency and crib tools make Playfair feasible by hand; novices pay a bureau or use a book (incriminating). Every decrypt used is a wager: exploit and risk the source, or hold.
Directive coverage
Coding and deception primary. Secondary: items (code books, inks), economy (tariffs, bureau fees), politics (wartime censorship), travel (intercepts worth a train), hunt (reading hunters' traffic), time (censor delays).
Why captivating & addictive
Puzzle "aha" and competence, a skill acquired, not a stat raised; novices progress while experts earn faster, cheaper intelligence; the source-burning dilemma is tension with legible consequence.
Engine implications
String-level encryption of templated plaintexts with a crib vocabulary; the abstract rung returns identical outputs and reads the character's cipher skill (C11), so player-head mastery and character skill coexist on one ladder; messages as entities with routing paths, copy-holders and decay; copies and censors' flags are C17 records; per-office anomaly scoring with date-indexed parameters; per-faction hidden compromise-suspicion.
Historical grounding
Bentley's code; Playfair in the Boer War and the Great War; naval code books captured 1914; Room 40; ADFGVX 1918; Okhrana black cabinets; wartime postal censorship.
