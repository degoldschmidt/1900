# Round 1A Proposals — Economy, Business & Artifacts Designer (Panelist P06)

## Panelist statement
A spy in 1895–1925 is expensive: legends, households, bribes and tickets are paid for in gold-standard money that has weight, a location and a paper trail. I want a game in which the player must run a business to afford being a spy and must spy to make the business work, with every pound, mark and rouble moving through period instruments that the hunter can read. Objects should not be inventory icons but records: each carries a provenance that can enrich the player or hang them. An economy that does not itself generate time pressure and pursuit is decoration; the proposals below are built so that it does.

## P06.01 — The Double Ledger
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player runs a real commission and trading house whose books are at once economic engine, cover-identity generator and the game's most incriminating artifact. Business and espionage are one loop: trade funds and excuses travel, travel yields intelligence, intelligence yields trading advantage.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player takes orders (commodities, machinery, arms), finances them with bills of exchange, ships them and collects commissions. Each contract demands presence in a city — cover for tasks the services pay for. Every transaction writes a double-entry ledger line. The public ledger (auditable by tax offices and police) and the true ledger diverge whenever spy money enters; the player reconciles them by laundering through trade. The firm is a legal person in a chosen jurisdiction: it holds assets, can be sold and can go bankrupt while the player walks away.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy primary. Travel: contracts pull the player onto the timetable. Deception: the firm is a legend with its own paper. Items: goods, ledgers, bills. Politics: wars and tariffs change what sells where. Time: bills mature, ships sail. Hunt: auditors, creditors and counter-intelligence read the books.
- **Historical grounding:** Commission houses as the face of pre-1914 trade; discounted bills; gold-standard parities; company registration regimes; Trading with the Enemy legislation (1914).
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Two interlocking loops each relieve the other's pressure, producing constant near-solvency tension; loss aversion over an enterprise the player built; mastery of legible accounting; the dread-pleasure of knowing one seized ledger ends everything.
- **Engine implications:** Transaction log as first-class data; two ledger views from one event stream; audit AI querying the log; jurisdiction-tagged company entity; contracts as scheduled multi-step dependencies.
- **Risks / open questions:** Accounting must be abstracted enough to stay fun; onboarding complexity.
## P06.02 — Money Has a Body
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Wealth is not a number but a portfolio of period instruments — gold coin, banknotes, letters of credit, circular notes, bills of exchange, telegraphic transfers, bearer bonds, pawn tickets — each with weight, geography, liquidity, identity-binding and traceability.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player composes a purse per journey. Gold is universal and anonymous but heavy and seizable. A letter of credit is safe but redeemable only at named correspondent banks, in banking hours, by the identity whose signature specimen (the letter of indication) it carries. A bill of exchange matures on a date: a deadline. A telegraphic transfer is instant but leaves a copy at the cable office. Bearer bonds and hallmarked jewellery cross frontiers nameless. Pawnshops give cash at a loss and leave a ticket — an artifact.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy primary. Travel: what you can carry, where you can draw. Deception: identity-bound money forces that legend to sign in person. Items: instruments are items. Politics: wartime exchange controls and gold-export bans rewrite the rules. Time: maturities, banking hours, mail delays. Hunt: the money trail — bank drawings, cable transfers, serial numbers.
- **Historical grounding:** Classical gold standard (£1 ≈ 25.2 F ≈ 20.4 M ≈ 9.5 R ≈ $4.87); letters of credit and indication; circular notes (from 1874); banknote serials; 1914 suspension of convertibility; wartime gold-export restrictions.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The purse is a packing puzzle (planning pleasure); endowment effect over gold physically carried; dread of being cashless abroad; satisfaction of a clean, untraceable payment.
- **Engine implications:** Instrument entity with location, owner-identity, redemption rules and trace flag; bank hours on the world clock; hunter queries over trace events.
- **Risks / open questions:** Fiddliness; needs strong defaults and a "banker" automation.
## P06.03 — Provenance: Every Object Remembers
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Each significant item carries an append-only chain of custody: issued, bought, stolen, copied, forged, carried through a frontier at a time, pawned, planted. Hunters, buyers and courts read it; the player can launder, forge or sever it, at a cost and leaving its own trace.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Naval plans have a registry number; a rifle a serial and proof marks; a passport an issuing office and stamps; a banknote a serial; a camera's film must be developed somewhere. When the player sells, plants or loses an item, the hunter AI gains its record up to recovery and infers the path: hotels registered, frontiers crossed, banks visited. Laundering means reselling through intermediaries, restriking hallmarks, retyping documents on another machine (typeface identification exists), or destroying — which forfeits an asset and may leave ash, witnesses or a missing-item alarm.
- **Directive coverage:** Items primary. Hunt: provenance is the hunter's main evidence stream. Travel: frontier stamps and hotel registers append to provenance. Deception: forgery is provenance editing. Economy: laundering costs; clean goods fetch more. Politics: contraband definitions change by regime and war. Time: items run "hot" then cool.
- **Historical grounding:** Hallmarks and assay offices; rifle serials; Bertillon and fingerprint practice; typewriter forensics; hotel police registers; customs stamps; telegraph companies retaining cable copies.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Information-gap curiosity (what does the hunter know?); dramatic irony; the thrill of a perfect laundering; regret when a careless sale resurfaces months later as a knock on the door: persistent consequence.
- **Engine implications:** Item event log with timestamps and location; hunter deduction as log queries; scalability by tiering (only significant items logged, commodities aggregated).
- **Risks / open questions:** Over-punishing; the player must be able to inspect their own exposure.
## P06.04 — Six Verbs for Every Artifact
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every artifact affords sell, trade, plant, leverage, use and destroy, plus "register" for technical secrets (file as a foreign patent). The market for secrets prices them by scarcity, verifiability and decay.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A stolen blueprint can be sold to one service for cash; sold again as a copy to a second (each copy lowers the price and raises exposure, because copies carry distinguishing marks); planted in a rival's luggage before a frontier; held as leverage over the official who lost it; used to inform a business bid; registered as a patent in another jurisdiction to turn theft into royalties (a public filing, hence traceable); or burned to end a hunt. Secrets decay: plans for a ship are worthless after launch, a mobilisation table after mobilisation. Prices come from per-faction demand curves modulated by the current crisis level.
- **Directive coverage:** Items and economy primary. Deception: planting, forging copies. Politics: demand follows the crisis state; war inflates both prices and penalties. Travel: buyers sit in specific cities, so decay makes a race on the timetable. Hunt: every verb but destroy leaves a provenance trace.
- **Historical grounding:** Naval plans and mobilisation schedules as the age's prize documents; national patent systems under the Paris Convention; commercial codebooks; services with small budgets paying in favours and passports as much as cash.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Optionality is intrinsically rewarding; double-selling is push-your-luck with variable-ratio payoff; decay timers produce goal-gradient urgency; a clever plant delivers the con-artist's superiority high.
- **Engine implications:** Affordance table per item class; demand model per faction; decay functions bound to world-clock events; copy lineage recorded in the provenance log.
- **Risks / open questions:** Dominant strategies (always double-sell) unless exposure mathematics bites.

## P06.05 — The Commission Agent
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player brokers arms and industrial contracts between manufacturers and governments or insurgents, living on commission, with espionage as the means of learning what rivals bid and what buyers secretly want.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A deal is a months-long state machine: identify a buyer (ministry, navy, insurgency), learn its specification and budget by conversation, bribery or theft, align a manufacturer, outbid rivals (or sabotage their bid by selling evidence of it to the buyer's counter-intelligence), secure the contract, finance delivery, ship the goods, collect commission on delivery. World-clock deadlines govern each stage: a crisis makes buyers desperate; a peace collapses prices; an embargo makes a delivery criminal overnight. Services also pay for bidder intelligence, so the same information sells twice — to the buyer and to a foreign service — at the risk of being seen as both dealer and spy.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and politics primary. Travel: factories, ministries and ports are far apart; deals require presence. Deception: dual-selling; fronting for a manufacturer under a legend. Items: sample weapons, contracts, bills of lading. Time: crisis windows, delivery dates. Hunt: rival agents, customs, counter-intelligence tracing shipments.
- **Historical grounding:** Vickers, Krupp, Schneider and Škoda and their commission agents; the naval race; Balkan arms purchases before 1912; Irish gun-running 1914; wartime contraband lists.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Long-horizon anticipation (large payoffs months away) interleaved with short-horizon tension; rivalry against AI agents; the Zeigarnik pull of a deal left half-closed at session end.
- **Engine implications:** Contract state machines dependent on world-clock events; rival agent AI; freight routing; demand per faction.
- **Risks / open questions:** Could collapse into spreadsheet management; each stage needs a field action.
## P06.06 — The Concession Hunter
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** Concessions — railway, oil, mining, timber and telegraph rights granted by weak or venal states — are the great assets: won by access and bribery, valued by political forecasting, flipped to syndicates on the bourses, and voided by the revolutions and wars the world clock delivers regardless.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each concession is a state machine (rumoured → petitioned → granted → financed → operating → disputed → voided) inside a granting polity whose stability is simulated. The player travels to the capital, cultivates and bribes, assembles capital from banks and syndicates by promising shares, then decides: hold for revenue, or sell while the political-risk premium is low. Espionage reveals what the rival bidder offered and what the ministry's confidential stability report says. A concession's paper value moves daily with news on the bourse, so a well-timed intercepted cable is worth more than the mine.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and politics primary. Travel: capitals at the edges of the rail network. Deception: legends as engineers or financiers. Items: the deed, maps, survey reports. Time: tender deadlines, revolution clocks. Hunt: a rival syndicate's agents and the host state's police.
- **Historical grounding:** Persian oil concession 1901 (oil struck 1908); Baghdad Railway 1903; Manchurian and Korean timber and railway concessions preceding 1904; Siberian goldfields; the Ottoman Public Debt Administration; 1917 expropriation.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Speculating on a hidden political model is a forecasting game with rich feedback; large swings exploit loss aversion and sunk cost; "sell now or hold" recurs as an agonising, meaningful choice.
- **Engine implications:** Polity stability model emitting events; asset valuation function; syndicate AI; bourse ticker fed by the news system.
- **Risks / open questions:** Abstract; needs tactile stakes in the field.

## P06.07 — Legends Have Burn Rates
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every cover identity has a lifestyle cost that must be paid to remain credible. An underfunded legend decays in credibility and attracts attention; an overfunded one attracts thieves and envy.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each legend has a social class with a monthly burn (club subscriptions, hotel grade, tailoring, servants, carriage, gifts) and a wardrobe inventory. Households in several cities each cost rent and staff and each offer a safe house, a mail drop and a cache. The player decides which legends are live (paid up, resident) and which dormant (cheap, but reactivation takes time and generates registration events). Gambling is both class behaviour and a liquidity option with variance; gambling debts are debts of honour, unenforceable at law but enforced socially by club expulsion, which kills a legend.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and life-sim primary. Deception: credibility is a function of spend matching class. Travel: ticket class must match the legend. Items: wardrobe, cards, club tokens. Politics: wartime scarcity raises burn rates; a revolution destroys a household. Time: monthly debits on the world clock. Hunt: unpaid tradesmen talk; hotel registers record who paid, and how.
- **Historical grounding:** Club culture and blackballing; visiting cards and letters of introduction; the Gaming Act 1845 (gambling debts unenforceable); Monte Carlo and racecourse betting; hotel police registration.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Several burning fuses create plate-spinning tension; income spikes relieve it, then obligations accrue again — a clean tension/relief cycle; maintaining legends breeds attachment to identities the player built.
- **Engine implications:** Recurring debit events; credibility stat per legend driven by spend versus class; household entities with locations and caches.
- **Risks / open questions:** Must not become chores; aggregate the bookkeeping.

## P06.08 — Creditors Are the Second Hunter
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Debt is a pursuit system running parallel to counter-intelligence. Every creditor — bank, moneylender, tailor, casino, or an intelligence service that advanced funds — has patience, information and an enforcement method. Default escalates to bailiffs, protest of bills and bankruptcy, which the state publishes.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player borrows to bridge deals and lifestyles. A bank discounts bills and expects payment at maturity; a protested bill is a public act; a London Gazette bankruptcy notice prints the name — a legend's name — exposing it to every hunter who reads the papers. A service that paid an advance calls the loan as a demanded mission at the worst moment. Creditors share information across cities slowly (by letter) or quickly (by cable), so fleeing a jurisdiction buys time, not freedom. Pawning artifacts raises cash but puts incriminating items into the market with the player's ticket on them.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy primary. Hunt: creditors are a second, legible pursuer. Travel: jurisdiction-hopping against information lag. Deception: debts attach to identities, so a burned identity's debts die with it, tempting deliberate fraud. Items: collateral, pawn tickets. Politics: wartime moratoria; revolutionary repudiation.
- **Historical grounding:** Notarial protest of bills; London Gazette notices; Bankruptcy Acts 1883 and 1914; the German Konkursordnung; Money-lenders Act 1900; the August 1914 moratorium.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Hyperbolic discounting makes borrowing seductive and the arriving bill dreadful; a legible countdown with escalating stakes; letting an identity die with its debts is a moral-economic choice with lasting consequences.
- **Engine implications:** Creditor agents with patience timers and information propagation across cities; identity-scoped liabilities; publication events that feed the hunter.
- **Risks / open questions:** Death spirals; insolvency must be a playable reset, not a game over.

## P06.09 — Bribery with Hidden Reservation Prices
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every official has a hidden price and a preferred form of payment. Bribery is price discovery under risk, and every paid bribe is both a relationship and a liability.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Approaching a customs officer, clerk, stationmaster, police registrar or minister, the player chooses channel (direct, intermediary, contract "commission", gift, a post for a relative), amount and timing. Too low insults; too high signals desperation and invites reporting or escalation; the wrong form fails — a clerk needs cash, a governor a decoration, a chinovnik the appearance of legality. Success yields a service (a stamp, a look at a register, a delayed arrest) and a corrupted contact with a recurring price, who may later be flipped by the hunter or, dismissed, become the one who talks. Prices are indexed to regime: baksheesh economies versus Prussian rigidity; wartime desperation lowers prices but raises penalties.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and deception primary. Travel: frontiers and stations are bribe points. Politics: corruption is a property of polity and era. Items: gifts and receipts — some bribes leave paper. Time: urgent bribes cost more. Hunt: bribe-takers are the hunter's route into the player's network.
- **Historical grounding:** Ottoman and Russian officialdom; arms-contract "commissions"; hotel register inspections; the Prevention of Corruption Act 1906 as a counter-tool.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Negotiating against a hidden value is a read-the-table skill with instant feedback; a network of corrupted contacts feels like a growing power base and, equally, a growing exposure.
- **Engine implications:** Official entities with reservation price, form preference and loyalty decay; corruption indices per polity and year.
- **Risks / open questions:** Avoid "always pay" dominance; detection risk must scale with frequency.
## P06.10 — Arbitrage on the Timetable
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** A city-level price system for goods and securities, with tariffs, luggage limits and telegraph-speed information, lets the traveller profit from movement — and turns an intercepted cable into a trading edge.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each city holds prices for tradable classes (caviar, carpets, furs, machine parts, securities). Differentials arise from distance, tariff walls (free-trade Britain versus protective Germany, France, Russia), season and crisis. The player buys within luggage weight and customs risk and sells on arrival — the 80 Days trade loop made real. The deeper layer: news moves prices and travels by cable, so a player who reads a telegram before the bourse (via a tapped cable office or a bought clerk) trades ahead of it: shorting Russian bonds before a known revolution, buying marine insurance ahead of a known submarine order. Wartime adds contraband lists, blockade and neutral transshipment via the Netherlands and Scandinavia.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and travel primary. Deception: customs declarations, smuggling compartments. Items: goods with weight and provenance. Politics: tariffs, wars and blockades reshape the price field. Time: information lag is the margin. Hunt: customs searches; a too-lucky trader gets investigated.
- **Historical grounding:** Méline (1892) and Bülow (1902) tariffs; Russian 1891 tariff; British free trade to 1915; retained cable copies; Paris bourse's role in Russian debt; Netherlands Overseas Trust (1914).
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Insider trading is the purest "I know something you don't" fantasy; frequent small trades give variable-ratio reinforcement; the luggage puzzle is tactile.
- **Engine implications:** Price vectors per city with mean reversion plus event shocks; information propagation modelled separately from the event itself (an event and its news are two things).
- **Risks / open questions:** Exploitability; must not eclipse espionage as income.
## P06.11 — The Thirty-Year Storm (Currency Regimes)
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** Across 1895–1925 money itself changes nature: fixed gold parities, then suspension, exchange controls, confiscation and hyperinflation. Stored wealth is only as safe as the player's forecast of the political clock.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Before 1914 the player holds any major currency carelessly. From 1914 the world clock introduces per-country regimes: convertibility suspended, gold exports banned, moratoria, controls on cash at exits. 1917 Russia: banks nationalised, safes opened, debts repudiated — every rouble asset, bond and household evaporates unless moved in time. 1922–23: marks and crowns run to trillions; wages must be spent the hour they are paid; hard assets, foreign currency and gold rule. A player who reads the macro-events, and whose espionage supplies early warning, converts in time; one who does not watches a fortune die. Hoarding gold is itself illegal in several regimes, so the prudent player becomes a smuggler.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and politics primary. Time: regime changes are dated events; intelligence is the counter. Travel: moving wealth is physical movement across frontiers. Deception: hidden compartments, false declarations. Items: gold, jewellery, bearer bonds. Hunt: currency police and customs; wartime censors open letters that contain drafts.
- **Historical grounding:** 1914 suspension; Soviet decrees on banks and debts 1917–18; Austrian and German hyperinflation; Rentenmark 1923; British return to gold 1925.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** A looming, known catastrophe is slow-motion loss aversion; successful foresight is deeply satisfying; high replay value as players try different hedges against the same clock.
- **Engine implications:** Per-currency regime state machines; asset classes with regime-dependent liquidity; early-warning events tied to intelligence actions.
- **Risks / open questions:** Inevitable disasters can feel unfair without strong foreshadowing; largely a late-game system.

## P06.12 — Freight Shadow: Your Cargo Travels Too
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** Contraband and trade goods move on the same network as the player but on freight schedules, with bills of lading, manifests and transshipment — a second hidden-movement game in which the hunter pursues the crate.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player consigns a crate of rifles, a case of plates or a trunk of banknotes under a false manifest. It moves at freight speed (slow versus fast goods classes), stops at customs, and is inspected with a probability shaped by the manifest's plausibility, bribes paid and the current contraband regime. The player can accompany it, meet it at transshipment, or let it run. The hunter sees waybills and customs records — trace evidence of mode, like Scotland Yard's transport tokens — and may seize it or follow it to the consignee, who may be the player's legend. Seized cargo enters the hunter's provenance record.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and items primary. Economy: freight rates, insurance, demurrage. Deception: false manifests, straw consignees. Politics: contraband lists, embargoes, wartime requisition. Time: freight timetables, port demurrage. Hunt: the crate is a second fugitive.
- **Historical grounding:** Grande vitesse and petite vitesse goods classes; bills of lading; Lloyd's marine insurance; transshipment ports (Hamburg, Antwerp, Trieste, Genoa); customs at gauge-break frontiers; 1914 gun-running.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Doubling the hidden-movement puzzle creates split-attention tension; "will the crate make it" is a cliffhanger on a real timetable; a successful rendezvous is a planning triumph.
- **Engine implications:** Freight layer over the passenger graph; cargo entities with manifest and inspection logic; hunter can target cargo as well as persons.
- **Risks / open questions:** Scope; a few key routes may be enough to deliver the fantasy.
