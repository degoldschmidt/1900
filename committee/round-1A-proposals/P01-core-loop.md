# Round 1A Proposals — Core Loop & Systems Designer (Panelist P01)

## Panelist statement
I think in verbs, loops and feedback. A spy life-sim with six "incredibly deep" systems will die as six minigames unless one scarce thing is spent in all of them; here that thing must be *time on a schedule*, and the second thing must be *visibility*. Every proposal below is a way to make travel, deception, items, money, politics and the hunt draw on the same two accounts, so that every 30-second decision (which train, which hotel, which name) moves the 10-minute loop (the day's appointments), the session loop (the commission) and the campaign loop (how long until the file on you is thick enough). The game must get the cadence right first; depth is cheap once the cadence is compulsive.

## P01.01 — The Departure Is the Turn
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** No abstract turn. The world runs on a continuous clock and the player's decision points are *departures*: every action is placed in the gaps between timetabled trains, steamers and mail collections. The itinerary is the turn structure.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Every 30 seconds: pick what fits before the departure you have provisionally booked (meet, send, buy, observe, wait). Every 10 minutes: assemble a day as time-stamped slots (bank hours, postal deliveries, a 14:05 express) where *slack* is the real resource. Every session: one commission carried across two or three cities. Every campaign: thirty years in which the network speeds up (expresses, telephones, airlines) while borders harden (1914+): the same verbs, faster and riskier.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel is the skeleton; deception, items, business and politics are actions that *take hours* and compete for the same slots; time pressure is native; hunters read the same timetable and cordon stations at departure times. The spy's life is an itinerary.
- **Historical grounding:** Monthly Bradshaw's Continental Guide; through-trains; customs stops; the Russian gauge break; bank hours; several mail deliveries a day; wartime requisition deleting services.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The "one more leg" pull of 80 Days generalised to every verb; the near-miss effect (connection missed by eleven minutes); Zeigarnik tension from booked departures not yet earned by a finished task. Slack management is the mastery curve.
- **Engine implications:** A global priority queue of scheduled events (departures, deliveries, openings) plus player intentions; timetables as monthly data tables; interrupts (a telegram) re-open the slot planner. Simulate the player's city and legs in flight at minute granularity, elsewhere at day granularity.
- **Risks / open questions:** Spreadsheet feel; needs strong defaults ("next sensible departure") so planning depth is opt-in.

## P01.02 — Wants and Windows
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** No scripted missions. The world simulation emits *wants* (an institution will pay for a document, introduction, assessment or delivery by a date) and *windows* (that thing is obtainable at a place during an interval). The game is matching windows to wants across the graph before either closes.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Scan the board (newspapers, rumours, handlers, correspondence) → pick a want you can fund and reach → find or create a window → route yourself or a cut-out → extract → deliver → get paid in cash, credit or protection. One window can feed several wants (selling the same intelligence twice, at risk); a want without a window can be met by forgery.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and politics *generate* the wants (a naval scare raises blueprint prices; a concession auction creates a want for a ministry's intentions); travel is the matching constraint; items are the goods; deception fabricates windows; the hunt prices visible extraction; deadlines are the time pressure.
- **Historical grounding:** Commission agents for arms firms; concession hunting in Persia, Manchuria and Ottoman lands; services buying documents from freelancers; newspaper editions and Reuters/Havas/Wolff wires as the public signal of demand.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Variable-ratio reward (the next window is uncertain but legible), autonomy (you choose the want) and the trader's delight of arbitrage. Open wants are open loops; the board refreshes every edition.
- **Engine implications:** Wants and windows as typed records with open/close times, generated from world-state triggers through templates with parameterised value curves; a dependency link so fulfilment perturbs the state that generates further wants. Volume comes from templates, not authoring.
- **Risks / open questions:** Generated wants can feel samey; needs rich want *types* and consequences that visibly reshape the board.

## P01.03 — One Ledger: Cash, Credit, Heat
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Three accounts; every action moves at least two. **Cash** (gold-standard money, convertible everywhere until 1914). **Credit** (trust held with each institution — services, banks, firms, parties — non-transferable and slow to earn). **Heat** (what each police or intelligence service knows about each legend; decaying with time and distance, spiking with registered acts). The loop is converting one into another at favourable rates.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Sell intelligence: credit → cash, plus heat. Bribe a hotel clerk: cash → lower heat. Take a commission: heat → credit. Burn a legend: zero its heat, lose its accounts, addresses and letters of introduction. The player watches three balances per legend and city; the hunt is simply heat crossing a threshold in a jurisdiction you are inside.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and deception are the exchange mechanisms; travel changes which jurisdiction's heat applies; items (papers, codebooks) are stored credit; politics sets exchange rates (a service at war pays more for less); the hunt is heat turned hostile; the spy's life is the legend portfolio.
- **Historical grounding:** Fixed parities (£1 ≈ 25.2 francs ≈ 20.4 marks); letters of introduction and credit; hotel registration feeding police files; Okhrana Paris bureau and Special Branch dossiers; post-1914 passport controls making heat sticky.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Legible conversions build mastery; loss aversion over hard-earned credit; the endowment effect makes burning a well-built legend hurt. Three balances are few enough to reason about, too many to optimise.
- **Engine implications:** Credit as a sparse (legend × institution) matrix, heat as (legend × jurisdiction); every action declares deltas; decay tied to the world clock; thresholds emit hunt events.
- **Risks / open questions:** Heat must stay estimated, not exact, or the hunt loses its dread.

## P01.04 — The Visibility Price
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every service the world sells — a train class, a hotel, a bank, a telegraph office, a cab — sits on a triangle of *speed, cost and visibility*. You may have two. This is the thirty-second decision the game is made of.
- **How it plays (core loop):** First class with a sleeping-car berth is fast and rested, but the conductor sees your papers and the hotel at the end registers you with the police. Third class is anonymous and exhausting. A cable is instant and leaves a copy at the office; a letter is slow and private; a courier costs credit. The player chooses a visibility profile per leg and action; the hunt consumes the visible residue.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and communication (coding) are the surfaces; economy prices the choice; items (forged papers, a second passport) bend the triangle; politics bends it for whole regions (wartime: everything visible); time pressure makes speed tempting; the hunt reads only what you made visible.
- **Historical grounding:** 1st/2nd/3rd class fares; Wagons-Lits sleeping cars; hotel police registration (Germany, Austria, France, Russia); telegraph companies retaining cable copies; poste restante; grand hotels versus pensions; motor taxis replacing cabs c. 1905–10.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** A clean trilemma produces interesting choice at the smallest scale and makes consequences attributable — the player knows *which* comfort betrayed them. Micro risk-reward makes the macro hunt feel fair rather than arbitrary.
- **Engine implications:** Each service entry carries (duration, price, visibility vector by observer type); actions emit trace records tagged with observer, mode, place, time; the hunter AI consumes only trace records, never ground truth.
- **Risks / open questions:** Players may converge on one profile; counter with fatigue, baggage limits and police attention to the always-anonymous.

## P01.05 — Burn Rate
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Idleness costs money. Every legend, household, cover address, retainer and sub-agent has a daily burn; every debt accrues interest; every hotel night is a night on a register. Burn rate is the forcing function that turns a sandbox into a loop.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player maintains a portfolio of standing costs (a flat in Paris, a cable address in London, a bank account in Petersburg, a second household elsewhere — line items, not characters). Income is lumpy (commissions, deals), costs are continuous, so the player must periodically earn, which means moving, which means being seen. Gambling and speculation exist as high-variance ways to cover burn. Insolvency escalates: creditors, lawsuits, bailiffs — each a registered act that feeds heat.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy is primary; travel is forced by the need to work; deception is funded by cash; items (jewels, bearer bonds) are liquid reserves; politics changes burn (inflation, requisition, expropriation); the hunt feeds on money's paper trail; time pressure is literal.
- **Historical grounding:** Multiple households across cities as a documented agent lifestyle; letters of credit and circular notes; interest and bills of exchange; wartime inflation and exchange controls; German hyperinflation 1922–23, in which mark-denominated debts evaporate while mark-denominated income collapses.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Pressure without a scripted timer; loss aversion on the household you built; the gambler's bargain of one big deal. It stops turtling, the enemy of every immersive sim.
- **Engine implications:** Standing obligations as recurring scheduled events in a jurisdictional currency; insolvency as a dependency chain (default → suit → warrant). Trivial to scale; all arithmetic.
- **Risks / open questions:** Tuning: too steep and the game is a treadmill; too shallow and players never leave the safehouse.

## P01.06 — The Information Front (risky)
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** Knowledge about you travels at cable speed; you travel at train speed. Every revelation (photograph, name, description) propagates from its origin across the telegraph and postal network at the real latency of each link. The hunt is the race between your body and your own description.
- **How it plays (core loop):** After a visible act the player sees, as an estimated front on the network, where their description has probably reached. Choices: outrun it toward places not yet on the wire or off the cable's path; slow it (bribe a telegraph clerk, cross a frontier before the warrant is relayed, exploit wartime cable cuts); or change the thing described (new legend) so the front chases a ghost. Every border, hotel and bank is a checkpoint against what has arrived.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel versus communication latency is the whole game; deception resets the front; items (camera, wanted notice) are its payload; economy buys speed; politics decides which offices talk (allies share, enemies do not; 1914 rewires the graph); the hunt *is* the front.
- **Historical grounding:** The All Red Line (1902); telegraph versus rail speeds; shipboard wireless reaching liners mid-ocean (early 1900s); Okhrana–Sûreté cooperation; Bertillon cards circulated by post; wartime censorship and cut cables.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Dread made legible: the player can *see* what chases them and reason about it, converting anxiety into planning. Escape feels earned, capture fair, every crossing a tension-relief beat.
- **Engine implications:** Knowledge tokens propagating over a weighted graph with per-edge latency and per-node sharing policy; checkpoints query local knowledge only. Lazy propagation (evaluated on query) keeps it scalable.
- **Risks / open questions:** Visualising a probabilistic front without creating a minimap exploit; defining what the player legitimately knows of hunters' knowledge.

## P01.07 — The Double Book (risky)
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The spy's product is the *report*. The player serves several paymasters and writes each a version of events; the game keeps two books — what happened, and what each service believes — and survival is their consistency.
- **How it plays (core loop):** After every episode the player composes reports from tokens they hold (observed facts, documents, rumours) plus fabrications. Each service pays for novelty and corroboration with its other sources. Services cross-check against each other's leaks, newspapers and world events; a report contradicted later costs credit, and one proven *impossible* (you claimed Vienna on a day a hotel register placed you in Berlin) opens a counter-intelligence case. The player carries a consistency debt that grows with every lie and is paid down by making lies true (planting evidence, arranging the event).
- **Directive coverage:** Deception and coding are primary (reports travel ciphered; interception leaks them); economy is report income; politics sets what each service wants to hear; travel creates alibis and contradictions; items are evidence; the hunt begins inside your own employers; report deadlines are the time pressure.
- **Historical grounding:** Freelance agents paid per report by several services; sharing policy following alliances (1904, 1907); Room 40 reading cables; hotel registers as alibi checks; the press as corroboration.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The cognitive load of maintaining lies is the gameplay — the heist pleasure of "did they notice?". Dread of the cross-check, relief at the paid report, emergent stories of the lie that came true.
- **Engine implications:** A provenance-tagged fact store; per-service belief graphs; a consistency checker run on new evidence; deterministic, so contradictions can be explained afterwards.
- **Risks / open questions:** Composition must be token-based, never free text; belief modelling cost at scale.

## P01.08 — Perishable Prizes
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every item of intelligence or artifact has a value curve over time, a buyer set, and a *provenance* recording which legend obtained it, where and how. Selling realises cash and leaks provenance; holding loses value; carrying is a liability at every customs stop.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Obtain (steal, buy, photograph, copy) → decide: sell now locally for less, or carry to the best buyer across the graph before the curve decays (a mobilisation table is worthless after mobilisation; a codebook after the code changes; a concession map after the auction). Copy it (hours, a camera, exposure) to sell twice. Launder provenance through a cut-out at a discount. Physical artifacts (jewels, bonds, plans) need concealment compartments and are exactly what border searches find.
- **Directive coverage:** Items and economy are primary; travel is the delivery constraint; deception launders provenance; politics draws the curves (a crisis spikes demand, then collapses it); the hunt follows provenance back to a legend; time pressure is the decay itself.
- **Historical grounding:** Naval plans and blueprints as the age's prize; commercial and diplomatic codebooks changed periodically; Brownie (1900) and Vest Pocket Kodak (1912) copying; frontier customs inspection; the Zimmermann telegram's value lying in its timing.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Perishable inventory is the strongest urgency device in trading games (80 Days' luggage with a clock attached); provenance adds a sting of consequence to every sale, so the trade-off is felt, not read.
- **Engine implications:** Item records with value functions keyed to world-state variables, buyer lists derived from faction wants, provenance chains; decay evaluated lazily on inspection or sale.
- **Risks / open questions:** Too many curve types confuse; show value only as a buyer's quote, never as a raw number.

## P01.09 — Three Clocks
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The game runs three clocks at different frequencies — the *day clock* (hours, appointments, departures), the *world calendar* (crises, wars, laws, seasons) and the *reveal clock* (the cadence at which hunters compare notes and the player learns something of what is known) — and pacing is designed by how they beat against each other.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Minute to minute the player lives on the day clock. Every few days the reveal clock ticks: a notice appears in a paper, a hotel is visited, a cable is answered; the player gets a partial read of their own heat (Scotland Yard's reveal, inverted). Monthly or yearly the world calendar shifts rules: a new passport regime, a frontier closed by war, a currency collapse. The compulsive moments are *alignments*: a commission deadline, a frontier closure and the next reveal landing in the same week.
- **Directive coverage:** Time pressure and the hunt are primary; travel lives on the day clock; politics and warfare are the world calendar; economy and items follow both; deception resets the reveal clock for a legend.
- **Historical grounding:** The fixed macro-calendar 1895–1925 (Boer War, 1905, Balkan Wars, 1914, 1917, 1923); seasonal ice closures; monthly timetables; multi-edition dailies; periodic police file circulation.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Tension-relief cycles need a rhythm, and three interleaved rhythms produce the "one more turn" effect — something is always about to resolve. Alignments are emergent set-pieces with no author.
- **Engine implications:** A multi-cadence scheduler that lets designers *query* upcoming alignments to bias generated wants and windows toward them; the world calendar as data with perturbation hooks.
- **Risks / open questions:** The reveal clock must never become a visible countdown; it is inferred from events, not displayed.

## P01.10 — Politics as the Price Field
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Politics and warfare are not a separate screen. Each region carries a *tension vector* (between powers, regime and opposition, labour and capital) that is a multiplier field over every other system's prices: fares, border friction, visibility, document values, commission payouts, interest rates. The player reads the field and, rarely and expensively, nudges it.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Daily: read the papers to update your estimate of the field. Weekly: choose where to operate given gradients (high tension = high payouts, high visibility, closing routes). Rarely: spend credit and cash on influence actions (fund a newspaper, broker an arms contract, carry a message between factions) that move a tension value with lagged, leaky effect. War is the field saturating: routes vanish, passports appear, currencies float, services stop sharing.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics and warfare are primary but expressed entirely through travel, economy, deception and the hunt; time pressure comes from the field moving on its own schedule; the spy's business is reading it before the market does.
- **Historical grounding:** Moroccan and Bosnian crises as price shocks; Balkan Wars closing routes; 1914 passport regimes; alliance shifts (1904, 1907) changing which services cooperate; rouble collapse after 1917; concessions as political instruments.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Reading the field is a perception-mastery skill (forecasting from newspapers), and influence is rare enough to feel momentous; the strategist's pleasure without a wargame bolted on.
- **Engine implications:** A small vector per region updated by the world calendar and by faction/player actions; all price lookups are functions of it; a dependency graph for lagged propagation between neighbouring regions.
- **Risks / open questions:** Accuracy versus agency — the field must bend around the fixed macro-events, never override them.

## P01.11 — The Press Is the Scoreboard
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player receives almost no direct feedback. Consequences arrive through period channels — newspaper editions, telegrams, post, rumour in conversation — each with its own latency, reliability and cost. The world's reaction to you is something you *read*, days later, in a paper you had to buy.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Morning and evening editions summarise world-state deltas (a concession granted, a ministry fallen, an unnamed foreigner sought over a theft). Telegrams carry urgent, short, interceptable updates. Letters carry rich, slow, private ones. Rumour is cheap and wrong. The player triangulates. Then the loop inverts: the player *writes* into the channels — plants a story, sends a false cable to be intercepted, posts a letter to a watched address — and the scoreboard becomes a weapon.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception and coding are primary (channels are the attack surface); politics is what the papers report; economy pays for subscriptions, cables and journalists; travel sets which editions you can buy where; the hunt talks through the same channels (wanted notices, hotel queries by wire); editions are the clock.
- **Historical grounding:** Multi-edition dailies; Reuters/Havas/Wolff sharing agreements; pneumatic post; several deliveries a day; cable copies retained; press wanted notices; wartime censorship blacking out columns.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Delayed, mediated feedback produces anticipation (the walk to the kiosk), information-gap curiosity and the detective pleasure of reading your own actions in the third person. Planting stories is agency over the feedback loop itself.
- **Engine implications:** One event log rendered through channel-specific "lenses" (lag, reliability, censorship) into generated items; player-authored insertions enter the same log tagged as claims; hunters read lenses too.
- **Risks / open questions:** Generated prose quality; mitigate with structured headlines and tables rather than articles.

## P01.12 — Span of Control
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** You can be in one place; the board has many windows. The player builds a network of cut-outs, cover addresses and sub-agents across cities and acts remotely by post and cable, paying in latency, cost and interception risk. Presence is fast and certain but visible; delegation slow and private but corruptible.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each session the player decides where their body goes (the most valuable or fragile window) and what the network does elsewhere (instructions dispatched with hours-to-days latency). Sub-agents report back the same way: late, garbled or — if turned — false. The player grows the network with credit and cash, protects it with cut-outs and codes, prunes it when a node's heat rises. Travel collapses latency to zero for one node at a time.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel versus communication is the structural trade-off; deception and coding protect channels; economy pays retainers; items (codebooks, cover addresses) are infrastructure; politics changes reachability; the hunt attacks nodes and reads channels; deadlines decide whether delegation is affordable.
- **Historical grounding:** Cover addresses and cut-outs; commercial code books; poste restante; per-word telegraph rates making codes economic; Okhrana informants inside émigré networks; wartime censorship delaying post.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The agony of delegation — learning what your agent did three days ago — is a strong anticipation loop; span of control gives a strategy-layer pleasure (where should I be?) renewed every session.
- **Engine implications:** Agents as autonomous actors with goal scripts, loyalty state and message queues; delivery over the same latency graph as the Information Front; turning an agent is a hunter action.
- **Risks / open questions:** Remote action must stay less efficient than presence or travel withers; sub-agent behaviour must be legible.
