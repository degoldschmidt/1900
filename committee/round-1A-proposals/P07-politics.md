# Round 1A Proposals — Politics & Warfare Designer (Panelist P07)

## Panelist statement
My lens is the macro-world as a machine the player can touch but never steer: intelligence services, ministries, armies and revolutionary movements as factions with budgets, agendas and beliefs; wars and crises as scheduled processes running on real timetables; and dependency chains that carry one agent's small acts into geopolitics and back into his own life. The game must get one paradox right: the Great War begins in August 1914 whatever the player does, yet everything at the player's scale — who wins the contract, which service takes the credit, whose file is opened — is genuinely his to perturb. If both halves hold, foreknowledge becomes a resource, every faction becomes a market, and the hunt becomes a political process rather than a chase.

## P07.01 — The Many-Mastered Agent
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player is a freelance asset on the books of several intelligence services and ministries at once, each a faction with a budget, standing requirements and a *belief state* about him — what it thinks he knows and whom he serves. The game is managing those beliefs for twenty-five years.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Services post requirements drawn from current worries. The player accepts tasks, travels to fulfil them, decides which service gets which fragment and what "chickenfeed" the others get, and watches per-service trust and suspicion gauges. Services cross-check: a report too good, or surfacing in a rival's hands, moves suspicion. When a belief flips to "double", the service stops paying and starts hunting — or runs him against his other patrons.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics primary. Travel: requirements are geographic; clashing deadlines force route choices. Deception/coding: one cipher and one legend per service. Items: documents with provenance. Economy: retainers, bonuses, audited expenses. Time pressure: requirements expire with crises. Hunt: counter-intelligence sections pursue, funded by the player's successes.
- **Historical grounding:** Commission agents serving several governments; Secret Service Bureau (1909), Deuxième Bureau, Evidenzbureau, Abteilung IIIb, Okhrana foreign bureau; annual secret-service votes.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Plate-spinning tension — concurrent commitments whose collisions are foreseeable but never fully preventable; dramatic irony (the player knows more than any faction); variable-ratio payoffs when a fragment sold twice pays twice; dread-and-relief at every cross-check.
- **Engine implications:** Per-faction belief models (tagged fact sets, suspicion scalars, attributed sources); requirement generators fed by the world clock; leak propagation along alliance edges; yearly budgets scaling task value and hunter capacity.
- **Risks / open questions:** Legibility of belief states without exposing them; cross-checks must bite so "sell everything to everyone" never dominates.
## P07.02 — Tramlines and Switchpoints: Three-Tier Bounded History
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every world fact is tagged Tier 0 (immovable: war dates, revolutions, treaties), Tier 1 (bounded within plausible windows: who wins the 1906 Serbian artillery contract, which firm finances a railway section) or Tier 2 (free: arrests, exposures, prices, credit). Actions push *pressure accumulators* on Tier 1 and 2 variables; saturation guarantees no pressure moves a Tier 0 rail.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player never sees tiers, only a world that answers him at human scale and stays textbook-accurate over decades. Stealing a battleship's gunnery tables changes which yard gets the follow-on order and which service gets the budget — never whether the naval race happens.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics primary. Travel: through-service inauguration days jitter within their season (Tier 1); the 1914 closures are Tier 0. Deception: exposures are Tier 2, propagating upward as pressure. Items: documents carry *lever weight* against Tier 1 variables. Economy: contract outcomes are Tier 1. Hunt: hunter budgets are Tier 1 outputs of Tier 2 events. Time pressure: windows close.
- **Historical grounding:** The 1906 Serbian artillery contract (Schneider over Škoda) and the ensuing Austro-Serbian customs war; section-by-section financing of the Baghdad railway; naval orders after the 1906 Dreadnought.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** "I mattered, and the world is still real" — agency with credibility; detective pleasure in tracing cause through the dependency graph; a bounded sandbox invites experimentation without breaking the period contract.
- **Engine implications:** Tiered variable schema; pressure accumulators with decay and saturation; weighted dependency edges; a scheduler reconciling Tier 1 windows with the Tier 0 clock. A new decade adds rows, not code.
- **Risks / open questions:** Players testing the rails may feel the designer's hand; saturation must present as institutional inertia, not a wall.
## P07.03 — Crisis Ladders
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Diplomatic crises (Fashoda 1898, Morocco 1905 and 1911, Bosnia 1908–09, July 1914) run as explicit state machines — note, counter-note, ultimatum with a literal deadline, partial mobilisation, general mobilisation — each rung altering the world's parameters for its duration, some permanently.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Days before the first rung the player reads price twitches, cable volume and cancelled leave; during the ladder, borders tighten, trains are requisitioned, bourses panic, services open emergency funds, hunters gain emergency powers. The player runs toward the crisis (best pay, most exposure) or away (asset preservation). Some rungs leave *ratchets*: laws and registrations that never revert.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics and time pressure primary. Travel: the timetable degrades rung by rung; at general mobilisation the military schedule overwrites the civilian one. Deception: emergencies check papers never checked before. Items: ultimatum texts and cipher cables are the week's prizes. Economy: short-dated plays in gold, insurance, shipping. Hunt: emergency powers raise the hunters' reveal cadence.
- **Historical grounding:** The 48-hour Austrian ultimatum of 23 July 1914; the 12-hour German ultimatum to Russia of 31 July; the Official Secrets Act 1911 passed in an afternoon during Agadir; Aliens Restriction Act of 5 August 1914; mobilisation plans as railway timetables.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Countdown dread and relief; scarcity windows creating diegetic appointments ("be in Vienna by Thursday"); ratchets reward learning the diplomatic calendar as mastery; because the ladder cannot be stopped, tension is pure.
- **Engine implications:** Ladders as data (rungs, durations, Tier 1 jitter, parameter deltas); global modifiers per rung; permanent ratchets; hooks into travel graph, price model and hunter rulesets.
- **Risks / open questions:** Too many crises numb; density must follow the real calendar, long calms included.
## P07.04 — War as Weather
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Wars are long-running world regimes, not events. When one runs (Boer, Russo-Japanese, Balkan, Great), a layered rule overlay changes travel, identity, money, communication and hunting across whole regions; fronts move on the historical schedule; neutral capitals become the game's bazaars.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player prepares before the switch — relocating households, opening neutral accounts, acquiring a neutral identity — then lives inside it. Contraband lists make trade illegal and lucrative; censorship forces coded correspondence; enemy-alien status threatens the internment of an identity's household; a cover identity of military age receives call-up papers; exit permits become mandatory. Neutral hubs (Bern, Rotterdam, Copenhagen, Stockholm, Madrid) concentrate every service's buyers in a few hotels.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics/warfare primary. Travel: requisition, blockade zones, submarine risk on sea legs. Deception: an identity's nationality becomes life-or-death. Items: contraband, passes, permits. Economy: profiteering versus expropriation and exchange controls. Time pressure: fronts move whether or not you are there. Hunt: states of siege, alien registration, censors reading your post.
- **Historical grounding:** Britain cut Germany's Atlantic cables on 5 August 1914; Defence of the Realm Act; enemy-alien internment; navicerts (1916); the Bolshevik publication of the secret treaties (November 1917).
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The pleasure of preparation meeting contact — plans survive or shatter; regime change renews a world the player had mastered (optimal-challenge reset); neutral hubs are dense social-deduction arenas.
- **Engine implications:** Regime overlays as modifier stacks on graph edges, prices, legal rules and faction powers; front lines as time-indexed polygons; identity attributes (nationality, age) checked against regime rules.
- **Risks / open questions:** Four years is a long regime; it needs internal phases (1914 mobility, 1915–16 stasis, 1917 Russian collapse, 1918 fluidity).
## P07.05 — The Requirements Market: Secrets Have Expiry Dates
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every stolen document, intercepted cable or observed fact has a value curve over time for each faction — rising as the related crisis approaches, collapsing when superseded or published — and a holding risk, because possession is evidence. The player trades on timing.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A naval construction programme is worth little in 1905, a fortune during the 1909 scare, nothing once the estimates are tabled. The player sells now, holds for the spike, splits copies across buyers, or burns. Rival agents race the same curves: a document becomes worthless the moment another seller delivers. Holding means concealment compartments, dead drops and sweating through customs stops.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and politics primary. Items: documents with provenance, copies, forgeries that pass until cross-checked. Travel: deliver before the window closes, across borders that may now demand passports. Deception: selling the same secret twice under different legends. Time pressure: explicit value curves. Hunt: holding time multiplies exposure.
- **Historical grounding:** The 1913 compromise of Austro-Hungarian deployment plans; mobilisation schedules as the prize document of the age; the 1909 naval scare; the Zimmermann telegram, whose publication destroyed its secret value while creating political value.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Timing games engage loss aversion and regret aversion at once ("sold too early", "held too long"); watching curves gives a reason to return daily; forecasting pleasure in reading the diplomatic calendar correctly.
- **Engine implications:** Value functions per item class per faction, parameterised by distance to scheduled events and by supersession events; a daily market-clearing step; forgery-detection probability by buyer competence.
- **Risks / open questions:** Must not collapse into spreadsheet play; the physical act of holding and moving must stay dangerous.

## P07.06 — Your File Travels with the Alliances
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky)
- **Central mechanical hook:** Each service keeps a dossier on each of the player's identities. Dossiers are copied or traded — partially, with delays — when states align (Entente 1904, Convention 1907, war coalitions), inherited by revolutionary successors (Cheka from Okhrana, 1917), and lost when regimes fall. The diplomatic calendar therefore decides who will know what about you, and when.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player watches approaching realignments: a legend burned in Paris is safe in St Petersburg until 1907, then not. He can race the merger — break in and thin the file, make himself indispensable to the receiving service before it reads him, or retire the identity. Successor states inherit incomplete files, opening windows of invisibility after collapses.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and politics primary. Deception: identity hygiene across alliance blocs. Travel: where you can still go is a function of where your file has not yet arrived. Items: the file as a stealable artifact (photographs, Bertillon cards). Economy: buying archivists. Time pressure: merger dates.
- **Historical grounding:** Okhrana Paris bureau cooperation with the French police; Anglo-Russian Convention 1907; wartime Allied intelligence exchange; the partial destruction and inheritance of Okhrana archives in 1917; Bertillonage and fingerprint records.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Foreseeable dread — the player knows the date his past arrives and must act first; Zeigarnik tension around unfinished identity clean-ups; catharsis when a collapse wipes a slate.
- **Engine implications:** Dossier as faction-held fact sets with provenance and confidence; alliance graph whose edge events copy facts; regime-fall events that delete or mutate stores; hunter AI reads only its own dossier.
- **Risks / open questions:** Legibility: the player needs some way to learn roughly what a service holds, perhaps only by taking risks.

## P07.07 — Doomed Projects
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player may join or sponsor political projects — coups, counter-revolutions, separatist risings, concession wars — modelled as multi-stage ventures with resources, timetables and hidden feasibility. Some are Tier 0 doomed: history forbids their success. They can still be played to the hilt, because the game scores *extraction*, not victory: get money, people and reputation out before the collapse.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A project needs funds, arms, officers' loyalty, foreign backing and timing. Each stage consumes travel, bribes and secrecy; each leak raises the counter-intelligence tempo. Feasibility is read from intelligence, never shown. At collapse the project becomes a pursuit: evacuate by scheduled routes while the new regime's service hunts.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics/warfare primary. Hunt: collapse is the Scotland Yard phase. Travel: evacuation under closing frontiers. Deception: false flags; who really funds whom. Items: arms consignments, gold. Economy: project finance as venture capital paying only in Tier 1 and 2 successes. Time pressure: the project's clock races the world's.
- **Historical grounding:** Anti-Bolshevik conspiracies sponsored by Allied missions in 1918; the Russian Civil War; émigré counter-revolution to 1925; the 1912 US arms embargo on Mexico and the gun-running it bred.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Commitment escalation under uncertainty, redeemed by mastery of exit timing; the gambler's "one more stage"; emergent tragedy from systems rather than scripts — the player's own venture becomes the story he tells afterwards.
- **Engine implications:** Project templates as stage graphs with resource gates; feasibility values hidden behind intelligence quality; collapse triggers that spawn pursuit states and city regime changes.
- **Risks / open questions:** Players may resent guaranteed failure if they detect it; extraction must read as real victory, and real Tier 1 wins must exist elsewhere.
## P07.08 — Betting on the Calendar
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player's historical foreknowledge is a resource with a price. He can act on 1914 before anyone else — but the factions model *prescience* as evidence. Being right too often, too early, about too much marks him as an insider, a plotter or a provocateur, and the hunters recalibrate.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player quietly shorts shipping, buys armament shares and moves households to Switzerland in spring 1914. Every prescient act adds to a suspicion-of-foreknowledge score read by services and police; thresholds trigger investigation, frozen accounts or a recruitment approach ("you knew — who told you?"). Tier 1 jitter keeps textbook knowledge approximate: the player knows the war is coming, not the day a given frontier closes, so bets remain bets.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and hunt primary, politics as the clock. Travel: pre-positioning before closures. Deception: laundering prescience through plausible cover reasons — a business pretext for being in Zurich. Items: gold and neutral papers bought early. Time pressure: a window before each macro-event.
- **Historical grounding:** Bourse panics and bank runs of late July 1914; moratoria and exchange controls; prewar spy fever; wartime trading-with-the-enemy prosecutions.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Forecasting pleasure (being right) tempered by risk — a built-in temptation loop; the inversion that the player's meta-knowledge becomes diegetic danger; replay value as players refine how much to exploit and how to disguise it.
- **Engine implications:** Per-faction prescience accumulator keyed on actions correlated with upcoming Tier 0 events; cover-reason tags that discount suspicion; jittered Tier 1 windows.
- **Risks / open questions:** Players without historical knowledge act on in-world signals and must not be disadvantaged; threshold tuning is delicate.

## P07.09 — Influence Chains
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Political effects are achieved only through chains of cultivated nodes — generic role-holders such as a contracts clerk, a staff officer's orderly, a bourse jobber, an agency correspondent — each with access, loyalty vector, upkeep and exposure. A desired effect (swing a contract, kill a story, delay a visa) resolves as a path search through the network; every hop costs time, money and risk.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player builds a network across cities while rival services prune it through arrests, transfers and turning. Nodes are activated by meetings, requiring travel, or by coded correspondence, slower and interceptable. Hunters read activations as trace: a cluster of meetings in Trieste is a reveal.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics primary. Travel: nodes are physical, so reach is bounded by the timetable. Deception/coding: cover addresses and a cipher per node. Items: letters of introduction and gifts as access keys. Economy: the network is a cost centre that must earn its retainers. Time pressure: effects must land before decisions are taken. Hunt: activation as footprint.
- **Historical grounding:** Letters of introduction and visiting cards as the access regime; commission agents' networks inside war and marine ministries; news-agency correspondents as information nodes; cover addresses and poste restante.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Ownership and the IKEA effect — a network built over years is beloved; loss aversion when a node is burned; the click of a long chain resolving; nurturing play between crises.
- **Engine implications:** Graph of role-nodes with attributes; path-cost resolution against desired effects on Tier 1 and 2 variables; rival pruning as scheduled faction actions; activation events feeding hunter inference.
- **Risks / open questions:** Nodes must remain roles with numbers, not characters; per-node content stays procedural.
## P07.10 — The Concession Hunter
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** A career built on commercial diplomacy in the semi-sovereign lands (Ottoman, Persian, Manchurian, Chinese) where every railway, oil or mining concession needs a grantor, a syndicate, a Great Power sponsor and a defeated rival — and where espionage is the tool, not the job.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player identifies a concession in play, travels to the grantor's capital and assembles a chain (introduction → court faction → finance house → embassy backing) while rival Powers' agents do the same. He steals the rival bid, bribes the clerk who times the decree, plants a story about the rival's insolvency. Payoff is commission plus a syndicate seat — long-dated income that becomes political exposure when the Powers fall out. Concessions are Tier 1: holders vary; that oil is struck in Persia in 1908 does not.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and politics co-primary. Travel: long legs by Trans-Siberian, Suez and the Baghdad railway's advancing railhead. Deception: bidding under cover, forging telegraphic authority. Items: decrees, firmans, share certificates. Time pressure: decrees expire, rival ultimatums land. Hunt: the embarrassed Power's service pursues the agent responsible.
- **Historical grounding:** Baghdad railway concession (1903); Persian oil concession (1901) and the Anglo-Russian spheres of 1907; Yalu timber and Manchurian railway concessions feeding the Russo-Japanese War; Ottoman Public Debt Administration as gatekeeper.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Deal-making highs on an intermittent schedule; long-horizon payoffs persisting as income; success itself breeds tension because the syndicate becomes a target.
- **Engine implications:** Concession objects with multi-party state; sponsor and rival faction AI that bids; income streams and expropriation events tied to regime changes.
- **Risks / open questions:** Pacing may be slow between deals; the hunt layer must keep sessions tense.
## P07.11 — Pressure Systems: Cities with a Regime State
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every city carries regime-state variables — police effectiveness, strike pressure, arms availability, émigré funding, curfew — driven toward the scheduled revolutions (Tier 0) but perturbable by the player (Tier 1 and 2). They are the interface between politics and everything else: papers checked, prices, trains running, who hunts you.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player runs rifles to a restive port by steamer, funds an émigré press elsewhere, sells a cell to a third city's police; each act moves dials and earns from a faction. As a scheduled revolution nears, dials converge to history, but the player decides which quarters fall first, how long the general strike halts the railways, whose arms depot is seized.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics primary. Travel: strikes and curfews as dynamic edge closures (the October 1905 railway strike closed a nation). Deception: legends judged against regime state — a bourgeois cover in a soviet-held city is a liability. Items: rifles, presses, the right currency. Economy: black markets scale with stress. Time pressure: revolutions on schedule. Hunt: police effectiveness is literally the hunter's reveal cadence.
- **Historical grounding:** 1905 general strike and Moscow rising; Young Turk Revolution 1908; Mexican Revolution and the 1912 US arms embargo; Petrograd 1917; Irish War of Independence.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Dynamic-system play — small inputs, visible local effects; presence at history with the player's fingerprints on its texture; difficulty that breathes with the world rather than a slider.
- **Engine implications:** Per-city variable vectors with convergence functions toward scheduled states; hooks into travel edges, prices, hunter rulesets and identity checks; faction actions as pressure inputs.
- **Risks / open questions:** Convergence must not feel like a leash; differentiated city trajectories are where the design earns its keep.
## P07.12 — The Scare Economy: Press, Opinion, Credit and Blame
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Public opinion and bureaucratic attribution are the conduits through which the player's small acts reach budgets and laws. A planted story raises a *scare* variable (naval, spy, invasion) pressuring ministries' estimates; every political outcome distributes credit and blame tokens among services — credit buys budget, blame buys inquiries and purges.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player leaks to an agency correspondent, times the story to a budget debate, profits from armament shares, collects a bonus from the service that gets the credit — or engineers blame onto the rival service hunting him. Public credit builds reputation (letters open, fees rise) but fattens his dossier; anonymous credit is safer and poorer.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics primary. Economy: scares move shares and orders. Deception: forged provenance. Travel: editions are city-timed; a story must reach the right capital before the evening edition. Items: the planted document. Time pressure: debate dates, estimates seasons. Hunt: blame sharpens a service's zeal; reputation raises visibility.
- **Historical grounding:** The 1909 naval scare driving dreadnought estimates; spy fever preceding the Secret Service Bureau (1909); multi-edition mass dailies; the Admiralty, War Office and Press Committee (1912) as censorship ratchet; Reuters, Havas and Wolff as the wire.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Visible macro feedback from micro acts; status set against safety, so the fame-versus-anonymity dilemma recurs rather than resolving once; schadenfreude when a hunting service is defunded.
- **Engine implications:** Scare variables per state and topic with decay; attribution ledger keyed to outcomes and visible actors; budget functions reading credit; press as propagation medium with edition timing.
- **Risks / open questions:** Must stay within Tier 1 bounds (scares cannot build fleets that never existed); the press must not become a cheap win-button.
