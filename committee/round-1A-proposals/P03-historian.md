# Round 1A Proposals — Period Historian (1895–1925) (Panelist P03)

## Panelist statement
My lens is archival: the period's institutions — timetables, hotel registers, cable copies, letters of credit, visa regimes, censorship offices, commercial code books — are already systems with inputs, states, lags and failure modes, and none of them would behave the same way in any other era. The game must get the *procedures* right, because accurate procedure is what makes the systems legible and the pressure real: a player who learns how a Meldezettel reaches the Polizeidirektion is learning the hunt. Grounding-kit audit: at the Russian gauge break passengers changed trains at the frontier (Wirballen/Eydtkuhnen, Alexandrowo) — bogie exchange of passenger stock was not period practice; Trans-Siberian through running began in 1903 via the Chinese Eastern Railway, with the Circum-Baikal loop closing the Baikal gap in 1904 (the 1916 Amur line gave the all-Russian route); and the kit should add that Britain had no hotel or resident registration before the Aliens Restriction Orders of 1914–16 and the National Registration Act 1915, the single most important sanctuary/trap asymmetry of the pre-war map. Otherwise I find the kit sound.

## P03.01 — The Paper Trail: Registers, Manifests and Bureaucratic Lag
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The hunter never sees the player, only the paper the player's body generates — hotel registration slips, passport desks, port passenger lists — each reaching the police with historically specific coverage, lag and error rate. Scotland Yard's reveal cadence becomes archival latency.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A Berlin or Vienna hotel sends a Meldezettel to the Polizeidirektion within about a day; a Russian hotel carries your passport to the police desk; pre-1914 London records nobody until the Aliens Restriction Orders of 1914–16. A night train avoids the register but puts you on the sleeping-car conductor's berth sheet; steerage at Dover appears on Aliens Act 1905 lists, first class does not. The player reasons about the hunter's probable knowledge ("my Munich slip arrives Thursday") and routes around it.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and travel primary; deception (which legend signs); items (registered passports); economy (private lodgings cost more, leave less paper); politics/warfare (1914 flips the map); time pressure (lags are deadlines).
- **Historical grounding:** German and Austrian Meldewesen; French innkeepers' police registers; Russian hotel passport desks; Wagons-Lits conductors presenting passports at frontiers; Aliens Act 1905 lists; Police Gazette circulars; no British registration before 1914.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Dread becomes a legible planning puzzle (competence motivation) with delayed consequences: the Zeigarnik tension of a slip "in transit" keeps the player thinking between sessions, and near-misses when a lag almost closes are the memorable moments.
- **Engine implications:** A record store: actions emit typed records (jurisdiction, timestamp, legend, lag distribution, reliability); the hunter queries only arrived records. Scales as jurisdiction × date tables.
- **Risks / open questions:** Hunter knowledge must feel fair without becoming a solved puzzle.

## P03.02 — Cable Copies: Telegraphy as an Archive That Fights Back
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every telegram exists three times — handed-in form, office copy, delivered copy — and the office copy is a dormant item the hunter can obtain under warrant or, in wartime, read as routine. Speed is bought with permanent evidence.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Cables are priced per word (transatlantic about a shilling), so brevity pays; commercial code books (ABC, Lieber's, Bentley's) compress meaning, but anyone holding the same edition reads you; private cipher is legal before 1914 but conspicuous, and from August 1914 belligerent censors admit only codes on an approved, deposited list. Registered cable addresses hide a name but tie a trail to one office. After the cable ship Alert cut Germany's Channel cables on 5 August 1914, German traffic to the Americas passes through neutral hands, each a copy point.
- **Directive coverage:** Coding/deception and hunt primary; travel (telegraph offices at stations); items (code books as owned, versioned objects); economy (word tariffs, deferred rates from 1912); politics/warfare (censorship, cable cutting, Room 40); time pressure (latency arbitrage).
- **Historical grounding:** Telegraph Act 1868 and Secretary of State warrants; 1903 London Telegraph Regulations on code words; cable companies' retained copies; All Red Line 1902; wartime cable censorship; the Zimmermann interception (1917).
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Loss aversion over text you can never recall; a crafting loop (shaving words, choosing codes) with delayed, legible consequences — the player learns that the cheapest word was the one that hanged them.
- **Engine implications:** Messages as first-class entities with routing paths, copy-holders and decay; censor and police AIs query them under jurisdiction-specific warrants; tariff tables by year.
- **Risks / open questions:** Composition must stay fast; tune how often old copies resurface.

## P03.03 — The Censor's Desk and the Black Cabinet
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Correspondence passes through adversarial reading offices whose detection heuristics are modelled and improve over time: the pre-war Russian "black cabinets" (perlustration by watch-list) and every belligerent's wartime postal and cable censorship. Writing a letter is stealth against an institution.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player composes letters from structured parts: plausible open text, an agreed open code (quantities and product names carrying meaning), secret-ink layers, and routing (direct, via a neutral cover address, or via the Thomas Cook scheme that forwarded enemy mail through neutral offices under censor sanction). The censor AI scores anomalies — word-frequency oddities, addresses on the Black List of suspect cover addresses, chemical tests (iodine vapour from 1915) — and passes, delays, excises or flags. Flags start inquiries weeks later. Censors learned historically (cigar-order codes were broken by 1915), so codes have a half-life and must rotate. Pre-1914 the loop runs only in Russia.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception/conversation/coding primary; hunt (flags seed inquiries); travel (letters ride the same graph and delays); items (inks, developers, address books); economy (forwarding fees; business cover yields plausible traffic); politics/warfare (censorship switches on with mobilisation); time pressure (censor delays miss departures).
- **Historical grounding:** Okhrana chernye kabinety; UK postal censorship (MO5(h), later MI9, several thousand staff by 1918) and Chief Cable Censor; German Auslandsbriefprüfungsstellen; French contrôle postal; Austrian Zensurstellen; 1915 invisible-ink cases.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Flow-state composition with stakes; a visibly learning adversary yields mastery curves and arms-race novelty; delayed flags create slow-burn dread.
- **Engine implications:** Anomaly scoring over structured letter components (not free text), per-office parameters evolving by date; inquiry events spawned from flags.
- **Risks / open questions:** Must not become a detached word minigame; structured composition mitigates.

## P03.04 — Instruments of Money: Letters of Credit, Gold and the Great Freeze
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Money is a set of period instruments — gold coin, circular letters of credit, bills of exchange, telegraphic transfers — each with weight, speed, cost, reach, identity binding and paper trail. August 1914 freezes the system; the war then melts it currency by currency.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A letter of credit draws only at listed correspondents and leaves a signature record at each; gold is anonymous but heavy and, after 1914, conspicuous; a telegraphic transfer arrives in hours but leaves a cable copy. Fixed parities (£1 ≈ 25.2 francs ≈ 20.4 marks ≈ 9.5 roubles ≈ $4.87) make pre-war accounting restful — then the August 1914 moratoria, closed bourses, Trading with the Enemy sequestrations, Soviet bank nationalisation (December 1917) and the mark's death in 1923 arrive on the world clock. Which passport opened each account decides whether it is seized: the player hedges identity, not merely currency.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy/business primary; deception (financial identities per legend); items (losable, stoppable instruments); travel (correspondent networks shape routes); politics/warfare (sequestration, inflation); hunt (drawings are records); time pressure (closing windows).
- **Historical grounding:** Gold-standard parities; specimen-signature practice; UK moratorium and Stock Exchange closure 31 July 1914–4 January 1915; Trading with the Enemy Acts 1914; German Devisenverordnung 1916; Soviet decrees 1917–18; hyperinflation to November 1923.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Endowment and loss aversion at scale; the visible approach of collapse creates anticipatory tension and system-generated "get it out in time" set-pieces; hoarding versus liquidation is endlessly replayable.
- **Engine implications:** Instrument objects with jurisdiction × date rule tables; historical exchange series as data; sequestration hooks keyed to legend nationality.
- **Risks / open questions:** Replay foreknowledge of 1914 and 1923 — perturb timing; keep exposure uncertain.

## P03.05 — The Document Regime Graph, 1895–1925
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every travel edge carries a dated document requirement — none, passport, visa, exit permit, photograph — that changes with the world clock, so one route is a stroll in 1908, a wall in 1915 and a queue in 1921.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Before 1914 the player crosses Western and Central Europe without papers, but Russia demands a consular visa, swaps the passport for a residence permit and requires an exit permit; the Ottoman Empire wants passports. Decrees of late July–August 1914 impose passports in Germany and France; Britain adds photograph passports in 1915, compulsory from November 1915; the Soviet state, the League's standardised booklet (1920) and the Nansen certificate (1922) redraw the map again. Each legend's papers carry issuer, dates, photograph, description. Forgeries fail inspection probabilistically; papers genuinely issued on false particulars (the lax pre-1915 British declaration system) pass but leave archival trails. Journeys become document chains planned around consulate hours.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and deception primary; items (papers as versioned artefacts); economy (fees, bribes, visa brokers); politics/warfare (war and revolution switch regimes); hunt (inspection points are reveal points); time pressure (expiries, imminent regime change).
- **Historical grounding:** Passport-free pre-1914 Europe; 1914 decrees; British Nationality and Status of Aliens Act 1914; 1920 Paris passport conference; Nansen passport 1922; US quota acts 1921/1924.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Planning satisfaction plus information-gap curiosity about the next frontier's demands; mid-journey regime shifts produce stranded-at-the-frontier drama; a well-built document set is a cherished possession (endowment effect).
- **Engine implications:** Edge requirement tables keyed by (origin, destination, legend nationality, date); inspection as a probabilistic check against papers and hunter alerts.
- **Risks / open questions:** Pre-1914 may feel easy; make Russia and Ottoman lands high-value, high-paper regions.

## P03.06 — Jurisdiction as Terrain: Extradition, Capitulations and Police Reach
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The hunter's power is bounded by extradition treaties, the political-offence exception, consular jurisdiction and dated inter-police agreements. The passport in your pocket decides which law applies, so a spy with several legends chooses jurisdiction by choosing identity.
- **How it plays (core loop):** In Constantinople before 1 October 1914 a British-papered player arrested by Ottoman police goes before the British consular court, a Russian-papered one to the Russian consulate, an Ottoman subject to the local court; Shanghai, Egypt and Persia follow the same capitulatory logic. Britain has no extradition treaty with Russia and surrenders no political offenders, making London a sanctuary until wartime alien registration. The Okhrana's Paris bureau works closely with the Sûreté, so France is porous to Russian pursuit; Switzerland shelters exiles but expels troublemakers. Secret protocols (Rome 1898, St Petersburg 1904) share anarchist files; the International Criminal Police Commission (Vienna, 1923) networks everyone. Sanctuary is a resource the clock erodes.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and politics primary; deception (legend nationality as legal shield); travel (routing through favourable jurisdictions); economy (consular registration, sureties, bribes); items (protection papers, consular certificates); warfare and time pressure (capitulations abolished 1914).
- **Historical grounding:** UK Extradition Act 1870; Ottoman capitulations and consular courts; Egyptian Mixed Courts (1876); Okhrana Zagranichnaya agentura; Swiss Bundesanwaltschaft political police (1889); ICPC 1923.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Terrain reading is the oldest strategic pleasure; standing legally untouchable while watched, then losing that status to a treaty or a war, yields tension/relief cycles generated by the system.
- **Engine implications:** A cooperation/extradition matrix (service × jurisdiction × date) consulted before any arrest or request; regime-event hooks.
- **Risks / open questions:** Legal nuance must compress to a few reach levels or become opaque.

## P03.07 — Stale Bradshaws: Timetables, Calendars and Clocks as Owned Knowledge
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The network is revealed only by the dated guides the player owns — Bradshaw's monthly Continental Guide, Cook's, the Reichskursbuch, the Russian official indicator — which go stale at seasonal changeovers, vanish in wartime and quote times in different clocks and calendars.
- **How it plays (core loop):** An outdated Bradshaw yields phantom connections and stranded nights; fresh knowledge is bought at stations or learned from porters. Russian railways run on St Petersburg time along the whole line and Russia keeps the Julian calendar until 1918; France keeps Paris time until 1911; Ottoman clocks count from sunset. The engine keeps one true clock, but every displayed time passes through the local regime — a forgery with the wrong calendar is a tell for the hunter and a skill check for the forger. Baltic ice, strikes (Russia 1905, France 1910, Britain 1911 and 1919) and the August 1914 suspension of civilian trains perturb the schedule.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel primary; items (guides as dated artefacts); deception/coding (calendar tells); economy (guide prices, fares, classes); politics/warfare (war timetables); hunt (pursuers read the same timetables); time pressure (fixed departures, edition expiry).
- **Historical grounding:** Bradshaw's Continental Railway Guide (monthly, suspended for the war); changeovers typically 1 May and 1 October; Petersburg railway time; Julian calendar to 31 January 1918; French adoption of GMT 1911.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Mastery over an information artefact — the pleasure of knowing the book; planning under uncertainty with crisp feedback; "one more leg" compulsion when a connection is discovered.
- **Engine implications:** Schedule graph versioned by edition; player knowledge as a subset view; a true-time to local-time rendering layer.
- **Risks / open questions:** Overlaps the travel designer's remit; owned-edition rules must stay convenient or become busywork.

## P03.08 — The Wire Is the Market: News Latency, Agencies and Editions
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** World-clock events do not reach cities instantly; they propagate through cable routes, the Reuters–Havas–Wolff territorial cartel, multiple daily editions and wartime press bureaux with measurable latency and distortion. Whoever is upstream acts in the gap.
- **How it plays (core loop):** An event fires at time T; the engine computes arrival per city from cable hops, agency territory (Havas for Latin Europe and South America, Reuters for the Empire and Far East, Wolff for Germany, Scandinavia and Russia), censor delay and next edition time. The player trades before a bourse reprices or crosses a frontier before a ban is gazetted. The hunt uses the same channels: a wanted notice spreads city by city, so the player outruns it along low-latency corridors. Planting items through press bureaux and bribed correspondents feeds false states into rivals' propagation.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy/business and politics/warfare primary; travel (latency corridors); deception/coding (planted news, coded commercial cables); items (ticker tape, dated editions); hunt (notice propagation); time pressure (gaps of minutes to days).
- **Historical grounding:** The agencies' "ring combination"; Exchange Telegraph tickers from 1872; multi-edition London evening papers; UK Press Bureau (August 1914); bourse closures of 1914; publication of the Zimmermann telegram (1 March 1917).
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The oldest dopamine — knowing before they do; anticipation and information-gap curiosity; markets give immediate numerical reward for latency mastery, and the same skill saves lives in the hunt.
- **Engine implications:** A propagation layer over the communications graph with per-city knowledge states; cheap to scale because every macro-event uses the same code.
- **Risks / open questions:** Replaying players know the macro-calendar — perturb timing and lean on contingent micro-events; markets must not swallow the life sim.

## P03.09 — Timetables of War: Mobilisation, Requisition and Train-Watching
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** War in this period was a railway timetable. Military movement runs on the same graph as civilian travel, so mobilisation seizes the player's trains, and intelligence is literally counting carriages.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Peacetime: general staffs hold mobilisation schedules as data objects (trains per day per bridge); fragments are the top prize. Crisis: as mobilisation is declared the civilian timetable collapses edge by edge (Germany and France suspended passenger traffic in early August 1914), frontiers close, requisition strips rolling stock, and a player on the wrong side improvises. War: the player builds or joins observation networks counting train composition — ratios of coaches, wagons and flat cars reveal infantry, supply or artillery — reporting through censored channels, as networks in occupied Belgium did in 1916–18. Warfare is observable as flows, never as battles.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics/warfare and travel primary; coding (reports via neutral post); items (schedules, counts, maps); economy (requisition kills trade, war contracts create it); hunt (counter-intelligence sweeps watching zones); time pressure (mobilisation is a countdown).
- **Historical grounding:** Great General Staff railway section and roughly 11,000 German mobilisation trains; French plan de transport; UK Railway Executive Committee (4 August 1914); Austro-Hungarian deployment plans sold to Russia in 1913; Belgian train-watching organisations' composition counts.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** An abstract war becomes visceral through the player's own logistics; the mobilisation countdown is a supreme tension/relief cycle; sampling flows is a slot machine of partial information (variable-ratio reinforcement).
- **Engine implications:** Military flows as scheduled edge loads; civilian capacity equals total minus military; observers sample loads noisily. Reuses the travel graph.
- **Risks / open questions:** Depth versus legibility; campaigns must abstract to flows and front states.

## P03.10 — Backstopped Lives: Legends as Institutional Record-Sets
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** A cover identity is the set of institutional records a real person would have — bank references, consular registration, club membership, a Meldeamt card. The player builds legends by generating those records through play; the hunter breaks them by auditing.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Every ordinary act under a legend — opening an account, registering at a hotel, joining a club, filing a Berlin residence registration — deepens its record-set. A suspicious hunter runs period inquiries: a commercial-agency report (Schimmelpfeng, Dun), a consular register check, a Medical Register lookup, an Einwohnermeldeamt request. Inconsistencies — a "doctor" absent from the Register, a photograph passport dated pre-1915 — accumulate doubt. Legends age and incur debts; a shallow legend is cheap and brittle, a deep one costly and traceable. Britain, unregistered before 1915, is where people are invented; Germany, with resident cards on everyone, is where inventions die.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception and hunt primary; travel (registration regimes decide where invention works); items (documents, monogrammed luggage, tailors' labels); economy (each legend has upkeep); politics/warfare (legend nationality decides internment and sequestration from 1914); time pressure (records arrive and age).
- **Historical grounding:** German Meldewesen and Einwohnermeldeämter; commercial inquiry agencies; professional directories; UK National Registration Act 1915; photograph passports 1915; police identification by laundry marks and tailors' labels.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The IKEA effect — players value legends they built; loss aversion when one burns; a construction loop against an intelligent auditor produces mastery and emergent betrayal by one's own carelessness.
- **Engine implications:** Record store shared with the Paper Trail; legend graph with consistency constraints; hunter inquiries as queries with cost and lag.
- **Risks / open questions:** Bookkeeping burden — needs a clear summary of legend depth and exposure.

## P03.11 — The Neutral Hub, 1914–1918
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** A complete game inside the war, played from the neutral cities where every service operated at once — Rotterdam, Copenhagen, Stockholm, Berne, Madrid, New York until April 1917 — under blockade economics, double censorship and neutrality police whose expulsion ends the game.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player freelances for several belligerents. Travel is a war graph: North Sea crossings under mine and submarine risk, the single Haparanda–Torneå land crossing to Russia (change of train and gauge, passport check, sledge or carriage between the towns until the 1919 bridge), the electrified Belgian–Dutch fence closing an edge from 1915. Blockade arbitrage: the Netherlands Overseas Trust polices imports, navicerts (1916) and rationing agreements squeeze neutral trade, smuggling margins explode. Letters between enemies pass only through neutrals, both censors reading. Guilder and Swiss franc hold value as belligerent currencies melt. The hunt is three-sided — enemy counter-intelligence, paymasters suspecting double-dealing, and Dutch or Swiss political police who simply expel. Russia's 1917 collapse and America's entry rewrite the hub map mid-game.
- **Directive coverage:** All seven are structural; politics/warfare, economy and hunt primary; the war itself is the time pressure.
- **Historical grounding:** Rotterdam as base for Allied networks into occupied Belgium; German networks in the Netherlands and Spain; the "Swedish roundabout" cable channel; Netherlands Overseas Trust (1914); Swiss Bundesanwaltschaft.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** A pressure-cooker with maximal double-dealing and a known four-year arc gives relentless tension/relief; scarcity makes every purchase meaningful; the three-sided hunt turns paranoia into a resource-management skill.
- **Engine implications:** Smaller map, denser rules — the ideal vertical slice of the full engine with war-era table values.
- **Risks / open questions:** Narrower than the 25-year life-sim brief; offer as a scenario or the engine's first slice.

## P03.12 — The Concession Agent: Commerce as the Engine of Espionage
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player is a commission agent brokering the period's great concessions and contracts — railways, oil, arms, wartime munitions — won by espionage: stolen tenders, bought officials, friendly legations. Business funds spying, spying wins business; the hunt comes from rivals' inquiry agents and double-crossed services.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A concession is a multi-year project object needing a registered company (a Belgian société anonyme for Russian ventures), a guaranteeing bank (Deutsche Bank, Banque Impériale Ottomane, HSBC), a sovereign grant (irade, firman, ukase) via intermediaries and bakshish, and legation backing the politics system grants or withdraws. The player rides the Bradshaw graph between capital markets, legations and concession territory, sells intelligence to services with a stake in the grant, and takes commissions on munitions contracts. The world clock renegotiates everything: 1908 reopens Ottoman grants, 1914 sequesters enemy concessions, 1917–18 expropriates all of Russia — where the fortunes sit.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy/business and politics primary; travel (constant journeys); deception/coding (commercial code cables as espionage cover); items (deeds, tenders, bank guarantees); hunt (private inquiry agencies, rival services); time pressure (tenders, option expiries, regime change).
- **Historical grounding:** Baghdad Railway concession (1903) with kilometric guarantees through the Ottoman Public Debt Administration; Persian oil concession 1901; Yalu timber concession; Turkish Petroleum Company 1912–14; Russian munitions contracts via New York agents 1915–16.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Long-horizon investment with variable-ratio payoff is the compulsive core of tycoon play; a life-and-death hunt layered on sunk costs yields "one more month" persistence and emergent tragedy.
- **Engine implications:** Project objects with dependency chains and political preconditions; faction utilities over concessions; a direct test of the directive-5 engine.
- **Risks / open questions:** Two interlocking careers risk complexity; espionage verbs must be mandatory.
