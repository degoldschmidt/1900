# Round 1A Proposals — Period Historian (1895–1925) (Panelist P03)

## Panelist statement
My lens is archival: the period's institutions — timetables, hotel registers, cable copies, letters of credit, visa regimes, censorship offices, commercial code books — are already systems with inputs, states, lags and failure modes, and none of them would behave the same way in any other era. The game must get the *procedures* right, because accurate procedure is what makes the systems legible and the pressure real: a player who learns how a Meldezettel reaches the Polizeidirektion is learning the hunt. Grounding-kit audit: at the Russian gauge break passengers changed trains at the frontier (Wirballen/Eydtkuhnen, Alexandrowo) — bogie exchange of passenger stock was not period practice; Trans-Siberian through running began in 1903 via the Chinese Eastern Railway, with the Circum-Baikal loop closing the Baikal gap in 1904 (the 1916 Amur line gave the all-Russian route); and the kit should add that Britain had no hotel or resident registration before the Aliens Restriction Orders of 1914–16 and the National Registration Act 1915, the single most important sanctuary/trap asymmetry of the pre-war map. Otherwise I find the kit sound.

## P03.01 — The Paper Trail: Registers, Manifests and Bureaucratic Lag
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The hunter never sees the player, only the paper the player's body generates — hotel registration slips, passport desks, port passenger lists — each reaching the police with historically specific coverage, lag and error rate. Scotland Yard's reveal cadence becomes archival latency.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Every night and frontier forces a documentary choice. A Berlin or Vienna hotel sends a Meldezettel to the Polizeidirektion within about a day; a Russian hotel carries your passport to the police desk; pre-1914 London records nobody, until the Aliens Restriction Orders of 1914–16 impose alien registration and hotel registers. A night train avoids the register but puts you on the sleeping-car conductor's berth sheet; steerage at Dover appears on Aliens Act 1905 lists, first class does not. The player reasons about the hunter's probable knowledge ("my Munich slip arrives Thursday") and routes around it, choosing jurisdictions, classes and lodgings as moves on a deduction board.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and travel primary; deception (which legend signs); items (passports as registered objects); economy (private lodgings and first class cost more, leave less paper); politics/warfare (1914 flips the map); time pressure (lags are deadlines).
- **Historical grounding:** German and Austrian Meldewesen; French innkeepers' police registers; Russian hotel passport desks; Wagons-Lits conductors presenting passengers' passports at frontiers; Aliens Act 1905 steerage lists; Police Gazette circulars; Britain's absence of registration before 1914.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Dread becomes a legible planning puzzle (competence motivation) with delayed consequences: the Zeigarnik tension of a slip "in transit" keeps the player thinking between sessions, and near-misses when a lag almost closes are the memorable moments.
- **Engine implications:** A record store: actions emit typed records (jurisdiction, timestamp, legend, lag distribution, reliability); hunter AI queries only arrived records. Scales as jurisdiction × date tables, no scripted content.
- **Risks / open questions:** Hunter knowledge must be exposed enough to feel fair without becoming a solved puzzle; lag distributions tuned for drama.

## P03.02 — Cable Copies: Telegraphy as an Archive That Fights Back
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every telegram exists three times — the handed-in form, the office's retained copy, the delivered copy — and the retained copy is a dormant item the hunter can obtain under warrant or, in wartime, read as routine. Speed is bought with permanent evidence.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player composes cables under real constraints: priced per word (transatlantic about a shilling), so brevity pays; commercial code books (ABC, Lieber's, Bentley's) compress meaning but anyone holding the same edition reads you; private cipher is legal before 1914 but conspicuous, and from August 1914 belligerent censors admit only codes on an approved, deposited list. Registered cable addresses hide a name but tie a trail to one office. The player weighs cost, latency, who holds copies and which jurisdictions the route touches: London–Buenos Aires lands in British relay stations; after the cable ship Alert cut Germany's Channel cables on 5 August 1914, German traffic to the Americas passes through Swedish or other neutral hands, each a copy point.
- **Directive coverage:** Coding/deception and hunt primary; travel (telegraph offices and poste restante at stations); items (code books as owned, stolen, versioned objects); economy (word tariffs, deferred rates from 1912); politics/warfare (censorship, cable cutting, Room 40); time pressure (latency arbitrage).
- **Historical grounding:** Telegraph Act 1868 and Secretary of State warrants; 1903 London Telegraph Regulations on code words; cable companies' retained copies; All Red Line 1902; wartime cable censorship; the Zimmermann interception (1917) as proof that copies kill.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Loss aversion over text you can never recall; a crafting loop (shaving words, choosing codes) with delayed, legible consequences — the player learns that the cheapest word was the one that hanged them.
- **Engine implications:** Messages as first-class entities with routing paths, copy-holders and decay; censor and police AIs query them under jurisdiction-specific warrants. Tariff and route tables by year.
- **Risks / open questions:** Code-book composition UI must stay fast; tune how often old copies resurface.

## P03.03 — The Censor's Desk and the Black Cabinet
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Correspondence passes through adversarial reading offices whose detection heuristics are modelled and improve over time: the pre-war Russian "black cabinets" (perlustration by watch-list) and the wartime postal and cable censorships of every belligerent. Writing a letter is stealth against an institution.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player composes letters from structured parts: plausible open text (trade orders, family news), an agreed open code (quantities, dates, product names carrying meaning), secret-ink layers, and routing (direct, via a neutral cover address, or via the Thomas Cook scheme that forwarded enemy mail through neutral offices under censor sanction). The censor AI scores anomalies — word-frequency oddities, stilted commercial phrasing, addresses on the Black List of suspect cover addresses, chemical tests (iodine vapour from 1915) — and passes, delays, excises or flags. Flags do not stop the letter; they start an inquiry weeks later. Censors learned historically — cigar-order codes were broken by 1915 — so the player's codes have a half-life and must rotate. Before 1914 the same loop runs only in Russia, against perlustration lists in Petersburg, Moscow, Warsaw, Odessa and Kiev.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception/conversation/coding primary; hunt (flags seed inquiries); travel (letters ride the same graph and delays); items (inks, developers, address books); economy (forwarding fees; a real business generates plausible traffic); politics/warfare (censorship switches on with mobilisation); time pressure (a letter held six days misses a departure).
- **Historical grounding:** Okhrana chernye kabinety; UK postal censorship (MO5(h), later MI9, several thousand staff by 1918) and Chief Cable Censor; German Auslandsbriefprüfungsstellen; French contrôle postal; Austrian Zensurstellen; "Passed by Censor" labels; 1915 invisible-ink cases.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Flow-state composition with stakes; a visibly learning adversary yields mastery curves and arms-race novelty; delayed flags create slow-burn dread that resolves as emergent drama.
- **Engine implications:** Anomaly scoring over structured letter components (not free text), per-office parameters evolving by date; inquiry events spawned from flags.
- **Risks / open questions:** Must not decay into a detached word minigame; structured composition is the mitigation.

## P03.04 — Instruments of Money: Letters of Credit, Gold and the Great Freeze
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Money is never a number but a set of period instruments — gold coin, notes, circular letters of credit, Cook's circular notes, bills of exchange, telegraphic transfers — each with weight, speed, cost, reach, identity binding and paper trail. August 1914 freezes the system; the war then melts it currency by currency.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player holds a portfolio across legends and jurisdictions. A letter of credit draws only at listed correspondents and leaves a signature record at each; gold is anonymous but heavy and, after 1914, hoarded, export-restricted and conspicuous; a telegraphic transfer arrives in hours but leaves a cable copy. Fixed parities (£1 ≈ 25.2 francs ≈ 20.4 marks ≈ 9.5 roubles ≈ $4.87) make pre-war accounting restful — then the moratoria of August 1914, closed bourses, exchange controls (Central Powers 1916), Trading with the Enemy legislation sequestering enemy nationals' assets, Soviet bank nationalisation (December 1917) and safe-deposit confiscations, and the mark's death in 1923 arrive as scheduled world-clock processes. Which passport opened each account decides whether it is seized: the player hedges identity, not merely currency.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy/business primary; deception (financial identities per legend); items (instruments as physical objects, losable, stealable, stoppable by cable); travel (correspondent networks shape routes); politics/warfare (sequestration, inflation); hunt (drawings are records); time pressure (dated instruments, closing windows).
- **Historical grounding:** Gold-standard parities; letter-of-indication and specimen-signature practice; UK moratorium and Stock Exchange closure 31 July 1914–4 January 1915; Trading with the Enemy Acts 1914 and the Public Trustee; German Devisenverordnung 1916; Soviet decrees 1917–18; hyperinflation to November 1923.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Endowment and loss aversion at scale; the visible approach of a known collapse creates anticipatory tension and system-generated "get it out in time" set-pieces; hoarding versus liquidation is endlessly replayable.
- **Engine implications:** Instrument objects with jurisdiction × date rule tables; historical exchange series as data; moratorium and sequestration hooks keyed to the holding legend's nationality.
- **Risks / open questions:** Replay foreknowledge of 1914 and 1923 — mitigate with perturbable timing and uncertainty about which legend is exposed.

## P03.05 — The Document Regime Graph, 1895–1925
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every travel edge carries a dated document requirement — none, passport, consular visa, exit permit, photograph, transit visa — that changes with the world clock, so one route is a stroll in 1908, a wall in 1915 and a queue in 1921.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Before 1914 the player crosses Western and Central Europe with no papers, but Russia demands a passport visaed by a Russian consul, exchanges it for a residence permit on entry and requires an exit permit to leave; the Ottoman Empire wants passports and internal travel permits. From the belligerent decrees of late July–August 1914 Germany and France impose passports; Britain introduces photograph passports in 1915 and compulsory passports for every crossing from November 1915; neutrals add transit visas; the Soviet state, the League's standardised booklet (1920) and the Nansen certificate for stateless Russians (1922) redraw the map again. Each legend owns a document set with issuing authority, dates, photograph and description. Forgeries fail inspection probabilistically; papers genuinely issued on false particulars (the lax pre-1915 British declaration system) pass but leave archival trails. Journeys are planned as document chains, visas obtained days ahead at consulates with opening hours.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and deception primary; items (papers as versioned artefacts); economy (fees, bribes, visa brokers); politics/warfare (war and revolution switch regimes); hunt (inspection points are reveal points); time pressure (consulate hours, expiries, imminent regime change).
- **Historical grounding:** Passport-free pre-1914 Europe; Russian and Ottoman regimes; 1914 decrees; British Nationality and Status of Aliens Act 1914 and the Permit Office; 1920 Paris passport conference; Nansen passport 1922; US Passport Control Act 1918 and 1921/1924 quota acts.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Planning satisfaction plus information-gap curiosity about what the next frontier demands; mid-journey regime shifts produce stranded-at-the-frontier drama without authored content; a well-built document set becomes a cherished possession.
- **Engine implications:** Edge requirement tables keyed by (origin, destination, legend nationality, date); inspection as a probabilistic check against document state and hunter alerts.
- **Risks / open questions:** Pre-1914 may feel too easy; make Russia and the Ottoman lands high-value, high-paper regions.

## P03.06 — Jurisdiction as Terrain: Extradition, Capitulations and Police Reach
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The hunter's power is bounded by extradition treaties, the political-offence exception, consular jurisdiction and dated inter-police agreements. The passport in your pocket decides which law applies, so a spy with several legends chooses jurisdiction by choosing identity.
- **How it plays (core loop):** In Constantinople before 1 October 1914 a British-papered player arrested by Ottoman police goes before the British consular court, a Russian-papered one to the Russian consulate, an Ottoman subject to the local court; Shanghai's settlements, Egypt's Mixed Courts and Persia follow the same capitulatory logic. Britain has no extradition treaty with Russia and surrenders no political offenders, making London a sanctuary until wartime alien registration. The Okhrana's Paris bureau works closely with the Sûreté, so France is porous to Russian pursuit; Switzerland shelters exiles but expels troublemakers. Secret protocols (Rome 1898, St Petersburg 1904) share anarchist files; the International Criminal Police Commission (Vienna, 1923) finally networks everyone. The player's map shows reach gradients per pursuing service; sanctuary is a resource the clock erodes.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and politics primary; deception (legend nationality as legal shield); travel (routing through favourable jurisdictions); economy (consular registration, sureties, bribes); items (protection papers, consular certificates); warfare and time pressure (capitulations abolished 1914, confirmed at Lausanne 1923).
- **Historical grounding:** UK Extradition Act 1870; Ottoman capitulations and consular courts; Egyptian Mixed Courts (1876); Okhrana Zagranichnaya agentura; Swiss Bundesanwaltschaft political police (1889); ICPC 1923.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Terrain reading is the oldest strategic pleasure; standing legally untouchable while watched, then losing that status to a treaty or a war, yields tension/relief cycles the system generates by itself.
- **Engine implications:** A cooperation/extradition matrix (service × jurisdiction × date) consulted before any arrest or request; regime-event hooks.
- **Risks / open questions:** Legal nuance must compress to three or four reach levels or it becomes opaque.

## P03.07 — Stale Bradshaws: Timetables, Calendars and Clocks as Owned Knowledge
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The network is never shown; it is known only through the dated guides the player physically owns — Bradshaw's monthly Continental Guide, Cook's, the Reichskursbuch, the Livret-Chaix, the Russian official railway indicator — which go stale at seasonal changeovers, vanish in wartime and quote times in different clocks and calendars.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Planning a leg means consulting your edition; an outdated Bradshaw yields phantom connections and a night stranded at a junction. Fresh knowledge is bought at stations and Cook's offices or learned from porters and fellow passengers. Russian railways run on St Petersburg time along the whole line and the empire keeps the Julian calendar until 1918; France keeps Paris time until 1911; the Ottoman Empire uses Rumi dates and sunset-based hours; daylight saving appears in 1916. The engine keeps one true clock, but every displayed time passes through the local regime — a forged document with the wrong calendar is a tell for the hunter and a skill check for the forger. Baltic and Archangel ice, strikes (Russia 1905, France 1910, Britain 1911 and 1919) and the August 1914 suspension of civilian trains perturb the schedule as the world moves.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel primary; items (guides as dated artefacts to buy, carry, lose); deception/coding (calendar tells); economy (guide prices, fares, classes); politics/warfare (war timetables); hunt (predictable connections are predictable to pursuers); time pressure (fixed departures, edition expiry).
- **Historical grounding:** Bradshaw's Continental Railway Guide (monthly, suspended for the war); Continental changeovers typically 1 May and 1 October; Petersburg railway time; Julian calendar to 31 January 1918; French adoption of GMT 1911; DST 1916; Circum-Baikal 1904.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Mastery over an information artefact — the pleasure of knowing the book; planning under uncertainty with crisp feedback; "one more leg" compulsion when a connection is discovered to exist.
- **Engine implications:** Schedule graph versioned by edition; player knowledge as a subset view; a true-time to local-time rendering layer.
- **Risks / open questions:** Overlaps the travel designer's remit; owned-edition rules must stay convenient or become busywork.

## P03.08 — The Wire Is the Market: News Latency, Agencies and Editions
- **Type:** game-defining system (flagged: risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** World-clock events do not reach cities instantly; they propagate through cable routes, the Reuters–Havas–Wolff territorial cartel, multiple daily editions and wartime press bureaux with measurable latency and distortion. Whoever is upstream acts in the gap — on markets, on movement, on the hunt.
- **How it plays (core loop):** An event fires at time T; the engine computes arrival per city from cable hops, agency territory (Havas for Latin Europe and South America, Reuters for the Empire and Far East, Wolff for Germany, Scandinavia and Russia), censor delay and next edition time. Placards and stop-press columns are the world's visible state. The player trades before a bourse reprices, crosses a frontier before a ban is gazetted, or sells advance news to a service. The hunt uses the same channels: a wanted notice spreads city by city, so the player outruns it along low-latency corridors. Planting items through press bureaux and bribed correspondents feeds false states into rivals' propagation.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy/business and politics/warfare primary; travel (latency corridors); deception/coding (planted news, coded commercial cables); items (ticker tape, dated editions as evidence); hunt (notice propagation); time pressure (the gap is minutes to days).
- **Historical grounding:** The agencies' "ring combination"; Exchange Telegraph tickers from 1872; multi-edition London evening papers; UK Press Bureau (August 1914); Wolff as official conduit; bourse closures of 1914; publication of the Zimmermann telegram (1 March 1917) as information warfare.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The oldest dopamine — knowing before they do; anticipation and information-gap curiosity; markets give immediate numerical reward for latency mastery, and the same skill saves your life in the hunt.
- **Engine implications:** A propagation layer over the communications graph with per-city knowledge states; cheap to scale because every macro-event uses the same code.
- **Risks / open questions:** Replaying players know the macro-calendar — perturb timing and lean on contingent micro-events; markets must not swallow the life sim.

## P03.09 — Timetables of War: Mobilisation, Requisition and Train-Watching
- **Type:** game-defining system (flagged: risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** War in this period was a railway timetable. Military movement runs on the same graph as civilian travel, so mobilisation seizes the player's trains, and intelligence is literally counting carriages: deployment schedules are the prize documents and train-watching the finest espionage.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Peacetime: general staffs hold mobilisation schedules as data objects (trains per day per bridge, concentration areas); fragments are the top espionage prize and the politics layer prices them. Crisis: as mobilisation is declared the civilian timetable collapses edge by edge (Germany and France suspended passenger traffic in the first weeks of August 1914), frontiers close, requisition strips rolling stock, and a player on the wrong side improvises. War: the player builds or joins observation networks counting train composition — ratios of coaches, wagons and flat cars reveal infantry, supply or artillery — reporting through censored channels, as networks in occupied Belgium did for Allied intelligence in 1916–18. Warfare is observable as flows, never as battles the player fights.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics/warfare and travel primary; coding (reports via neutral post); items (schedules, counts, maps); economy (requisition kills trade, war contracts create it); hunt (counter-intelligence sweeps watching zones); time pressure (mobilisation is a countdown of known shape).
- **Historical grounding:** Great General Staff railway section and roughly 11,000 German mobilisation trains; French plan de transport; UK Railway Executive Committee (4 August 1914); Austro-Hungarian deployment plans sold to Russia in 1913; Belgian train-watching organisations reporting composition counts.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** An abstract war becomes visceral through the player's own logistics; the mobilisation countdown is among the strongest tension/relief cycles available; sampling flows is a slot machine of partial information with real strategic payoff (variable-ratio reinforcement).
- **Engine implications:** Military flows as scheduled edge loads; civilian capacity equals total minus military; observers sample loads with noise. Reuses the travel graph.
- **Risks / open questions:** Depth versus legibility; campaigns must abstract to flows and front states.

## P03.10 — Backstopped Lives: Legends as Institutional Record-Sets
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** A cover identity is the set of institutional records that would exist if the person were real — bank references, consular registration, trade-directory entries, club membership, a Meldeamt card, a professional-register listing, laundry marks on shirts. The player builds legends by generating those records through play; the hunter breaks legends by auditing them.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Live under a legend, and every ordinary act — opening an account, registering at a hotel, joining a club, renting a Berlin flat and filing the compulsory registration — deepens its record-set. When suspicious, the hunter AI runs period inquiries: a commercial-agency report (Schimmelpfeng, Stubbs, Dun), a consular register check, a Medical Register or Law List lookup, a request to the Einwohnermeldeamt. Inconsistencies — a "doctor" absent from the Register, a photograph passport dated before 1915, a man of fifty claiming a 1900 matriculation — accumulate doubt. Legends age, incur debts and gather relationships; a shallow legend is cheap and brittle, a deep one costly and traceable. Britain, unregistered before 1915, is where people are invented; Germany, with resident cards on everyone, is where inventions die.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception and hunt primary; travel (registration regimes decide where invention is possible); items (documents, monogrammed luggage, tailors' labels); economy (every legend has an account and upkeep); politics/warfare (a legend's nationality decides sequestration and enemy-alien internment from 1914); time pressure (records arrive and age on the clock).
- **Historical grounding:** German Meldewesen and Einwohnermeldeämter; commercial inquiry agencies; trade and professional directories; UK National Registration Act 1915; photograph passports 1915; police identification by laundry marks and tailors' labels.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The IKEA effect — players value legends they built; endowment and loss aversion when one burns; a creative-construction loop against an intelligent auditor produces mastery and emergent betrayal by one's own carelessness.
- **Engine implications:** Record store shared with the Paper Trail; legend graph with consistency constraints; hunter inquiries as queries with cost and lag.
- **Risks / open questions:** Bookkeeping burden — needs a strong summary of legend depth and exposure.

## P03.11 — The Neutral Hub, 1914–1918
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** A complete game inside the war, played from the neutral cities where every service operated at once — Rotterdam, Copenhagen, Stockholm, Berne and Zurich, Madrid, New York until April 1917 — under blockade economics, double censorship and neutrality police whose expulsion order ends the game.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player freelances for several belligerents from a neutral base. Travel is a war graph: North Sea crossings to Rotterdam under mine and submarine risk, Bergen–Newcastle, the single Haparanda–Torneå land crossing to Russia (change of train and gauge, passport check, sledge or carriage between the towns until the 1919 bridge), the electrified Belgian–Dutch fence closing an edge from 1915. Business is blockade arbitrage: the Netherlands Overseas Trust polices imports, navicerts (1916) and rationing agreements squeeze neutral trade, smuggling margins explode. Communication is the censor loop twice over, letters between enemies passing only through neutrals with both sides reading. Money is the guilder and Swiss-franc premium against melting belligerent currencies. The hunt comes from three directions — enemy counter-intelligence, paymasters suspecting double-dealing, and Dutch or Swiss political police who simply expel. Russia's 1917 collapse and America's entry rewrite the hub map mid-game.
- **Directive coverage:** All seven are structural; politics/warfare, economy and hunt primary; the war itself is the time pressure.
- **Historical grounding:** Rotterdam as base for Allied networks into occupied Belgium; German networks in the Netherlands and Spain; the "Swedish roundabout" cable channel; Netherlands Overseas Trust (1914); British blockade instruments; Swiss Bundesanwaltschaft; Haparanda–Torneå as the only East–West land link.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** A pressure-cooker with maximal double-dealing and a known five-year arc gives relentless tension/relief; scarcity economics make every purchase meaningful; a three-sided hunt turns paranoia into a resource-management skill.
- **Engine implications:** Smaller map, denser rules — the ideal vertical slice of the full 1895–1925 engine with war-era table values.
- **Risks / open questions:** Narrower than the 25-year life-sim brief; offer as a scenario or the engine's first slice.

## P03.12 — The Concession Agent: Commerce as the Engine of Espionage
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player is a commission agent brokering the period's great concessions and contracts — railways, oil, timber, mining, arms, wartime munitions — and espionage is how they are won: stolen tenders, bought officials, friendly legations. Business funds spying, spying wins business, and the hunt comes from rivals' inquiry agents and the services you double-cross.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A concession is a multi-year project object needing a registered company (a Belgian société anonyme for Russian ventures, a London company under the Companies Acts), a guaranteeing bank (Deutsche Bank, Banque Impériale Ottomane, Russo-Chinese Bank, HSBC), a sovereign grant (irade, firman, ukase, imperial edict) obtained through intermediaries and bakshish, and legation backing the politics system grants or withdraws. The player rides the Bradshaw graph between capital markets, legations and concession territory, sells intelligence to services that want the grant to fall a certain way, and takes commissions on arms and munitions contracts. The world clock renegotiates everything: 1908 reopens Ottoman grants, 1914 sequesters enemy concessions, 1917–18 expropriates all of Russia — exactly where the fortunes sit.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy/business and politics primary; travel (constant purposeful journeys); deception/coding (commercial code cables are natural cover for espionage traffic); items (deeds, tenders, bank guarantees); hunt (private inquiry agencies, rival services); time pressure (tender dates, option expiries, regime change).
- **Historical grounding:** Baghdad Railway concession (1903) with kilometric guarantees through the Ottoman Public Debt Administration; Persian oil concession 1901 and APOC 1909; Chinese Eastern Railway; Yalu timber concession; Turkish Petroleum Company 1912–14; Russian munitions contracts placed through New York agents 1915–16.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Long-horizon investment with variable-ratio payoff is the compulsive core of tycoon play; a life-and-death hunt layered on sunk costs yields "one more month" persistence and emergent tragedy without authored plot.
- **Engine implications:** Project objects with dependency chains and political preconditions; faction utilities over concessions; a direct exercise of the directive-5 dependency engine.
- **Risks / open questions:** Two interlocking careers risk complexity; espionage verbs must be mandatory, not optional.
