# Round 1A Proposals — Core Loop & Systems Designer (Panelist P01)

## Panelist statement
I think in verbs, loops and feedback. A spy life-sim with six "incredibly deep" systems will die as six minigames unless one scarce thing is spent in all of them; here that thing must be *time on a schedule*, and the second thing must be *visibility*. Every proposal below is a way to make travel, deception, items, money, politics and the hunt draw on the same two accounts, so that every 30-second decision (which train, which hotel, which name) moves the 10-minute loop (the day's appointments), the session loop (the commission) and the campaign loop (how long until the file on you is thick enough). The game must get the cadence right first; depth is cheap once the cadence is compulsive.

## P01.01 — The Departure Is the Turn
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** No abstract turn. The world runs on a continuous clock and the player's decision points are *departures*: every action is placed in the gaps between timetabled trains, steamers and mail collections. The itinerary is the turn structure.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Every 30 seconds: pick what fits before the departure you have provisionally booked (meet, send, buy, observe, wait). Every 10 minutes: assemble a day as time-stamped slots (bank hours, postal deliveries, a 14:05 express) where *slack* is the real resource. Every session: one commission carried across two or three cities. Every campaign: thirty years in which the network speeds up (expresses, telephones, airlines) while borders harden (1914+): the same verbs, faster and riskier.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel is the skeleton; deception, items, business and politics are actions that *take hours* and compete for the same slots; time pressure is native; hunters read the same timetable and cordon stations at departure times. The spy's life is an itinerary.
- **Historical grounding:** Monthly Bradshaw's Continental Guide; through-trains; customs stops; the Russian gauge break; bank hours; several mail deliveries a day; wartime requisition deleting services.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The "one more leg" pull of 80 Days generalised to every verb; the near-miss effect (connection missed by eleven minutes); Zeigarnik tension from booked departures not yet earned by a finished task. Slack management is the mastery curve.
- **Engine implications:** A global priority queue of scheduled events (departures, deliveries, openings) plus player intentions; timetables as monthly data tables; interrupts (a telegram) re-open the slot planner. Simulate the player's city and legs in flight at minute granularity, elsewhere at day granularity.
- **Risks / open questions:** Spreadsheet feel; needs strong defaults ("next sensible departure") so planning depth is opt-in.

## P01.02 — Wants and Windows
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** No scripted missions. The world simulation emits *wants* (an institution will pay for a document, introduction, assessment or delivery by a date) and *windows* (that thing is obtainable at a place during an interval). The game is matching windows to wants across the graph before either closes.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Scan the board (newspapers, rumours, handlers, correspondence) → pick a want you can fund and reach → find or create a window → route yourself or a cut-out → extract → deliver → get paid in cash, credit or protection. One window can feed several wants (selling the same intelligence twice, at risk); a want without a window can be met by forgery.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and politics *generate* the wants (a naval scare raises blueprint prices; a concession auction creates a want for a ministry's intentions); travel is the matching constraint; items are the goods; deception fabricates windows; the hunt prices visible extraction; deadlines are the time pressure.
- **Historical grounding:** Commission agents for arms firms; concession hunting in Persia, Manchuria and Ottoman lands; services buying documents from freelancers; newspaper editions and Reuters/Havas/Wolff wires as the public signal of demand.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Variable-ratio reward (the next window is uncertain but legible), autonomy (you choose the want) and the trader's delight of arbitrage. Open wants are open loops; the board refreshes every edition.
- **Engine implications:** Wants and windows as typed records with open/close times, generated from world-state triggers through templates with parameterised value curves; a dependency link so fulfilment perturbs the state that generates further wants. Volume comes from templates, not authoring.
- **Risks / open questions:** Generated wants can feel samey; needs rich want *types* and consequences that visibly reshape the board.

## P01.03 — One Ledger: Cash, Credit, Heat
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Three accounts; every action moves at least two. **Cash** (gold-standard money, convertible everywhere until 1914). **Credit** (trust held with each institution — services, banks, firms, parties — non-transferable and slow to earn). **Heat** (what each police or intelligence service knows about each legend; decaying with time and distance, spiking with registered acts). The loop is converting one into another at favourable rates.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Sell intelligence: credit → cash, plus heat. Bribe a hotel clerk: cash → lower heat. Take a commission: heat → credit. Burn a legend: zero its heat, lose its accounts, addresses and letters of introduction. The player watches three balances per legend and city; the hunt is simply heat crossing a threshold in a jurisdiction you are inside.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and deception are the exchange mechanisms; travel changes which jurisdiction's heat applies; items (papers, codebooks) are stored credit; politics sets exchange rates (a service at war pays more for less); the hunt is heat turned hostile; the spy's life is the legend portfolio.
- **Historical grounding:** Fixed parities (£1 ≈ 25.2 francs ≈ 20.4 marks); letters of introduction and credit; hotel registration feeding police files; Okhrana Paris bureau and Special Branch dossiers; post-1914 passport controls making heat sticky.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Legible conversions build mastery; loss aversion over hard-earned credit; the endowment effect makes burning a well-built legend hurt. Three balances are few enough to reason about, too many to optimise.
- **Engine implications:** Credit as a sparse (legend × institution) matrix, heat as (legend × jurisdiction); every action declares deltas; decay tied to the world clock; thresholds emit hunt events.
- **Risks / open questions:** Heat must stay estimated, not exact, or the hunt loses its dread.

## P01.04 — The Visibility Price
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every service the world sells — a train class, a hotel, a bank, a telegraph office, a cab — sits on a triangle of *speed, cost and visibility*. You may have two. This is the thirty-second decision the game is made of.
- **How it plays (core loop):** First class with a sleeping-car berth is fast and rested, but the conductor sees your papers and the hotel at the end registers you with the police. Third class is anonymous and exhausting. A cable is instant and leaves a copy at the office; a letter is slow and private; a courier costs credit. The player chooses a visibility profile per leg and action; the hunt consumes the visible residue.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and communication (coding) are the surfaces; economy prices the choice; items (forged papers, a second passport) bend the triangle; politics bends it for whole regions (wartime: everything visible); time pressure makes speed tempting; the hunt reads only what you made visible.
- **Historical grounding:** 1st/2nd/3rd class fares; Wagons-Lits sleeping cars; hotel police registration (Germany, Austria, France, Russia); telegraph companies retaining cable copies; poste restante; grand hotels versus pensions; motor taxis replacing cabs c. 1905–10.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** A clean trilemma produces interesting choice at the smallest scale and makes consequences attributable — the player knows *which* comfort betrayed them. Micro risk-reward makes the macro hunt feel fair rather than arbitrary.
- **Engine implications:** Each service entry carries (duration, price, visibility vector by observer type); actions emit trace records tagged with observer, mode, place, time; the hunter AI consumes only trace records, never ground truth.
- **Risks / open questions:** Players may converge on one profile; counter with fatigue, baggage limits and police attention to the always-anonymous.

## P01.05 — Burn Rate
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Idleness costs money. Every legend, household, cover address, retainer and sub-agent has a daily burn; every debt accrues interest; every hotel night is a night on a register. Burn rate is the forcing function that turns a sandbox into a loop.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player maintains a portfolio of standing costs (a flat in Paris, a cable address in London, a bank account in Petersburg, a second household elsewhere — line items, not characters). Income is lumpy (commissions, deals), costs are continuous, so the player must periodically earn, which means moving, which means being seen. Gambling and speculation exist as high-variance ways to cover burn. Insolvency escalates: creditors, lawsuits, bailiffs — each a registered act that feeds heat.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy is primary; travel is forced by the need to work; deception is funded by cash; items (jewels, bearer bonds) are liquid reserves; politics changes burn (inflation, requisition, expropriation); the hunt feeds on money's paper trail; time pressure is literal.
- **Historical grounding:** Multiple households across cities as a documented agent lifestyle; letters of credit and circular notes; interest and bills of exchange; wartime inflation and exchange controls; German hyperinflation 1922–23, in which mark-denominated debts evaporate while mark-denominated income collapses.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Pressure without a scripted timer; loss aversion on the household you built; the gambler's bargain of one big deal. It stops turtling, the enemy of every immersive sim.
- **Engine implications:** Standing obligations as recurring scheduled events in a jurisdictional currency; insolvency as a dependency chain (default → suit → warrant). Trivial to scale; all arithmetic.
- **Risks / open questions:** Tuning: too steep and the game is a treadmill; too shallow and players never leave the safehouse.

## P01.06 — The Information Front (risky)
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** Knowledge about you travels at cable speed; you travel at train speed. Every revelation (photograph, name, description) propagates from its origin across the telegraph and postal network at the real latency of each link. The hunt is the race between your body and your own description.
- **How it plays (core loop):** After a visible act the player sees, as an estimated front on the network, where their description has probably reached. Choices: outrun it toward places not yet on the wire or off the cable's path; slow it (bribe a telegraph clerk, cross a frontier before the warrant is relayed, exploit wartime cable cuts); or change the thing described (new legend) so the front chases a ghost. Every border, hotel and bank is a checkpoint against what has arrived.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel versus communication latency is the whole game; deception resets the front; items (camera, wanted notice) are its payload; economy buys speed; politics decides which offices talk (allies share, enemies do not; 1914 rewires the graph); the hunt *is* the front.
- **Historical grounding:** The All Red Line (1902); telegraph versus rail speeds; shipboard wireless reaching liners mid-ocean (early 1900s); Okhrana–Sûreté cooperation; Bertillon cards circulated by post; wartime censorship and cut cables.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Dread made legible: the player can *see* what chases them and reason about it, converting anxiety into planning. Escape feels earned, capture fair, every crossing a tension-relief beat.
- **Engine implications:** Knowledge tokens propagating over a weighted graph with per-edge latency and per-node sharing policy; checkpoints query local knowledge only. Lazy propagation (evaluated on query) keeps it scalable.
- **Risks / open questions:** Visualising a probabilistic front without creating a minimap exploit; defining what the player legitimately knows of hunters' knowledge.

## P01.07 — The Double Book (risky)
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The spy's product is the *report*. The player serves several paymasters and writes each a version of events; the game keeps two books — what happened, and what each service believes — and survival is their consistency.
- **How it plays (core loop):** After every episode the player composes reports from tokens they hold (observed facts, documents, rumours) plus fabrications. Each service pays for novelty and corroboration with its other sources. Services cross-check against each other's leaks, newspapers and world events; a report contradicted later costs credit, and one proven *impossible* (you claimed Vienna on a day a hotel register placed you in Berlin) opens a counter-intelligence case. The player carries a consistency debt that grows with every lie and is paid down by making lies true (planting evidence, arranging the event).
- **Directive coverage:** Deception and coding are primary (reports travel ciphered; interception leaks them); economy is report income; politics sets what each service wants to hear; travel creates alibis and contradictions; items are evidence; the hunt begins inside your own employers; report deadlines are the time pressure.
- **Historical grounding:** Freelance agents paid per report by several services; sharing policy following alliances (1904, 1907); Room 40 reading cables; hotel registers as alibi checks; the press as corroboration.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The cognitive load of maintaining lies is the gameplay — the heist pleasure of "did they notice?". Dread of the cross-check, relief at the paid report, emergent stories of the lie that came true.
- **Engine implications:** A provenance-tagged fact store; per-service belief graphs; a consistency checker run on new evidence; deterministic, so contradictions can be explained afterwards.
- **Risks / open questions:** Composition must be token-based, never free text; belief modelling cost at scale.

## P01.08 — Perishable Prizes
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every item of intelligence or artifact has a value curve over time, a buyer set, and a *provenance* recording which legend obtained it, where and how. Selling realises cash and leaks provenance; holding loses value; carrying is a liability at every customs stop.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Obtain (steal, buy, photograph, copy) → decide: sell now locally for less, or carry to the best buyer across the graph before the curve decays (a mobilisation table is worthless after mobilisation; a codebook after the code changes; a concession map after the auction). Copy it (hours, a camera, exposure) to sell twice. Launder provenance through a cut-out at a discount. Physical artifacts (jewels, bonds, plans) need concealment compartments and are exactly what border searches find.
- **Directive coverage:** Items and economy are primary; travel is the delivery constraint; deception launders provenance; politics draws the curves (a crisis spikes demand, then collapses it); the hunt follows provenance back to a legend; time pressure is the decay itself.
- **Historical grounding:** Naval plans and blueprints as the age's prize; commercial and diplomatic codebooks changed periodically; Brownie (1900) and Vest Pocket Kodak (1912) copying; frontier customs inspection; the Zimmermann telegram's value lying in its timing.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Perishable inventory is the strongest urgency device in trading games (80 Days' luggage with a clock attached); provenance adds a sting of consequence to every sale, so the trade-off is felt, not read.
- **Engine implications:** Item records with value functions keyed to world-state variables, buyer lists derived from faction wants, provenance chains; decay evaluated lazily on inspection or sale.
- **Risks / open questions:** Too many curve types confuse; show value only as a buyer's quote, never as a raw number.

## P01.09 — Three Clocks
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The game runs three clocks at different frequencies — the *day clock* (hours, appointments, departures), the *world calendar* (crises, wars, laws, seasons) and the *reveal clock* (the cadence at which hunters compare notes and the player learns something of what is known) — and pacing is designed by how they beat against each other.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Minute to minute the player lives on the day clock. Every few days the reveal clock ticks: a notice appears in a paper, a hotel is visited, a cable is answered; the player gets a partial read of their own heat (Scotland Yard's reveal, inverted). Monthly or yearly the world calendar shifts rules: a new passport regime, a frontier closed by war, a currency collapse. The compulsive moments are *alignments*: a commission deadline, a frontier closure and the next reveal landing in the same week.
- **Directive coverage:** Time pressure and the hunt are primary; travel lives on the day clock; politics and warfare are the world calendar; economy and items follow both; deception resets the reveal clock for a legend.
- **Historical grounding:** The fixed macro-calendar 1895–1925 (Boer War, 1905, Balkan Wars, 1914, 1917, 1923); seasonal ice closures; monthly timetables; multi-edition dailies; periodic police file circulation.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Tension-relief cycles need a rhythm, and three interleaved rhythms produce the "one more turn" effect — something is always about to resolve. Alignments are emergent set-pieces with no author.
- **Engine implications:** A multi-cadence scheduler that lets designers *query* upcoming alignments to bias generated wants and windows toward them; the world calendar as data with perturbation hooks.
- **Risks / open questions:** The reveal clock must never become a visible countdown; it is inferred from events, not displayed.

## P01.10 — Politics as the Price Field
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Politics and warfare are not a separate screen. Each region carries a *tension vector* (between powers, regime and opposition, labour and capital) that is a multiplier field over every other system's prices: fares, border friction, visibility, document values, commission payouts, interest rates. The player reads the field and, rarely and expensively, nudges it.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Daily: read the papers to update your estimate of the field. Weekly: choose where to operate given gradients (high tension = high payouts, high visibility, closing routes). Rarely: spend credit and cash on influence actions (fund a newspaper, broker an arms contract, carry a message between factions) that move a tension value with lagged, leaky effect. War is the field saturating: routes vanish, passports appear, currencies float, services stop sharing.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics and warfare are primary but expressed entirely through travel, economy, deception and the hunt; time pressure comes from the field moving on its own schedule; the spy's business is reading it before the market does.
- **Historical grounding:** Moroccan and Bosnian crises as price shocks; Balkan Wars closing routes; 1914 passport regimes; alliance shifts (1904, 1907) changing which services cooperate; rouble collapse after 1917; concessions as political instruments.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Reading the field is a perception-mastery skill (forecasting from newspapers), and influence is rare enough to feel momentous; the strategist's pleasure without a wargame bolted on.
- **Engine implications:** A small vector per region updated by the world calendar and by faction/player actions; all price lookups are functions of it; a dependency graph for lagged propagation between neighbouring regions.
- **Risks / open questions:** Accuracy versus agency — the field must bend around the fixed macro-events, never override them.

## P01.11 — The Press Is the Scoreboard
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player receives almost no direct feedback. Consequences arrive through period channels — newspaper editions, telegrams, post, rumour in conversation — each with its own latency, reliability and cost. The world's reaction to you is something you *read*, days later, in a paper you had to buy.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Morning and evening editions summarise world-state deltas (a concession granted, a ministry fallen, an unnamed foreigner sought over a theft). Telegrams carry urgent, short, interceptable updates. Letters carry rich, slow, private ones. Rumour is cheap and wrong. The player triangulates. Then the loop inverts: the player *writes* into the channels — plants a story, sends a false cable to be intercepted, posts a letter to a watched address — and the scoreboard becomes a weapon.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception and coding are primary (channels are the attack surface); politics is what the papers report; economy pays for subscriptions, cables and journalists; travel sets which editions you can buy where; the hunt talks through the same channels (wanted notices, hotel queries by wire); editions are the clock.
- **Historical grounding:** Multi-edition dailies; Reuters/Havas/Wolff sharing agreements; pneumatic post; several deliveries a day; cable copies retained; press wanted notices; wartime censorship blacking out columns.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Delayed, mediated feedback produces anticipation (the walk to the kiosk), information-gap curiosity and the detective pleasure of reading your own actions in the third person. Planting stories is agency over the feedback loop itself.
- **Engine implications:** One event log rendered through channel-specific "lenses" (lag, reliability, censorship) into generated items; player-authored insertions enter the same log tagged as claims; hunters read lenses too.
- **Risks / open questions:** Generated prose quality; mitigate with structured headlines and tables rather than articles.

## P01.12 — Span of Control
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** You can be in one place; the board has many windows. The player builds a network of cut-outs, cover addresses and sub-agents across cities and acts remotely by post and cable, paying in latency, cost and interception risk. Presence is fast and certain but visible; delegation slow and private but corruptible.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each session the player decides where their body goes (the most valuable or fragile window) and what the network does elsewhere (instructions dispatched with hours-to-days latency). Sub-agents report back the same way: late, garbled or — if turned — false. The player grows the network with credit and cash, protects it with cut-outs and codes, prunes it when a node's heat rises. Travel collapses latency to zero for one node at a time.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel versus communication is the structural trade-off; deception and coding protect channels; economy pays retainers; items (codebooks, cover addresses) are infrastructure; politics changes reachability; the hunt attacks nodes and reads channels; deadlines decide whether delegation is affordable.
- **Historical grounding:** Cover addresses and cut-outs; commercial code books; poste restante; per-word telegraph rates making codes economic; Okhrana informants inside émigré networks; wartime censorship delaying post.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The agony of delegation — learning what your agent did three days ago — is a strong anticipation loop; span of control gives a strategy-layer pleasure (where should I be?) renewed every session.
- **Engine implications:** Agents as autonomous actors with goal scripts, loyalty state and message queues; delivery over the same latency graph as the Information Front; turning an agent is a hunter action.
- **Risks / open questions:** Remote action must stay less efficient than presence or travel withers; sub-agent behaviour must be legible.

# Round 1A Proposals — Simulation Engine Architect (Panelist P02)

## Panelist statement
I build simulation engines, and I read the five directives as one requirement: a continent-sized world that keeps moving whether or not the player is looking, cheap enough to run thirty years of it on an ordinary machine, and authorable by designers as data rather than code. The game must get one thing right: a single unified event scheduler through which trains, telegrams, markets, crises, debts and hunters all flow, so that every consequence is a real scheduled event, every deduction by the hunters is derived from real trace data, and nothing is faked behind a curtain. Each proposal below is player-facing gameplay that only exists because of that architecture.

## P02.01 — Elastic Time
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** No turns, no fixed tick. The world is a discrete-event simulation: time jumps from the player's present to the next event touching them while everything else advances through the same queue. A quiet winter passes in minutes; a closing cordon takes an hour.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The diary is the scheduler's visible face: commitments and expected arrivals in order (a departure, rent due, an awaited cable, tonight's hotel registration reaching the police). Choose an action; the engine advances to the next relevant event, resolving the world between. Waiting costs: expenses accrue, hunters move, offers expire. Granularity follows danger: minutes near a reveal, days at sea.
- **Directive coverage:** Time pressure primary. Travel: departures are queue events. Deception: cover stories must fit a checkable timeline. Economy: debts fall due. Politics: ultimatums, mobilisation timetables. Hunt: pursuers' moves share the queue, unseen but felt.
- **Historical grounding:** Bradshaw's monthly guides, several daily postal deliveries, multiple newspaper editions, hotel registration deadlines, the 1914 mobilisation timetables that made war a railway problem.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** "One more event" compulsion: each advance delivers a result and a decision. An opaque queue makes arrivals variable-ratio reinforcement. Tension/relief cycles emerge from event density, not scripting; the world's indifference makes agency precious.
- **Engine implications:** A global priority queue of timestamped events with dependency edges; entities subscribe to event types; level-of-detail scheduling runs distant entities coarsely, nearby ones finely, with deterministic catch-up from seed plus aggregate state on arrival. Total ordering (time, priority, insertion id) makes saves "seed + input log" and replay exact. Handlers are data-driven rules.
- **Risks / open questions:** Players may feel they skip their life; the UI must show what will pass; conversations need a fine-grained "now" within the frame.

## P02.02 — Faster Than the Wire
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The spy's life is a race between the player's body on the transport network and news of the player on the communications network. Facts travel at their medium's speed: a telegram reaches any cable office in minutes but carries only words; a photograph takes days by post; rumour walks.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Act (steal, broker, bribe). A notoriety map shows what each place knows about each identity. Route to beat the slow channels and land where the fast one does not yet carry your face: a night train outruns tomorrow's newspaper, not tonight's wire. Bribe a telegraph clerk to delay the wire; switch identity where only the old name arrived.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and hunt primary. Deception: identities are the facts' subjects. Coding: ciphered police cables act slower. Economy: bribes, cables, speed. Politics: wartime censorship and 1914 passport controls add checkpoints.
- **Historical grounding:** Telegraph against post latency; wanted notices in daily editions; photographs by post; Scotland Yard fingerprint cards from 1901; the 1910 arrest of a transatlantic fugitive by ship's wireless.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** A legible risk model rewards planning; near misses ("the description arrived an hour after I left") are the strongest hook. Loss aversion over identities; mastery of two overlaid networks; the rush of being just ahead.
- **Engine implications:** Two graphs: time-expanded transport, and an information network of channels with latency distributions and capacities. Facts are entities (subject, content type, confidence, timestamp); propagation schedules arrival events at institution nodes; NPCs query their institution rather than holding knowledge; hunters search only facts that reached theirs. Hop-count bounded.
- **Risks / open questions:** Must not collapse into pure flight; latencies need tuning for a winnable but tense race; teaching the network without a lecture.

## P02.03 — The Default Timeline
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The thirty years run as history did unless the player pushes on a precondition. Macro-events are nodes of a dependency graph with default dates, preconditions and elasticity bounds; each playthrough jitters them by days to weeks; player and faction actions push them further within bounds. The player's life is a diff against history, revealed at the end.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Newspapers leak the default timeline. Commissions touch preconditions: an arms delivery before a mobilisation, a stolen memorandum that shifts a ministry's belief. Ride history (profit from approximate foreknowledge) or lean on it; every push draws counter-intelligence attention. At death, capture or retirement, an inquest shows which events moved, by how much, traced through the graph to the player's acts.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics/warfare and time pressure primary. Travel: wars close routes and rewrite the graph. Economy: foreknowledge trades. Deception: influence operations. Hunt: services escalate against whoever perturbs their plans.
- **Historical grounding:** The macro-event clock; the July 1914 chain of ultimatums; mobilisation timetables; the 1907 Anglo-Russian Convention; railway concessions as diplomatic causes.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Agency against a known script: the fantasy of "could I have changed it" and dramatic irony as a resource. The inquest gives systems-generated closure. Jitter keeps foreknowledge approximate, so replay stays uncertain.
- **Engine implications:** Macro-events as a DAG (preconditions as world-state predicates, default date, elasticity, consequences) as data tables; a constraint propagator reschedules dependents when a node moves; every state change carries provenance (causing event id), so the inquest is computed, not written. Deterministic given seed; jitter drawn from it.
- **Risks / open questions:** The Historian may object to any movement; butterfly effects must be bounded by elasticity; players may feel railroaded if bounds are tight. Risky but potentially brilliant.

## P02.04 — Several Lives at Once
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player maintains several identities, each an entity with residence, bank accounts, acquaintances, police registration, mail, debts and obligations in a different city; all keep running whether or not the player wears them. Switching lives means travelling there and facing what piled up.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Spin plates. An unattended legend goes stale: unpaid rent brings eviction and a police inquiry; unanswered mail drops a contact; a lapsed registration draws a fine and attention. Each identity has its own notoriety and assets. Plan a circuit by timetable; delegate upkeep to a housekeeper or clerk who reports by cable, at cost and leak risk. The hunters win by linking two identities.
- **Directive coverage:** Life-sim primary. Travel: the circuit. Deception: legends as maintained entities. Items: papers and letters of credit per name. Economy: accounts in several currencies. Politics: a German-national identity in London is interned in 1914. Hunt: hunters track identities, not the player.
- **Historical grounding:** Households in several capitals (the touchstone career); hotel registration; letters of credit issued to a name; enemy-alien internment from 1914; nationality-bound visa regimes thereafter.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Plate-spinning anxiety and relief; sunk-cost attachment to each life; "I must be in Petersburg before rent day" appointment compulsion; combinatorial strategy; the dread of a link being made.
- **Engine implications:** Identity as an entity with components (residence, accounts, registrations, relationships, obligations). Unattended identities run at low detail: obligation events fire with aggregated outcomes; on arrival the engine materialises specifics deterministically (what mail came, who called). Hunters deduce over identity entities; shared evidence (handwriting, photograph, luggage) triggers a link. Obligation templates are data.
- **Risks / open questions:** Cognitive load; cap at three to five identities; maintenance must generate opportunities, not chores.

## P02.05 — The Dossier Is Real
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Each hunting service keeps a file on each player identity, and that file is literally a filtered view of the game's own event log: only events whose evidence reached that service. Steal it and you read exactly what the hunters know, gaps included.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Actions emit evidence records with a channel (witness, register, ticket, cable copy, fingerprint) that reach services through the information network; a service's deduction runs only over its dossier. Obtain it by burglary, a bribed clerk or a double agent, then act: remove a page (deduction regresses), insert a false sighting, or protect the gap you now know exists.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt primary. Deception: forging your own file. Items: the dossier is an artifact with provenance. Travel: gaps are where you move freely. Economy: files are bought and sold. Politics: 1907 and 1914 liaison channels merge files; 1917 spills a service's archive.
- **Historical grounding:** Okhrana card indexes and Paris bureau files; Special Branch registries; fingerprint cards; wanted notices; the 1909 Secret Service Bureau's registry; wartime Allied liaison.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Information-gap curiosity (what do they know?) resolved into relief and dread; agency over your own history; legible deduction builds trust that the hunt is fair, making being hunted exhilarating rather than arbitrary.
- **Engine implications:** Append-only event log with data-driven evidence emission rules per event. A dossier is an indexed filter over the log, essentially free. Deduction is a rule engine over the dossier producing weighted hypotheses. Dossier edits are themselves events, so tampering is replayable and discoverable. One log drives save, replay and the hunt. Old evidence compacts.
- **Risks / open questions:** Readability of a raw file; exposing too much state; deduction must feel human, not perfect.

## P02.06 — The Operations Board
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player plans an operation as a dependency graph of tasks with time windows and conditional branches, then hands it to the same scheduler that runs the world and watches it execute, intervening when it breaks.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Lay tasks on a board: obtain an Ottoman visa by Thursday; take Friday's through-train from Vienna to Constantinople; meet the contact within two days; copy the plans (camera, one night); leave by Tuesday's Smyrna steamer; else fall back to Salonika. Tasks declare prerequisites (items, money, identity), duration and risk; sub-agents can take tasks. The engine validates against the timetable and shows slack. In execution the world intrudes (strike, cordon, late train); the board shows the cascade and the player repairs it.
- **Directive coverage:** All: travel (timetabled legs), deception (cover per task), coding (scheduled cable check-ins), items (prerequisites), economy (cost and cash flow per task), politics (operations are commissions), time pressure (slack is the key resource), hunt (each task emits evidence).
- **Historical grounding:** Period operations were logistics: visas, letters of introduction, cover firms, cable check-ins, through-train connections, consular registration.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Planning pleasure, anticipation while the plan unfolds, and flow when repairing under pressure. The board makes deep systems legible, so mastery grows from single legs to multi-agent operations.
- **Engine implications:** Plans are declarative DAGs with time windows; critical path and slack are computed against the time-expanded transport graph; the plan compiles into scheduled events with dependency edges; failures trigger re-planning of affected subtrees. NPC factions and hunters use the same plan format, so one planner serves all agents. Task templates are authored as data.
- **Risks / open questions:** UI complexity; drifting into project management; keeping moment-to-moment agency while the plan runs.

## P02.07 — Every Object Has a History
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every item is an entity with a provenance chain, and its safety is a live property computed from what the world has since learned. A forged passport is good until its forger is arrested; days later every document from his hand is a known forgery at every frontier post the news has reached.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The inventory shows provenance and estimated heat. Is this revolver traceable through a dealer's ledger? Are these blueprints from a batch now reported missing? Launder items (re-register, re-stamp), sell them on (heat transfers to the buyer), or hide them in luggage compartments. Stolen plans are valuable only while the theft is unknown and the buyer lacks them.
- **Directive coverage:** Items primary. Deception: forged papers. Travel: customs inspections check heat. Economy: provenance sets price; stolen-goods markets. Politics: a document's value depends on which service lacks it. Hunt: recovered items feed the dossier. Time pressure: heat moves.
- **Historical grounding:** Serial-numbered firearms and dealer ledgers; consular passport registers; banks circulating notices of stolen bonds; Lloyd's registers; customs stops at every frontier.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Loss aversion and the endowment effect: a trusted passport turning hot is felt as a wound. Risk assessment becomes a skill; a ticking inventory creates urgency; laundering plays are satisfying; items accumulate stories.
- **Engine implications:** Items are entities with a provenance log linking to other entities and events. Heat is lazily evaluated on inspection by walking provenance links against the fact store of the inspecting institution; no per-tick updates. Burn propagates as fact events through the information network. Item types declare which provenance facts they emit.
- **Risks / open questions:** Players ignoring provenance until punished; chain depth must be capped and summarised.

## P02.08 — Hunters Ride the Same Trains
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Pursuers are agents on the same timetable as the player. They cannot teleport: they wire ahead and board the next departure. Cordons form only where their agents can physically be in time: the hunt is a race across a Bradshaw's graph.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each movement leaves a mode trace (ticket, hotel register, frontier stamp). Hunters receive traces with latency and compute the player's possible positions. They post watchers at chokepoints they can reach first (frontier stations, ports, the Russian gauge-break) and wire local police. Reveals (a registration, a sighting) collapse their set. The player exploits night trains that outrun the morning dispatch, chokepoints hunters cannot reach in time, and mode changes that split their set.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and travel primary. Deception: false registrations poison the trace. Items: tickets and stamps are traces. Economy: first class leaves sharper traces. Politics: war adds checkpoints and changes who hunts. Time pressure: constant.
- **Historical grounding:** Continental timetables; frontier stations and gauge breaks; cabling police ahead; passport-free Western Europe before 1914 against Russian and Ottoman controls.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Scotland Yard's deduction tension made geographic and legible: "can they reach the frontier before me?" is computable by the player too, so outwitting feels earned. Near misses on platforms.
- **Engine implications:** One time-expanded transport graph per timetable period serves player routing, hunter routing and reachability. Hunter belief is a set of possible positions propagated as bitsets per time slice; cordon planning is a cut over that set within the hunters' own reach; reachability is precomputed monthly. Dozens of hunters cost microseconds. Trace emission is data.
- **Risks / open questions:** Exact reachability makes hunters too strong, so belief needs noise; overlaps with travel and hunt designs; merge.

## P02.09 — Trading Ahead of the Wire
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Prices on each bourse change only when news arrives there, and news travels at cable speed. Espionage puts the player hours or days ahead of the markets; profit is informational arbitrage, and it leaves a pattern hunters can read.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Hold bonds, arms-firm shares, commodity contracts and currencies across London, Paris, Berlin, Petersburg and New York. Events emit news; each bourse reprices on arrival. Knowing a contract award, a mobilisation order or a drilling result before the wire opens a trading window. Every trade is a timestamped record; a service that correlates "who traded before the news" acquires evidence; sudden fortunes must be laundered through front businesses.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy primary. Coding: ciphered commercial cables to brokers. Travel: being at a bourse, or instructing a broker by cable. Politics: crises are the news. Deception: fronts and nominees. Hunt: trading patterns are evidence. Time pressure: windows of hours.
- **Historical grounding:** Gold-standard parities; cable-linked bourses; commercial code books; arms firms' shares; Baku and Persian oil; the exchange closures of July 1914; the rouble's collapse; the 1923 mark.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The thrill of an edge; variable rewards; greed against exposure; mastery of reading news flow; the fantasy of funding espionage with espionage.
- **Engine implications:** Prices are entities repriced by news-arrival events from the same information network as P02.02; each bourse holds a set of known facts, so price is a data-driven function of known facts; no per-tick market simulation. Trade records are evidence; a pattern rule runs lazily when a service investigates. Scales to dozens of bourses and hundreds of instruments.
- **Risks / open questions:** Exploitability and balance; profits must tie to life costs and commissions, or it becomes a stock minigame.

## P02.10 — Serving Several Masters
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every intelligence service holds its own belief model of the player. The player can be paid by several at once; exposure comes not from script but from liaison events, when two services exchange information and their beliefs are compared.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Take commissions from several services; feed each a version of the truth through reports, forged documents and observed behaviour. A beliefs view estimates each service's model, imperfectly. Watch the political calendar: the 1907 convention opens a channel between two employers; 1914 fuses Entente counter-intelligence; 1917 shatters the Russian service and leaks its files. When a liaison fires, contradictions become suspicion events: interrogations, cut-offs, or a hunt.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics and deception primary. Hunt: suspicion escalates into pursuit. Economy: several salaries. Travel: handlers in different capitals. Coding: a separate cipher per service, and a captured cipher links identities. Items: reports as artifacts. Time pressure: liaisons are dated.
- **Historical grounding:** Freelance agents serving several services; Entente intelligence liaison, especially 1914–18; Okhrana files seized, partly burned and partly published in 1917.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Greed against dread with a visible political calendar of reckonings; the craft of consistent lying; betrayal emerges from systems rather than authorship, so every exposure is the player's own.
- **Engine implications:** A belief store per faction: facts as (subject, predicate, value, confidence, source, time). Liaison events, scheduled by the macro timeline or emergent, run a reconciliation rule over shared subjects; contradiction yields a suspicion event scaled by the product of confidences. Comparison happens only at liaison, bounding cost. Beliefs cover only entities of interest. Update rules are data.
- **Risks / open questions:** An honest beliefs view without full disclosure; liaison frequency; complexity for new players.

## P02.11 — The Archive Remembers
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Nothing sent or signed is ever gone. Telegraph companies keep cable copies; hotels, banks and censors keep registers, ledgers and files. These archives persist; later events (a war, a new codebreaking office, a revolution seizing files) schedule reviews that can discover the player's acts years later. The past catches up by mechanism.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Early careers are careless (commercial-code cables, real names in registers) and the archive accumulates. Later, world events trigger reviews: 1914 censorship offices read back cable copies; a service breaks an old cipher class and decrypts stored traffic; 1917 revolutions publish police files. Pre-empt: destroy copies (burglary, bribery, fire), buy the archive, or choose media that leave none (courier against cable), trading speed for permanence.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and time pressure primary, on a fuse of years. Coding: cipher strength sets how long the archive stays dark. Items: archived documents. Economy: ledgers. Politics: regime change opens archives. Travel: going back to burn a register.
- **Historical grounding:** Retained cable copies; wartime cable censorship at relay stations; the Okhrana archives seized and partly published in 1917; the Zimmermann telegram.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Dread of the long fuse rewards foresight; the touchstone arc of the past catching up emerges from systems; clean-up operations arise from one's own history; the catharsis of burning the last copy.
- **Engine implications:** Archive nodes store event ids, not copies: negligible cost. Macro-events schedule reviews with rule-based selection (which archives, period, cipher classes). Decryption is a probability over cipher class and elapsed time. Discoveries enter the information network as evidence. Deterministic, indexed by subject. Archive policies are data.
- **Risks / open questions:** Players may feel punished for early play, so foreshadowing and mitigation matter. Risky but potentially brilliant.

## P02.12 — The World Posts Its Own Jobs
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** No authored missions. Factions (services, arms firms, ministries, committees) derive goals from the world state and plan to achieve them; when a plan needs a human courier, thief, broker or forger, the engine emits a commission, and the player is one candidate among NPC agents.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each identity sees a market of commissions filtered by reputation, location and what factions believe about it. Accept, execute (the Operations Board), get paid; reputation and beliefs shift, changing future offers. Factions compete for the same object (a concession, a set of plans), so taking one job closes others and creates enemies. Being slow means an NPC agent takes the job and the world moves on without you.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics (faction goals) and economy (commissions, concessions) primary. Travel: jobs are located. Deception: jobs need cover. Items: jobs are about objects. Time pressure: offers expire; NPC rivals compete. Hunt: counter-intelligence factions post jobs whose target is the player.
- **Historical grounding:** Arms-commission agents; concession brokering in Persia, Manchuria and Ottoman lands; services hiring freelancers; the Boer War arms trade; Persian oil from 1908.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Coherent infinite content; the sense of a living economy of intrigue; rivalry with NPC agents; agency in choosing clients; long arcs because factions remember.
- **Engine implications:** Goal-oriented planners per faction over a shared world model decompose goals into tasks; tasks needing an agent become commissions in a bid market; NPC agents are coarse entities executing tasks as scheduled events with probabilistic outcomes. Factions re-plan only when subscribed state changes, keeping planning rare. Goals and task templates are data.
- **Risks / open questions:** Generated jobs feeling samey; needs a wide template library; emergent degenerate loops.

# Round 1A Proposals — Period Historian (1895–1925) (Panelist P03)

## Panelist statement
My lens is archival: the period's institutions — timetables, hotel registers, cable copies, letters of credit, visa regimes, censorship offices, commercial code books — are already systems with inputs, states, lags and failure modes, and none of them would behave the same way in any other era. The game must get the *procedures* right, because accurate procedure is what makes the systems legible and the pressure real: a player who learns how a Meldezettel reaches the Polizeidirektion is learning the hunt. Grounding-kit audit: at the Russian gauge break passengers changed trains at the frontier (Wirballen/Eydtkuhnen, Alexandrowo) — bogie exchange of passenger stock was not period practice; Trans-Siberian through running began in 1903 via the Chinese Eastern Railway, with the Circum-Baikal loop closing the Baikal gap in 1904 (the 1916 Amur line gave the all-Russian route); and the kit should add that Britain had no hotel or resident registration before the Aliens Restriction Orders of 1914–16 and the National Registration Act 1915, the single most important sanctuary/trap asymmetry of the pre-war map. Otherwise I find the kit sound.

## P03.01 — The Paper Trail: Registers, Manifests and Bureaucratic Lag
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The hunter never sees the player, only the paper the player's body generates — hotel registration slips, passport desks, port passenger lists — each reaching the police with historically specific coverage, lag and error rate. Scotland Yard's reveal cadence becomes archival latency.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A Berlin or Vienna hotel sends a Meldezettel to the Polizeidirektion within about a day; a Russian hotel carries your passport to the police desk; pre-1914 London records nobody until the Aliens Restriction Orders of 1914–16. A night train avoids the register but puts you on the sleeping-car conductor's berth sheet; steerage at Dover appears on Aliens Act 1905 lists, first class does not. The player reasons about the hunter's probable knowledge ("my Munich slip arrives Thursday") and routes around it.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and travel primary; deception (which legend signs); items (registered passports); economy (private lodgings cost more, leave less paper); politics/warfare (1914 flips the map); time pressure (lags are deadlines).
- **Historical grounding:** German and Austrian Meldewesen; French innkeepers' police registers; Russian hotel passport desks; Wagons-Lits conductors presenting passports at frontiers; Aliens Act 1905 lists; Police Gazette circulars; no British registration before 1914.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Dread becomes a legible planning puzzle (competence motivation) with delayed consequences: the Zeigarnik tension of a slip "in transit" keeps the player thinking between sessions, and near-misses when a lag almost closes are the memorable moments.
- **Engine implications:** A record store: actions emit typed records (jurisdiction, timestamp, legend, lag distribution, reliability); the hunter queries only arrived records. Scales as jurisdiction × date tables.
- **Risks / open questions:** Hunter knowledge must feel fair without becoming a solved puzzle.

## P03.02 — Cable Copies: Telegraphy as an Archive That Fights Back
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every telegram exists three times — handed-in form, office copy, delivered copy — and the office copy is a dormant item the hunter can obtain under warrant or, in wartime, read as routine. Speed is bought with permanent evidence.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Cables are priced per word (transatlantic about a shilling), so brevity pays; commercial code books (ABC, Lieber's, Bentley's) compress meaning, but anyone holding the same edition reads you; private cipher is legal before 1914 but conspicuous, and from August 1914 belligerent censors admit only codes on an approved, deposited list. Registered cable addresses hide a name but tie a trail to one office. After the cable ship Alert cut Germany's Channel cables on 5 August 1914, German traffic to the Americas passes through neutral hands, each a copy point.
- **Directive coverage:** Coding/deception and hunt primary; travel (telegraph offices at stations); items (code books as owned, versioned objects); economy (word tariffs, deferred rates from 1912); politics/warfare (censorship, cable cutting, Room 40); time pressure (latency arbitrage).
- **Historical grounding:** Telegraph Act 1868 and Secretary of State warrants; 1903 London Telegraph Regulations on code words; cable companies' retained copies; All Red Line 1902; wartime cable censorship; the Zimmermann interception (1917).
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Loss aversion over text you can never recall; a crafting loop (shaving words, choosing codes) with delayed, legible consequences — the player learns that the cheapest word was the one that hanged them.
- **Engine implications:** Messages as first-class entities with routing paths, copy-holders and decay; censor and police AIs query them under jurisdiction-specific warrants; tariff tables by year.
- **Risks / open questions:** Composition must stay fast; tune how often old copies resurface.

## P03.03 — The Censor's Desk and the Black Cabinet
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Correspondence passes through adversarial reading offices whose detection heuristics are modelled and improve over time: the pre-war Russian "black cabinets" (perlustration by watch-list) and every belligerent's wartime postal and cable censorship. Writing a letter is stealth against an institution.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player composes letters from structured parts: plausible open text, an agreed open code (quantities and product names carrying meaning), secret-ink layers, and routing (direct, via a neutral cover address, or via the Thomas Cook scheme that forwarded enemy mail through neutral offices under censor sanction). The censor AI scores anomalies — word-frequency oddities, addresses on the Black List of suspect cover addresses, chemical tests (iodine vapour from 1915) — and passes, delays, excises or flags. Flags start inquiries weeks later. Censors learned historically (cigar-order codes were broken by 1915), so codes have a half-life and must rotate. Pre-1914 the loop runs only in Russia.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception/conversation/coding primary; hunt (flags seed inquiries); travel (letters ride the same graph and delays); items (inks, developers, address books); economy (forwarding fees; business cover yields plausible traffic); politics/warfare (censorship switches on with mobilisation); time pressure (censor delays miss departures).
- **Historical grounding:** Okhrana chernye kabinety; UK postal censorship (MO5(h), later MI9, several thousand staff by 1918) and Chief Cable Censor; German Auslandsbriefprüfungsstellen; French contrôle postal; Austrian Zensurstellen; 1915 invisible-ink cases.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Flow-state composition with stakes; a visibly learning adversary yields mastery curves and arms-race novelty; delayed flags create slow-burn dread.
- **Engine implications:** Anomaly scoring over structured letter components (not free text), per-office parameters evolving by date; inquiry events spawned from flags.
- **Risks / open questions:** Must not become a detached word minigame; structured composition mitigates.

## P03.04 — Instruments of Money: Letters of Credit, Gold and the Great Freeze
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Money is a set of period instruments — gold coin, circular letters of credit, bills of exchange, telegraphic transfers — each with weight, speed, cost, reach, identity binding and paper trail. August 1914 freezes the system; the war then melts it currency by currency.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A letter of credit draws only at listed correspondents and leaves a signature record at each; gold is anonymous but heavy and, after 1914, conspicuous; a telegraphic transfer arrives in hours but leaves a cable copy. Fixed parities (£1 ≈ 25.2 francs ≈ 20.4 marks ≈ 9.5 roubles ≈ $4.87) make pre-war accounting restful — then the August 1914 moratoria, closed bourses, Trading with the Enemy sequestrations, Soviet bank nationalisation (December 1917) and the mark's death in 1923 arrive on the world clock. Which passport opened each account decides whether it is seized: the player hedges identity, not merely currency.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy/business primary; deception (financial identities per legend); items (losable, stoppable instruments); travel (correspondent networks shape routes); politics/warfare (sequestration, inflation); hunt (drawings are records); time pressure (closing windows).
- **Historical grounding:** Gold-standard parities; specimen-signature practice; UK moratorium and Stock Exchange closure 31 July 1914–4 January 1915; Trading with the Enemy Acts 1914; German Devisenverordnung 1916; Soviet decrees 1917–18; hyperinflation to November 1923.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Endowment and loss aversion at scale; the visible approach of collapse creates anticipatory tension and system-generated "get it out in time" set-pieces; hoarding versus liquidation is endlessly replayable.
- **Engine implications:** Instrument objects with jurisdiction × date rule tables; historical exchange series as data; sequestration hooks keyed to legend nationality.
- **Risks / open questions:** Replay foreknowledge of 1914 and 1923 — perturb timing; keep exposure uncertain.

## P03.05 — The Document Regime Graph, 1895–1925
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every travel edge carries a dated document requirement — none, passport, visa, exit permit, photograph — that changes with the world clock, so one route is a stroll in 1908, a wall in 1915 and a queue in 1921.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Before 1914 the player crosses Western and Central Europe without papers, but Russia demands a consular visa, swaps the passport for a residence permit and requires an exit permit; the Ottoman Empire wants passports. Decrees of late July–August 1914 impose passports in Germany and France; Britain adds photograph passports in 1915, compulsory from November 1915; the Soviet state, the League's standardised booklet (1920) and the Nansen certificate (1922) redraw the map again. Each legend's papers carry issuer, dates, photograph, description. Forgeries fail inspection probabilistically; papers genuinely issued on false particulars (the lax pre-1915 British declaration system) pass but leave archival trails. Journeys become document chains planned around consulate hours.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and deception primary; items (papers as versioned artefacts); economy (fees, bribes, visa brokers); politics/warfare (war and revolution switch regimes); hunt (inspection points are reveal points); time pressure (expiries, imminent regime change).
- **Historical grounding:** Passport-free pre-1914 Europe; 1914 decrees; British Nationality and Status of Aliens Act 1914; 1920 Paris passport conference; Nansen passport 1922; US quota acts 1921/1924.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Planning satisfaction plus information-gap curiosity about the next frontier's demands; mid-journey regime shifts produce stranded-at-the-frontier drama; a well-built document set is a cherished possession (endowment effect).
- **Engine implications:** Edge requirement tables keyed by (origin, destination, legend nationality, date); inspection as a probabilistic check against papers and hunter alerts.
- **Risks / open questions:** Pre-1914 may feel easy; make Russia and Ottoman lands high-value, high-paper regions.

## P03.06 — Jurisdiction as Terrain: Extradition, Capitulations and Police Reach
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The hunter's power is bounded by extradition treaties, the political-offence exception, consular jurisdiction and dated inter-police agreements. The passport in your pocket decides which law applies, so a spy with several legends chooses jurisdiction by choosing identity.
- **How it plays (core loop):** In Constantinople before 1 October 1914 a British-papered player arrested by Ottoman police goes before the British consular court, a Russian-papered one to the Russian consulate, an Ottoman subject to the local court; Shanghai, Egypt and Persia follow the same capitulatory logic. Britain has no extradition treaty with Russia and surrenders no political offenders, making London a sanctuary until wartime alien registration. The Okhrana's Paris bureau works closely with the Sûreté, so France is porous to Russian pursuit; Switzerland shelters exiles but expels troublemakers. Secret protocols (Rome 1898, St Petersburg 1904) share anarchist files; the International Criminal Police Commission (Vienna, 1923) networks everyone. Sanctuary is a resource the clock erodes.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and politics primary; deception (legend nationality as legal shield); travel (routing through favourable jurisdictions); economy (consular registration, sureties, bribes); items (protection papers, consular certificates); warfare and time pressure (capitulations abolished 1914).
- **Historical grounding:** UK Extradition Act 1870; Ottoman capitulations and consular courts; Egyptian Mixed Courts (1876); Okhrana Zagranichnaya agentura; Swiss Bundesanwaltschaft political police (1889); ICPC 1923.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Terrain reading is the oldest strategic pleasure; standing legally untouchable while watched, then losing that status to a treaty or a war, yields tension/relief cycles generated by the system.
- **Engine implications:** A cooperation/extradition matrix (service × jurisdiction × date) consulted before any arrest or request; regime-event hooks.
- **Risks / open questions:** Legal nuance must compress to a few reach levels or become opaque.

## P03.07 — Stale Bradshaws: Timetables, Calendars and Clocks as Owned Knowledge
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The network is revealed only by the dated guides the player owns — Bradshaw's monthly Continental Guide, Cook's, the Reichskursbuch, the Russian official indicator — which go stale at seasonal changeovers, vanish in wartime and quote times in different clocks and calendars.
- **How it plays (core loop):** An outdated Bradshaw yields phantom connections and stranded nights; fresh knowledge is bought at stations or learned from porters. Russian railways run on St Petersburg time along the whole line and Russia keeps the Julian calendar until 1918; France keeps Paris time until 1911; Ottoman clocks count from sunset. The engine keeps one true clock, but every displayed time passes through the local regime — a forgery with the wrong calendar is a tell for the hunter and a skill check for the forger. Baltic ice, strikes (Russia 1905, France 1910, Britain 1911 and 1919) and the August 1914 suspension of civilian trains perturb the schedule.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel primary; items (guides as dated artefacts); deception/coding (calendar tells); economy (guide prices, fares, classes); politics/warfare (war timetables); hunt (pursuers read the same timetables); time pressure (fixed departures, edition expiry).
- **Historical grounding:** Bradshaw's Continental Railway Guide (monthly, suspended for the war); changeovers typically 1 May and 1 October; Petersburg railway time; Julian calendar to 31 January 1918; French adoption of GMT 1911.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Mastery over an information artefact — the pleasure of knowing the book; planning under uncertainty with crisp feedback; "one more leg" compulsion when a connection is discovered.
- **Engine implications:** Schedule graph versioned by edition; player knowledge as a subset view; a true-time to local-time rendering layer.
- **Risks / open questions:** Overlaps the travel designer's remit; owned-edition rules must stay convenient or become busywork.

## P03.08 — The Wire Is the Market: News Latency, Agencies and Editions
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** World-clock events do not reach cities instantly; they propagate through cable routes, the Reuters–Havas–Wolff territorial cartel, multiple daily editions and wartime press bureaux with measurable latency and distortion. Whoever is upstream acts in the gap.
- **How it plays (core loop):** An event fires at time T; the engine computes arrival per city from cable hops, agency territory (Havas for Latin Europe and South America, Reuters for the Empire and Far East, Wolff for Germany, Scandinavia and Russia), censor delay and next edition time. The player trades before a bourse reprices or crosses a frontier before a ban is gazetted. The hunt uses the same channels: a wanted notice spreads city by city, so the player outruns it along low-latency corridors. Planting items through press bureaux and bribed correspondents feeds false states into rivals' propagation.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy/business and politics/warfare primary; travel (latency corridors); deception/coding (planted news, coded commercial cables); items (ticker tape, dated editions); hunt (notice propagation); time pressure (gaps of minutes to days).
- **Historical grounding:** The agencies' "ring combination"; Exchange Telegraph tickers from 1872; multi-edition London evening papers; UK Press Bureau (August 1914); bourse closures of 1914; publication of the Zimmermann telegram (1 March 1917).
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The oldest dopamine — knowing before they do; anticipation and information-gap curiosity; markets give immediate numerical reward for latency mastery, and the same skill saves lives in the hunt.
- **Engine implications:** A propagation layer over the communications graph with per-city knowledge states; cheap to scale because every macro-event uses the same code.
- **Risks / open questions:** Replaying players know the macro-calendar — perturb timing and lean on contingent micro-events; markets must not swallow the life sim.

## P03.09 — Timetables of War: Mobilisation, Requisition and Train-Watching
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** War in this period was a railway timetable. Military movement runs on the same graph as civilian travel, so mobilisation seizes the player's trains, and intelligence is literally counting carriages.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Peacetime: general staffs hold mobilisation schedules as data objects (trains per day per bridge); fragments are the top prize. Crisis: as mobilisation is declared the civilian timetable collapses edge by edge (Germany and France suspended passenger traffic in early August 1914), frontiers close, requisition strips rolling stock, and a player on the wrong side improvises. War: the player builds or joins observation networks counting train composition — ratios of coaches, wagons and flat cars reveal infantry, supply or artillery — reporting through censored channels, as networks in occupied Belgium did in 1916–18. Warfare is observable as flows, never as battles.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics/warfare and travel primary; coding (reports via neutral post); items (schedules, counts, maps); economy (requisition kills trade, war contracts create it); hunt (counter-intelligence sweeps watching zones); time pressure (mobilisation is a countdown).
- **Historical grounding:** Great General Staff railway section and roughly 11,000 German mobilisation trains; French plan de transport; UK Railway Executive Committee (4 August 1914); Austro-Hungarian deployment plans sold to Russia in 1913; Belgian train-watching organisations' composition counts.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** An abstract war becomes visceral through the player's own logistics; the mobilisation countdown is a supreme tension/relief cycle; sampling flows is a slot machine of partial information (variable-ratio reinforcement).
- **Engine implications:** Military flows as scheduled edge loads; civilian capacity equals total minus military; observers sample loads noisily. Reuses the travel graph.
- **Risks / open questions:** Depth versus legibility; campaigns must abstract to flows and front states.

## P03.10 — Backstopped Lives: Legends as Institutional Record-Sets
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** A cover identity is the set of institutional records a real person would have — bank references, consular registration, club membership, a Meldeamt card. The player builds legends by generating those records through play; the hunter breaks them by auditing.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Every ordinary act under a legend — opening an account, registering at a hotel, joining a club, filing a Berlin residence registration — deepens its record-set. A suspicious hunter runs period inquiries: a commercial-agency report (Schimmelpfeng, Dun), a consular register check, a Medical Register lookup, an Einwohnermeldeamt request. Inconsistencies — a "doctor" absent from the Register, a photograph passport dated pre-1915 — accumulate doubt. Legends age and incur debts; a shallow legend is cheap and brittle, a deep one costly and traceable. Britain, unregistered before 1915, is where people are invented; Germany, with resident cards on everyone, is where inventions die.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception and hunt primary; travel (registration regimes decide where invention works); items (documents, monogrammed luggage, tailors' labels); economy (each legend has upkeep); politics/warfare (legend nationality decides internment and sequestration from 1914); time pressure (records arrive and age).
- **Historical grounding:** German Meldewesen and Einwohnermeldeämter; commercial inquiry agencies; professional directories; UK National Registration Act 1915; photograph passports 1915; police identification by laundry marks and tailors' labels.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The IKEA effect — players value legends they built; loss aversion when one burns; a construction loop against an intelligent auditor produces mastery and emergent betrayal by one's own carelessness.
- **Engine implications:** Record store shared with the Paper Trail; legend graph with consistency constraints; hunter inquiries as queries with cost and lag.
- **Risks / open questions:** Bookkeeping burden — needs a clear summary of legend depth and exposure.

## P03.11 — The Neutral Hub, 1914–1918
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** A complete game inside the war, played from the neutral cities where every service operated at once — Rotterdam, Copenhagen, Stockholm, Berne, Madrid, New York until April 1917 — under blockade economics, double censorship and neutrality police whose expulsion ends the game.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player freelances for several belligerents. Travel is a war graph: North Sea crossings under mine and submarine risk, the single Haparanda–Torneå land crossing to Russia (change of train and gauge, passport check, sledge or carriage between the towns until the 1919 bridge), the electrified Belgian–Dutch fence closing an edge from 1915. Blockade arbitrage: the Netherlands Overseas Trust polices imports, navicerts (1916) and rationing agreements squeeze neutral trade, smuggling margins explode. Letters between enemies pass only through neutrals, both censors reading. Guilder and Swiss franc hold value as belligerent currencies melt. The hunt is three-sided — enemy counter-intelligence, paymasters suspecting double-dealing, and Dutch or Swiss political police who simply expel. Russia's 1917 collapse and America's entry rewrite the hub map mid-game.
- **Directive coverage:** All seven are structural; politics/warfare, economy and hunt primary; the war itself is the time pressure.
- **Historical grounding:** Rotterdam as base for Allied networks into occupied Belgium; German networks in the Netherlands and Spain; the "Swedish roundabout" cable channel; Netherlands Overseas Trust (1914); Swiss Bundesanwaltschaft.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** A pressure-cooker with maximal double-dealing and a known four-year arc gives relentless tension/relief; scarcity makes every purchase meaningful; the three-sided hunt turns paranoia into a resource-management skill.
- **Engine implications:** Smaller map, denser rules — the ideal vertical slice of the full engine with war-era table values.
- **Risks / open questions:** Narrower than the 25-year life-sim brief; offer as a scenario or the engine's first slice.

## P03.12 — The Concession Agent: Commerce as the Engine of Espionage
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player is a commission agent brokering the period's great concessions and contracts — railways, oil, arms, wartime munitions — won by espionage: stolen tenders, bought officials, friendly legations. Business funds spying, spying wins business; the hunt comes from rivals' inquiry agents and double-crossed services.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A concession is a multi-year project object needing a registered company (a Belgian société anonyme for Russian ventures), a guaranteeing bank (Deutsche Bank, Banque Impériale Ottomane, HSBC), a sovereign grant (irade, firman, ukase) via intermediaries and bakshish, and legation backing the politics system grants or withdraws. The player rides the Bradshaw graph between capital markets, legations and concession territory, sells intelligence to services with a stake in the grant, and takes commissions on munitions contracts. The world clock renegotiates everything: 1908 reopens Ottoman grants, 1914 sequesters enemy concessions, 1917–18 expropriates all of Russia — where the fortunes sit.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy/business and politics primary; travel (constant journeys); deception/coding (commercial code cables as espionage cover); items (deeds, tenders, bank guarantees); hunt (private inquiry agencies, rival services); time pressure (tenders, option expiries, regime change).
- **Historical grounding:** Baghdad Railway concession (1903) with kilometric guarantees through the Ottoman Public Debt Administration; Persian oil concession 1901; Yalu timber concession; Turkish Petroleum Company 1912–14; Russian munitions contracts via New York agents 1915–16.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Long-horizon investment with variable-ratio payoff is the compulsive core of tycoon play; a life-and-death hunt layered on sunk costs yields "one more month" persistence and emergent tragedy.
- **Engine implications:** Project objects with dependency chains and political preconditions; faction utilities over concessions; a direct test of the directive-5 engine.
- **Risks / open questions:** Two interlocking careers risk complexity; espionage verbs must be mandatory.

# Round 1A Proposals — Travel & Logistics Designer (Panelist P04)

## Panelist statement
Travel should be the primary verb of this game: it is the one action that necessarily spends time and money, moves the player through jurisdictions, and leaves evidence for the hunters, so every other system can hang from it. The game must get three things right: the network is a real timetable, not a map with distances; routes are knowledge the player acquires, loses and sells, not a menu; and the pursuer is bound by the same timetable and the same telegraph as the pursued. If those hold, "will I make the connection?" becomes the heartbeat of a thirty-year spy life.

## P04.01 — The Known World: Your Timetable Book Is Your Character Sheet
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player sees only the part of the transport network they currently know, each edge stamped with when and how they learned it. Progression is the growth and decay of this known world while the real network changes.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Plan on the known graph. Add edges by buying or stealing current guides (monthly editions), reading station posters, questioning porters, concierges and agency clerks. Stale edges fail at run time as ghost connections: a service withdrawn, a ferry ice-bound, a line cut by a front. Session loop: learn, plan, travel, update. Career loop: the known world migrates with commissions and the hunt, and corridor knowledge becomes saleable.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel primary. Deception/conversation: routes are extracted socially and can be faked (feeding a pursuer false connections). Items: dated guides and tickets. Economy: a known smuggling corridor is a business. Politics/warfare: wars and treaties rewrite the real graph. Hunt: hunters hold their own known graph; routes they lack are your escape.
- **Historical grounding:** Bradshaw's Continental Guide (monthly), Reichskursbuch, Indicateur Chaix; new lines (Simplon 1906, Tauern 1909, Lötschberg 1913, Circum-Baikal 1905, Amur 1916); Orient Express suspended 1914, Balkanzug 1916–18, Simplon-Orient from 1919.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Information-gap curiosity and collection drive; literal competence (going where others cannot); loss aversion as hard-won knowledge rots; emergent stories from the gap between believed and true graph.
- **Engine implications:** Date-versioned master graph with validity windows per edge; per-player overlay of known edges carrying learned-date, source, confidence; planner routes on the overlay, simulator executes on the truth and emits discrepancy events; overlays are sparse.
- **Risks / open questions:** Ghost connections feel unfair without legible confidence cues and recovery options.

## P04.02 — The Connection: A Timetable Clock Where Waiting Is Exposure
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The world clock is the timetable. Every journey is a chain of scheduled departures, and the atomic unit of tension is the connection: the gap between arrival and next departure, when the player is stationary, visible and spending.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player reads departure boards and builds chains; each leg has departure time, duration and reliability. Tight connections risk being missed (delay drawn from line and season); loose ones create dead hours at junctions where hunters' telegrams catch up and money drains. Missing the last connection forces an overnight stop and a compulsory hotel registration. Sunday and seasonal timetables matter; night trains buy distance and anonymity.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and time pressure primary. Hunt: dwell time at a node converts a vague trace into a sighting. Deception: dead hours are when meetings, cable-reading and ticket swaps happen. Items: through tickets with break-of-journey validity are stored slack. Economy: waiting costs fares, hotels, cabs. Politics: wartime and strike timetables shrink the options.
- **Historical grounding:** Bradshaw's connection tables; Sunday and seasonal services; night-boat patterns (Harwich–Hook of Holland, Dover–Calais); compulsory hotel registration in Germany, Austria, France and Russia; station telegraph offices.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Tension/relief cycles at a cadence of minutes; near-miss effect when a connection is just made; Zeigarnik pull of an unfinished chain ("one more leg"); mastery curve from naive planning to deliberate buffer management.
- **Engine implications:** Time-dependent routing over service patterns (calendar bitmaps per service) with connection-scan queries; delays as seeded distributions so replays are deterministic; node dwell emits events consumed by hunter and economy subsystems.
- **Risks / open questions:** Over-punishing randomness; delay probability must be legible before commitment.

## P04.03 — Luggage, Weight and the Customs Gamble
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Inventory is luggage: discrete pieces with weight, volume and visibility, split between hand luggage (with you, searched at frontiers) and registered luggage (in the van, following the ticket, sealed through or examined). What you carry shapes where you can go and how fast.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Pack before each journey: false bottoms, concealed documents, trade goods, gold. Each frontier and port applies an examination probability modified by class, nationality on papers, war status and the official's incentives. Registered luggage can be sent ahead by slow freight, decoupling you from incriminating items; left-luggage offices serve as caches and dead drops with receipts as tradable keys; hotel and steamship labels accumulate on trunks.
- **Directive coverage:** Items/artifacts and travel primary. Deception: luggage must match the cover (a commercial traveller carries samples). Economy: 80 Days-style trade bounded by weight and excess charges. Politics/warfare: wartime contraband lists change what is seizable. Hunt: a seized trunk is evidence bearing your labels; a trunk on another route splits the trail.
- **Historical grounding:** Weight-based excess charges with allowances varying by country and class; frontier customs halls; sealed through luggage; grande vitesse versus petite vitesse consignment in France; consigne/left-luggage offices; concealment compartments in travelling trunks.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Risk-reward decisions with visible odds; endowment effect and loss aversion around carried artifacts; packing as a puzzle; the customs gamble is a repeated micro-climax with real stakes.
- **Engine implications:** Item containers as graph entities travelling independently (luggage has its own itinerary and arrival events); examination as a rule table keyed on edge, date, class and papers; cheap to scale.
- **Risks / open questions:** Inventory fiddliness; needs sane defaults and a porter option.

## P04.04 — The Frontier Station: Regimes as Time-Dependent Edge Attributes
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every border-crossing edge carries a regime record valid for a date range: passport requirement, visa, exit permit, customs intensity, currency control, gauge break, quarantine. Crossings are dense decision nodes, and the layer flips with history (August 1914 above all).
- **How it plays (core loop):** At a frontier the player chooses crossing point, hour, class and identity. Gauge breaks (Russian broad gauge at Wirballen/Eydtkuhnen; Spanish gauge at Irun or Port Bou) force everyone off the train into a hall of passport desks, customs, money changers and a telegraph office: fifteen to ninety minutes of checked papers, opened trunks and recorded names. Before 1914 most Western crossings are near-formalities while Russia and the Ottoman Empire demand passports and visas; after 1914 every edge demands papers and exit permits.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel, deception and politics primary. Deception: the presented identity must hold valid documents for the regime; forgeries have per-regime detection odds. Items: visas and stamps accumulate as a dated trail. Economy: compulsory exchange and currency controls. Warfare: fronts delete edges; neutral corridors (Switzerland, Netherlands, Scandinavia) become bottlenecks. Hunt: frontier records are the richest trace hunters receive.
- **Historical grounding:** Passport-free Western Europe before 1914; Russian and Ottoman passport regimes; wartime exit permits; 1920 League passport conference; gauge breaks; frontier customs halls.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Anticipatory dread then relief; mastery of a rule set that changes under you; a clean crossing with contraband is a repeatable peak experience.
- **Engine implications:** Edge attribute layers with validity windows, evaluated by a rules table at crossing time; documents as entities checked against the regime; macro-events patch the layer.
- **Risks / open questions:** Data volume; abstract minor crossings into regional defaults.

## P04.05 — The Trace Ledger: Every Mode Leaves a Different Mark
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Each travel action writes trace records with four properties: what they reveal (mode, class, name, destination), forwarding latency, fidelity and persistence. Hunters never see the player; they consume the ledger through institutions at institutional speed.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A named sleeping-car berth is high-fidelity and persistent; a third-class day ticket reveals mode and direction only; a hotel form reaches the police overnight; a steamer passenger list is filed at the port; a cable receipt sits in the telegraph copy book for months; a cab is remembered for a day. The player sees an estimate of their own trail and thins it (tickets bought leg by leg, class changed mid-journey, a porter as cut-out). Reveals occur when a record matches the wanted description: Scotland Yard's cadence, driven by paperwork.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and travel primary. Deception: aliases on tickets and registers are the main lever. Items: receipts and labels are trace and evidence. Economy: anonymity costs money or comfort. Politics/warfare: wartime registration thickens every record. Coding: cables are readable by whoever holds the copy.
- **Historical grounding:** Hotel police forms; sleeping-car berths reserved under a name; printed passenger lists; retained telegraph copies; Bertillonage and fingerprint files; press wanted notices.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Managed paranoia: inferring what the unseen hunter knows is a theory-of-mind puzzle; controlling one's visibility satisfies; near-misses when a reveal lands one node behind you.
- **Engine implications:** Append-only event stream with per-record delivery time; hunter AI computes possible-position sets from delivered records only (cheap reachability on the time-expanded graph); cost is linear in actions.
- **Risks / open questions:** Keeping the player's estimate legible without making the hunter omniscient or trivial.

## P04.06 — Two Speeds of Pursuit: Warrant by Wire, Hunter by Train (risky)
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Pursuit propagates at two speeds: information at telegraph speed, but only between offices, in office hours, through jurisdictions that must agree to act; bodies on the player's own timetable. The hunt is played in the gap.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A matched trace becomes a wanted notice cabled to posts with an office and a liaison agreement; each hop has delay and loss. Hunter agents use the player's planner, suffering the same connections, strikes and frontiers, and needing permission to operate abroad. The player exploits this: cross into a jurisdiction without liaison, travel where offices are sparse (Ottoman interior, Russian provinces, at sea before wireless), move when offices are closed, use routes hunters do not know (P04.01).
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and travel primary. Coding: wanted notices are cables, interceptable and forgeable. Deception: liaison requests can be poisoned with false descriptions. Economy: bribing clerks and station police is a budget line. Politics: liaison follows alliances (the Franco-Russian alliance underwrites the Okhrana's Paris bureau; 1917 severs it). Items: a forged release telegram.
- **Historical grounding:** Telegraph network with retained copies; Okhrana foreign bureau, Special Branch and Sûreté cooperation; shipboard wireless from the mid-1900s; extradition and consular liaison as the slow human layer.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Outwitting a fair opponent bound by visible physics; racing your own description produces high-arousal planning; legible asymmetry makes victories feel earned.
- **Engine implications:** Two propagation models on two graphs (wire graph with office hours; transport graph with timetables); hunters as agents sharing the planner; possible-position reasoning updated per delivered notice.
- **Risks / open questions:** Hunter AI legibility and cost; needs debugging views and a hunter cap.

## P04.07 — Fares, Classes and the Price of Anonymity
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Class is a four-way trade between speed, comfort, society and visibility. Expresses and trains de luxe carry only first and second class at a supplement and record names; third class is slow, exhausting and anonymous. Money is instruments liquid only at certain nodes and hours.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Fare tables price each leg by class and distance, plus express, berth and reservation supplements. First class buys conversation access to officials, officers and businessmen (deals, rumours, routes) but writes your name into lists; third class costs fatigue and time but leaves almost no record. Cash is spatial: letters of credit and circular notes cash only at correspondent banks in business hours; gold is universal but heavy and seizable; after 1914 exchange controls and depreciating paper constrain routes. Circular-tour tickets permit breaks of journey; buying leg by leg conceals the destination.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and travel primary. Deception: class must match cover. Hunt: anonymity is purchased with discomfort. Items: tickets, coupons, notes. Politics/warfare: wartime fares and controls. Time pressure: bank hours and Sundays delay cash.
- **Historical grounding:** Gold-standard parities until 1914; Cook's circular notes; Wagons-Lits supplements; German express surcharge; many Continental expresses first/second class only; circular-tour tickets; rouble collapse 1917; mark hyperinflation 1923.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Constant small optimisation with visible trade-offs; scarcity makes routine choices meaningful; first-class social opportunities make spending feel like investing, which fights hoarding.
- **Engine implications:** Fare function per edge (distance band × class × supplements × date); money as typed instruments with node-and-hour liquidity; conversation opportunity tables keyed to class.
- **Risks / open questions:** Keep the fatigue model simple; tune so third class tempts rather than merely punishes.

## P04.08 — Disruption Weather: Strikes, Ice, Requisition and the Forecast Market
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The network has weather: a disruption layer partly deterministic (seasonal closures), partly scheduled by the world clock (war, revolution), partly stochastic (strikes, accidents, floods), and all of it forecastable at a price through newspapers, agency cables and insiders.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Before committing the player checks forecasts: strike rumours in the press, a shipping agent's cable, frost reports. Deterministic closures shape the calendar: St Petersburg's sea approach ices for months while Libau stays open; Lake Baikal is crossed by icebreaker ferry, by rails on the ice in winter 1904, by the Circum-Baikal line from 1905. Scheduled disruptions follow history: the October 1905 Russian strike halts the railways; the 1910 French and 1911 British railway strikes; 1914 requisition cuts civilian services. Buffer time is insurance; information reduces the buffer needed.
- **Directive coverage:** Time pressure and travel primary. Economy: information has a price, and disruption moves commodity prices, enabling trade. Politics/warfare: the macro-clock is the main disruption source. Deception/conversation: insiders and clerks leak forecasts. Items: newspapers and cables as dated information objects. Hunt: disruptions also strand hunters and create crowds to hide in.
- **Historical grounding:** As above; also wartime permit zones and epidemic port quarantines.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Planning under uncertainty with purchasable information engages anticipation and the pleasure of a correct forecast; variable-ratio disruption keeps routine travel from going flat; emergent stories ("the strike that stranded me in the wrong jurisdiction").
- **Engine implications:** Disruption layer as scheduled plus seeded-random edge modifiers tuned by region and year; forecast objects are events with lead time and accuracy; one shared layer affects all agents.
- **Risks / open questions:** Must not feel arbitrary; always offer a forecast channel.

## P04.09 — Cover Is a Route: Itinerary Plausibility
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every cover identity implies a pattern of movement, and every journey is a statement about who you are. Hunters and officials compare your observed itinerary against the pattern your papers claim; implausibility, not bad papers, is what gets you detained.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each identity carries an itinerary template: a textile-house commercial traveller visits manufacturing towns on weekdays in second class; a tourist follows guidebook circuits in season; a concession engineer travels to railheads and ports. Actual legs are scored against the active identity (season, class, frontier frequency, direction). Low plausibility raises examination odds at frontiers and hotels and accelerates matching in the trace ledger. The player repairs it by inserting business-like legs (costly in time, but potentially profitable), switching identity where no record links the two, or carrying supporting artifacts (samples, order books, letters of introduction).
- **Directive coverage:** Deception and travel primary. Economy: the cover's business should actually trade, funding travel. Items: the supporting artifacts. Politics/warfare: war redefines plausible movement (a tourist near a front is a spy). Time pressure: repairs cost days. Hunt: plausibility modifies every trace record.
- **Historical grounding:** Commercial travellers as a ubiquitous, unremarked class of passenger; letters of introduction and visiting cards; hotel registration; wartime suspicion of foreigners; legends matched to movement in real tradecraft.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Role-consistency as a puzzle; self-expression through chosen covers; the satisfaction of a journey that is simultaneously profitable, plausible and operational.
- **Engine implications:** Plausibility scoring as pattern matching over the itinerary log; identity templates as data; the score feeds examination and matching rules.
- **Risks / open questions:** Scoring must be explainable, or detentions feel unjust.

## P04.10 — The Long Leg: Compartment Time as a Bounded Session
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Long legs (Atlantic five to six days, London–Bombay via Suez two to three weeks, Trans-Siberian ten to fourteen days) are sealed sessions: no exit, fixed company, limited information in, and a world that moves on until arrival dumps the accumulated news.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Boarding commits the player. Inside, time is spent on an action economy: working ciphers on intercepted cables, forging papers, conversation with the passenger body (a social graph of institutions and types), trading at ports of call, writing letters for the next port, resting to recover health. News arrives only at port stops or by shipboard wireless once it exists, so decisions rest on stale information. Hunters cannot board mid-sea but can wire ahead, so the arrival port is a known choke point.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and time pressure primary. Coding/deception: the leg is the natural workshop for ciphers and legends. Items: tools and documents carried. Economy: port trade and shipboard gambling. Politics/warfare: a war can begin mid-crossing, changing the destination port's regime. Hunt: the wire-ahead problem.
- **Historical grounding:** Liner schedules; shipboard wireless bulletins from the mid-1900s; Wagons-Lits and Trans-Siberian de luxe trains; port quarantine; August 1914 stranding passengers mid-voyage.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Commitment devices heighten stakes; bounded sessions create natural stopping points and anticipation of arrival; the information blackout produces dramatic reveals generated by simulation, not authoring.
- **Engine implications:** Leg as a container state with an action budget; the world simulation continues; news gated by node (port) or technology (wireless by date); hunter messages to the destination are scheduled events.
- **Risks / open questions:** Legs must not become dead time; the action budget needs real content.

## P04.11 — The Dispatcher: Couriers, Cables and Cargo on One Timetable
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The spy is a network operator. The timetable graph that moves the player also moves the couriers, documents, contraband, money and agents they dispatch, each with per-node interception risk and dependence on connections. The career is building and defending routes, not only riding them.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Take commissions (plans from port to capital; arms samples to a buyer; an agent out of a hostile city). Plan itineraries for things and people: registered luggage, ambulant post, cable, courier in third class. Each edge carries interception odds shaped by regime, censorship and the hunters' attention. Build assets: cover addresses, poste restante drops, a shipping agency as legitimate business. Revenue comes from logistics clients (arms firms, concession hunters, newspapers); the hunt traces the network rather than the person; a compromised node cascades and everything in flight needs rerouting.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel, economy and hunt primary. Coding: cable routes and code books. Deception: the legitimate business as cover. Items: consignments as tracked entities. Politics/warfare: censorship, blockade and fronts reshape corridors; wartime smuggling margins explode. Time pressure: consignments move while you sleep.
- **Historical grounding:** Commercial code books; TPO/ambulant post; telegraph copy retention and wartime censorship offices; arms commission agents; wartime contraband control and neutral routes.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Optimisation pleasure and system-building; ownership of a growing machine; cascading failures create emergent crises of the player's own making.
- **Engine implications:** Any entity can hold an itinerary; interception as a rule table; a dependency graph between consignments, nodes and commissions so one compromise propagates; entities are cheap, so it scales.
- **Risks / open questions:** Can become a spreadsheet; keep the player's body central with legs ridden in person.

## P04.12 — Alibi Arithmetic: Several Lives Held Apart by the Timetable (risky)
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** Several identities with households in different cities, each with a presence record (where seen, when). Hunters test whether two records could be one body against the real timetable: if the fastest connection between sightings exceeds the gap, they are different people; otherwise suspicion links them. The game is keeping lives timetable-incompatible.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each household costs rent, servants and visibility; each identity runs its own business, debts and obligations demanding appearances on dates. The player plans circuits so that one identity's dinner here and another's bank visit there are provably impossible for a single person, or exploits a connection the hunters do not know (P04.01). Night trains, through sleepers and new air services shrink the world and threaten alibis; wars that cut lines strengthen them.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and deception primary. Economy: households and businesses with separate ledgers. Items: document sets per identity. Politics/warfare: an identity may be a belligerent's national, closing or opening edges. Time pressure: obligations fall due. Hunt: the correlation engine is the hunter.
- **Historical grounding:** Multi-identity, multi-city households of freelance agents; hotel and residence registration; passport regimes binding name to face after 1914; shrinking journey times as tunnels, through trains and air services open.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Plate-spinning cognitive load with clear rules; dread of correlation; deep satisfaction when an alibi holds because you knew a night service existed.
- **Engine implications:** Presence records as events; correlation via fastest-path queries on the time-dependent graph (bounded, cacheable); identity as a first-class entity with its own ledger.
- **Risks / open questions:** Complexity may overwhelm; needs a clear identity ledger and slow onboarding (one life first).

# Round 1A Proposals — Deception, Conversation & Cryptography Designer (Panelist P05)

## Panelist statement
My lens is information as the game's true currency: every fact has a cost to obtain, a cost to verify, a shelf-life and a market, and every lie is a debt the world may call in. Deception must never be dialogue flavour; it must be a system of typed claims, belief models, documents and messages that the hunter plays by the same rules as the player. The game must get right the asymmetry of knowledge — what each actor believes, how fast a belief can be checked through period infrastructure, and what it costs to keep a false picture standing.

## P05.01 — The Legend Ledger: Cover Identities as Falsifiable Fact-Graphs
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** A legend is a graph of typed claims (birthplace, employer, regiment, referees, bank), each with a *verification cost* — the real days and money a hunter needs to check it through period infrastructure — and a *corroboration level* raised by planting evidence.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player assembles legends in a ledger, favouring claims cheap to support and slow to check (an overseas registry answers in weeks; a Berlin employer in a day). Every assertion in conversation or on a hotel police form joins the legend's record. When a hunter checks, per-claim clocks run; a forewarned player shores it up (bribe, forged reference, back-dated register) or burns the legend. Concurrent legends in different cities must never be placeable together: the timetable is an alibi constraint.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception primary. Travel: verification latency is routing; alibis are timetable facts. Items: references, cards, registers. Economy: a merchant legend needs a real account. Politics: which registries each service reaches. Hunt: verification clocks; burned legends.
- **Historical grounding:** Passport-free Western Europe before 1914 alongside compulsory hotel registration (fiche d'hôtel, Meldezettel); letters of introduction; registries queried by post; consular enquiries by cable; wartime passport photographs and exit permits.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Spinning-plates cognitive load with legible failure; anticipatory dread from visible clocks; authorship pride in a legend surviving inspection; loss aversion when a long-tended identity burns.
- **Engine implications:** Claim registry of canonical propositions; verification as scheduled events with latency computed from the communications and travel graphs; per-faction registry access; lazy evaluation (clocks spawn only when someone checks).
- **Risks / open questions:** Players must remember their legends — needs an excellent ledger interface; latency tuning so checks bite, not annoy.

## P05.02 — Every Lie Is a Debt: The Claims Ledger with Maturity Dates
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every falsehood the player tells becomes a *liability* with a principal (distance from truth), a holder (who believes it) and a *maturity date* — when the world's clock will naturally test it ("my partner arrives on Thursday's Nord-Express").
- **How it plays (core loop):** A lie maturing untested hardens into accepted fact. A lie maturing tested defaults: suspicion, a demanded explanation, a burned relationship. Before maturity the player can *service* the debt (make it true — put a partner on that train), *roll it over* (a supporting lie, compounding principal and widening who must be deceived) or *buy it back* (partial confession, paid in rapport). Each outstanding lie raises its holder's verification appetite. Sessions acquire a rhythm of due dates.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception primary; time pressure intrinsic (maturities are world-clock events). Travel: lies about movement are serviced by travelling. Economy: lies about money, with money. Politics: lies to factions are debts to factions. Hunt: defaulted lies are leads.
- **Historical grounding:** Period society ran on checkable promises — bills of exchange with due dates, letters of credit, published steamer arrivals, society columns — so stated intentions were verifiable within days.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Converts deception into the gripping psychology of debt: sunk cost, compounding, relief at a quiet maturity, dread at a loud one; emergent stories when lies collide.
- **Engine implications:** Each lie is a proposition with holder set, truth-distance and a test event bound to a world-clock trigger; servicing updates truth value; propagation when holders talk. Scales as a priority queue of maturities.
- **Risks / open questions:** Free-text speech cannot be classified — claims come from typed menus; the ledger must read as tension, not bookkeeping.

## P05.03 — The Exchange: Conversation as a Game of Claims, Calls and Tells
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Conversation is a turn-based information game with poker's structure: parties make *claims*, back them with *evidence* (items, witnesses, hardened facts), *bluff* when they lack it, and either side may *call*. No dialogue trees — the state is a belief model, a want-list and resources.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each party has Composure (depletes under pressure), Rapport (per pair), Leverage (secrets, documents) and an Agenda (what to learn, obtain, conceal). Moves: Assert, Probe, Offer, Press, Concede, Deflect, Call. A Call forces evidence; a matching document wins the point; a bluff resolves against the opponent's suspicion and the player's composure. Every Assert commits a proposition to the opponent's belief model (feeding P05.02). NPCs have *tell profiles* — observable deltas when they bluff — learnable over repeated exchanges.
- **Directive coverage:** Conversation/deception primary. Items are evidence. Economy: Offers trade money, introductions, commissions. Politics: faction agendas drive NPC want-lists. Travel: timetable facts are admissible evidence ("you could not have been in Trieste"). Hunt: interrogation is this game played short of composure.
- **Historical grounding:** A society of calling cards, letters of introduction and club references where standing was continuously asserted and tested; Bertillonage and fingerprint evidence entering police interviews.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Bluff/call yields variable-ratio reinforcement and genuine tension; tell-reading gives a mastery curve; evidence-backed wins reward preparation done elsewhere in the game.
- **Engine implications:** Per-NPC bounded belief sets with confidence and provenance; agenda-driven NPC move selection (utility over information gain and loss); evidence matching as proposition unification. NPCs outside the hot set keep summary beliefs only.
- **Risks / open questions:** Can abstract moves feel like talking? Needs feedback strong enough that players learn opponents, not menus.

## P05.04 — Wires and Post: Every Message Is an Object on the Network
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Telegrams and letters are physical entities on the player's own scheduled network: cables pass through offices that keep copies readable by whoever controls them; letters ride mail trains through jurisdictions that may open, copy and reseal them. Messages are routed, intercepted, delayed, forged — or outrun.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Composing a cable is a word-cost puzzle (commercial codes compress; private ciphers conceal but attract censors); its route chooses its readers. Letters go by cover address, poste restante or courier. A posted denunciation starts a race: beat it to the recipient, intercept it at a sorting office, or overtake it by cable. Hunters use the same wires and post; bribed clerks show them to the player, who can poison them.
- **Directive coverage:** Coding/deception primary; travel co-primary (mail moves at timetable speed; a cable beats any train, so the hunt's reach is the wire's). Items: letters, seals, code books. Economy: per-word costs; bills of exchange are mail. Politics: censorship and cable control shift with war. Hunt: cable-ahead arrests versus unpredictability.
- **Historical grounding:** Retained telegraph copies; the All Red Line and 1914 cable-cutting; travelling post offices; pneumatic post; Okhrana black cabinets; wartime censorship; wireless letting a wanted notice overtake a liner.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The controlled leak as dramatic irony; Zeigarnik pull of a message in transit; legible races on a visible network; beating the post as "one more leg".
- **Engine implications:** Messages as scheduled entities on a comms graph beside the travel graph; office/jurisdiction ownership as faction attributes; tamper state with detection; one scheduler for passengers and mail.
- **Risks / open questions:** Postal speeds near express speeds narrow races — design around margins; templates against composition tedium.

## P05.05 — The Black Chamber: Hands-on Cryptanalysis, an Abstraction Ladder and Burning the Source
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Intercepts are *real ciphertexts* (Vigenère, Playfair, columnar transposition, codebook, later ADFGVX) the player may crack by hand with in-game tools, delegate to a hired cryptanalyst (time, cost, leak risk) or abstract to a skill roll — three rungs of one ladder yielding the same intelligence object.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A frequency tally, period test and crib tool make hand-solving feasible. Captured code books are items that make a traffic class readable until its owner *rotates* the book — triggered by a hidden compromise-suspicion value that rises when the player acts too visibly on intercepts. Every use of a decrypt is a wager: exploit now and risk the source, or hold.
- **Directive coverage:** Coding primary. Items: code books, cipher slides, developer chemicals. Economy: cryptanalysts cost; code books sell for fortunes. Politics: compromise events alter faction behaviour. Travel: intercepts reveal shipments and movements worth catching a train for. Hunt: reading the hunters' traffic is the ultimate counter-surveillance — and the most dangerous to act on.
- **Historical grounding:** Playfair in the Boer War and WWI; Vigenère and transposition in commercial and consular traffic; naval code books captured in 1914; Room 40 (1914); the 1918 German field cipher; one-time pads from 1917.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Puzzle "aha" and competence; the ladder lets novices progress while experts earn faster, cheaper intelligence; the source-burning dilemma is strategic tension with delayed, legible consequence.
- **Engine implications:** String-level encryption of generated messages; generators producing plausible, solvable traffic; compromise-suspicion as per-faction hidden state driven by the observability of player actions; the abstract rung returns identical outputs.
- **Risks / open questions:** Difficulty calibration; players who skip puzzles must never be strictly worse off.

## P05.06 — The Forgery Bench: Documents as Composed Items with Inspection Depth
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every document is a field-structured item (stock, typeface, seal, signature, serial, stamps, dates) in which each field has a *quality*, and every inspector has an *inspection depth*: a gendarme glances at the seal; a consulate wires the issuing register.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Forgery is crafting from components — blank stock (stolen, bought from printers), seals (carved, cast, stolen), signatures practised from specimens, typewriters with identifiable wear, inks. The player predicts who will inspect and how deeply, spending only the quality needed. Genuine documents can instead be *altered* (photograph substitution, date erasure) with different failure modes. Documents age, collect border stamps and build a history that must match the legend carrying them.
- **Directive coverage:** Items and deception primary. Travel: visas, exit permits and passes gate the network, intensifying after 1914. Economy: letters of credit and bills of exchange are forgeable money. Politics: which documents exist depends on regime and year. Hunt: a failed document is a hard fix on name and position.
- **Historical grounding:** Pre-war passport-free Europe against Russian and Ottoman passport regimes; 1915 photographic passports; 1920 standardised booklets; typewriter typeface identification in forensic practice; the stamp-and-seal culture of continental bureaucracy.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Crafting satisfaction plus calibrated risk ("good enough for a gendarme, not a consul"); the border check as a knowable, dice-free test rewards preparation; attachment to a well-worn passport.
- **Engine implications:** Documents as typed field vectors; inspectors as depth profiles per institution and year; inspection resolves field by field deterministically, with partial feedback to the player; histories as appended stamps.
- **Risks / open questions:** Component sourcing must not become a fetch quest; the bench needs its own tempo.

## P05.07 — Whisper Networks: Rumour and Reputation Propagation on the Social Graph
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Rumours are mutable propositions travelling a social graph with per-edge delays, mutation chances and credibility weights; a rumour corroborated by two independent paths hardens into "known". Player and hunters both inject and both listen.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player seeds rumours at high-reach nodes — cafés, clubs, bourses, salons, barracks, newspaper offices (a paid journalist), personal columns. Each identity carries a *reputation vector* per faction and city, moved by hardened rumours. The player reads the network through hired listeners (porters, concierges, telegraph boys) and by scanning each day's editions. Counter-rumours, denials and plausible alternative explanations are moves. The hunters' network leaks too: a forming cordon is itself a rumour.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception/conversation primary. Politics: faction credibility weights; rumour panics move crises. Economy: reputation gates credit and commissions; planted rumours move prices. Travel: rumours cross cities at post and telegraph speed — slower than an express the player is on. Hunt: a false sighting is a seeded rumour.
- **Historical grounding:** Multi-edition daily press and news agencies; agony columns as semi-public channels; café and club society; concierge and hotel-staff informant culture; bourse panics driven by rumour.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Agency at a distance with delayed payoff — planting a rumour and watching it arrive is a Zeigarnik open loop; attribution pleasure ("I started that"); legible social cause and effect.
- **Engine implications:** Propagation at city/faction aggregate with per-NPC resolution only for hot nodes; rumours as objects with provenance chains; hardening as a two-path corroboration test; triggers on reputation thresholds.
- **Risks / open questions:** Opacity — players must see enough network to feel causal; rumour-spam exploits need damping.

## P05.08 — Spot, Assess, Develop, Recruit — and the Chair Turned Round
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Recruiting an asset and being interrogated are the *same* information game from opposite seats: one side holds a dossier of vulnerabilities and known facts; the other must infer what it contains.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Potential assets have hidden motivation profiles in period terms — debt, grievance, conviction, vanity, fear — and discoverable vulnerabilities (gambling, affairs, compromising letters). The player investigates (observation, bribed servants, intercepted mail) to fill a dossier, then pitches at a size matched to the profile; a mismatched pitch alerts the target. Recruited assets run through cover addresses and dead drops; reliability decays without attention and payment. When detained, the player faces an interrogator whose dossier is built from the hunters' evidence; question patterns hint at its contents, and the player chooses what to admit, deny or trade.
- **Directive coverage:** Conversation/deception primary. Items: compromising letters, photographs. Economy: assets cost retainers; recruited clerks yield commercial intelligence. Politics: assets inside ministries and arsenals are the main lever. Travel: handling assets across cities sets the itinerary. Hunt: interrogation is the climax, resolved by information rather than dice.
- **Historical grounding:** Okhrana and Special Branch informant networks; blackmail of indebted officers as a documented recruitment method; dead drops and cover addresses; police detention before recorded statements.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Curiosity gap ("what do they know?"); mastery of reading people as profiles; dread of an asset's decay; the symmetry teaches players to feel their own exposure.
- **Engine implications:** Motivation profiles as vectors; dossiers as belief sets; interrogation questions generated from the hunters' evidence graph; asset reliability as decaying state with scheduled check-ins.
- **Risks / open questions:** Interrogation must be survivable and interesting, not a fail state.

## P05.09 — The Double Ledger
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player works simultaneously for several intelligence services and commercial principals, each holding its own *model of the player*: what it believes the player knows, has done, delivered and owes. The core resource is the *discrepancy* between those models and the truth.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each principal issues tasks, pays and periodically *reconciles*, cross-checking product against other sources. The player delivers real intelligence, chicken-feed or fabrication, trading money and standing against future reconciliation risk. Travel between principals' capitals sets the rhythm; ciphered correspondence sustains distance. Over years, services compare notes and the accumulated discrepancy becomes the hunt: whichever service finds the gap becomes a pursuer holding everything the player ever told it as a dossier. Victory is a retirement with the ledgers never reconciled.
- **Directive coverage:** All systems primary by construction — travel (principals in different capitals), deception/coding (fabricated product; ciphered reports), items (stolen plans as product), economy (commissions, double pay, debts), politics/warfare (demand follows the world clock: war makes naval plans priceless), time pressure/hunt (reconciliation events; defection into pursuit).
- **Historical grounding:** The freelance agent-cum-commission-broker career; competing services before 1914 (Secret Service Bureau, Deuxième Bureau, Evidenzbureau, Abteilung IIIb, Okhrana); wartime hunger for naval and industrial plans.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Sustained dramatic irony — knowing more than every faction; juggling tension with relief at each passed reconciliation; long-horizon consequence: early choices matter decades later.
- **Engine implications:** Per-faction belief models of the player as first-class objects; reconciliation as scheduled events comparing deliveries; product as proposition sets with truth distance; defection converting a model into a hunter dossier.
- **Risks / open questions:** Complexity ceiling beyond three principals; legibility of what each service believes.

## P05.10 — The Bourse of Secrets
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player's business is information arbitrage. Every fact has a market price set by *freshness*, *exclusivity*, *verifiability* and *buyer need*; services, newspapers, bourses, arms firms and embassies are rival buyer classes. Lies are inventory too — and every sold item carries provenance traceable to the seller.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Acquire information (eavesdropping, intercepts, recruited clerks), *verify* it (time and cables raise its price), *package* it (document, tip, rumour) and sell to the best buyer — or hold for a better moment on the world clock: a mobilisation crisis multiplies a railway timetable's value. Selling twice breaks exclusivity; fabrications pay until a buyer verifies. Information decays: plans sold after the launch are worthless. The hunt emerges from provenance — every item leaks who could have known.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and deception/coding co-primary. Travel: price gaps between cities; what cannot be cabled must be carried. Items: documents as provenanced inventory. Politics/warfare: demand follows crises; selling can move them. Time pressure: decay and event-driven spikes. Hunt: provenance tracing; buyers turning hunters.
- **Historical grounding:** Commission agents and concession brokers trading insider knowledge; news agencies paying for scoops; rumour-driven bourses; services buying naval and industrial plans; pre-1914 competing services.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Market play with information as the good — loss aversion over decaying inventory, the thrill of a held secret spiking, dramatic irony in selling one truth to rivals; emergent stories as provenance closes in.
- **Engine implications:** Information items as propositions with timestamps, exclusivity sets, provenance chains and per-buyer utility functions; prices from world-state demand; verification as scheduled queries; hunter-side provenance tracing.
- **Risks / open questions:** Pricing legibility; preventing fabrication farming — verification must bite often enough.

## P05.11 — Borrowed Lives
- **Type:** whole-game concept (risky)
- **Central mechanical hook:** Instead of inventing legends, the player takes over the identity of a *procedurally generated real person* — recently dead, emigrated or vanished — inheriting their social graph: creditors, employers, acquaintances, relatives, and every NPC's beliefs about them.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The world generates life histories for its population. The player scouts "donors" via death notices, passenger lists and hospital records, obtains their papers, and *performs* a life never lived. Acquaintances hold memories the player must infer from letters, effects and conversation; a Call can come from anyone who knew the donor. The player harvests the donor's assets, position and access for espionage and business, choosing how long to stay: longer tenure earns deeper access while risk compounds as distant contacts arrive.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception primary. Travel: donors are found far from where they are known; acquaintances travel too. Items: the donor's papers. Economy: inherited debts and businesses. Politics: a donor with faction ties grants access. Time pressure/hunt: returning relatives; hunters seeking someone who should be dead.
- **Historical grounding:** Weak identity infrastructure before photographic passports; emigration and wartime casualty churn; death notices and passenger lists in the press; identity established by acquaintance and paper.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Role-inhabitation through inference; constant "do they know?" tension; procedural intimacy — each borrowed life is an emergent mystery the player must solve to survive.
- **Engine implications:** Generated biographies whose facts are distributed consistently across NPC belief models; relationship graphs with expectation events; discovery as inference. Heavy but reusable: the same generator powers every NPC.
- **Risks / open questions:** Believable life histories at scale; sufficient clues; keeping generated histories mechanical (facts and relations), never authored narrative.

## P05.12 — The Notebook Is the Only Record
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky)
- **Central mechanical hook:** The engine records every claim the player has made but *never shows the player that record*. The only memory aid is a notebook the player writes — a physical item that can be lost, searched, stolen, read or used as evidence.
- **How it plays (core loop):** After each exchange the player may jot entries (typed shorthand plus free notes). Carrying the notebook risks catastrophic exposure at a search; leaving it in a safe or dead drop risks forgetting; ciphering it with a real player-chosen cipher costs time to consult; burning pages trades memory for safety. Hunters who seize it gain its contents as hard evidence; a planted *false* notebook becomes a weapon. Memory lapses surface as Calls the player cannot answer.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception/coding primary. Items: the notebook and its key. Travel: luggage searches at frontiers make carrying it a routing decision. Economy: the notebook of debts and commissions is also the business record. Politics: a seized notebook exposes networks and triggers faction consequences. Hunt: the most valuable trace object in the game.
- **Historical grounding:** Seized notebooks and address books as decisive evidence in period espionage prosecutions; luggage searches at Russian and wartime frontiers; book ciphers for personal records.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Memory becomes a genuine resource; the endowment effect binds players to the object; "do I take it across?" dilemmas; player-authored artifacts gain emergent meaning.
- **Engine implications:** Nothing new for the belief layer; the notebook as a free-text item with search and seizure events and an optional real cipher transform; notes must be searchable.
- **Risks / open questions:** Punishes forgetful players — needs mitigation (P05.01's ledger as a safer, weaker alternative); accessibility.

# Round 1A Proposals — Economy, Business & Artifacts Designer (Panelist P06)

## Panelist statement
A spy in 1895–1925 is expensive: legends, households, bribes and tickets are paid for in gold-standard money that has weight, a location and a paper trail. I want a game in which the player must run a business to afford being a spy and must spy to make the business work, with every pound, mark and rouble moving through period instruments that the hunter can read. Objects should not be inventory icons but records: each carries a provenance that can enrich the player or hang them. An economy that does not itself generate time pressure and pursuit is decoration; the proposals below are built so that it does.

## P06.01 — The Double Ledger
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player runs a real commission and trading house whose books are at once economic engine, cover-identity generator and the game's most incriminating artifact. Business and espionage are one loop: trade funds and excuses travel, travel yields intelligence, intelligence yields trading advantage.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player takes orders (commodities, machinery, arms), finances them with bills of exchange, ships them and collects commissions. Each contract demands presence in a city — cover for tasks the services pay for. Every transaction writes a double-entry ledger line. The public ledger (auditable by tax offices and police) and the true ledger diverge whenever spy money enters; the player reconciles them by laundering through trade. The firm is a legal person in a chosen jurisdiction: it holds assets, can be sold and can go bankrupt while the player walks away.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy primary. Travel: contracts pull the player onto the timetable. Deception: the firm is a legend with its own paper. Items: goods, ledgers, bills. Politics: wars and tariffs change what sells where. Time: bills mature, ships sail. Hunt: auditors, creditors and counter-intelligence read the books.
- **Historical grounding:** Commission houses as the face of pre-1914 trade; discounted bills; gold-standard parities; company registration regimes; Trading with the Enemy legislation (1914).
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Two interlocking loops each relieve the other's pressure, producing constant near-solvency tension; loss aversion over an enterprise the player built; mastery of legible accounting; the dread-pleasure of knowing one seized ledger ends everything.
- **Engine implications:** Transaction log as first-class data; two ledger views from one event stream; audit AI querying the log; jurisdiction-tagged company entity; contracts as scheduled multi-step dependencies.
- **Risks / open questions:** Accounting must be abstracted enough to stay fun; onboarding complexity.
## P06.02 — Money Has a Body
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Wealth is not a number but a portfolio of period instruments — gold coin, banknotes, letters of credit, circular notes, bills of exchange, telegraphic transfers, bearer bonds, pawn tickets — each with weight, geography, liquidity, identity-binding and traceability.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player composes a purse per journey. Gold is universal and anonymous but heavy and seizable. A letter of credit is safe but redeemable only at named correspondent banks, in banking hours, by the identity whose signature specimen (the letter of indication) it carries. A bill of exchange matures on a date: a deadline. A telegraphic transfer is instant but leaves a copy at the cable office. Bearer bonds and hallmarked jewellery cross frontiers nameless. Pawnshops give cash at a loss and leave a ticket — an artifact.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy primary. Travel: what you can carry, where you can draw. Deception: identity-bound money forces that legend to sign in person. Items: instruments are items. Politics: wartime exchange controls and gold-export bans rewrite the rules. Time: maturities, banking hours, mail delays. Hunt: the money trail — bank drawings, cable transfers, serial numbers.
- **Historical grounding:** Classical gold standard (£1 ≈ 25.2 F ≈ 20.4 M ≈ 9.5 R ≈ $4.87); letters of credit and indication; circular notes (from 1874); banknote serials; 1914 suspension of convertibility; wartime gold-export restrictions.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The purse is a packing puzzle (planning pleasure); endowment effect over gold physically carried; dread of being cashless abroad; satisfaction of a clean, untraceable payment.
- **Engine implications:** Instrument entity with location, owner-identity, redemption rules and trace flag; bank hours on the world clock; hunter queries over trace events.
- **Risks / open questions:** Fiddliness; needs strong defaults and a "banker" automation.
## P06.03 — Provenance: Every Object Remembers
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Each significant item carries an append-only chain of custody: issued, bought, stolen, copied, forged, carried through a frontier at a time, pawned, planted. Hunters, buyers and courts read it; the player can launder, forge or sever it, at a cost and leaving its own trace.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Naval plans have a registry number; a rifle a serial and proof marks; a passport an issuing office and stamps; a banknote a serial; a camera's film must be developed somewhere. When the player sells, plants or loses an item, the hunter AI gains its record up to recovery and infers the path: hotels registered, frontiers crossed, banks visited. Laundering means reselling through intermediaries, restriking hallmarks, retyping documents on another machine (typeface identification exists), or destroying — which forfeits an asset and may leave ash, witnesses or a missing-item alarm.
- **Directive coverage:** Items primary. Hunt: provenance is the hunter's main evidence stream. Travel: frontier stamps and hotel registers append to provenance. Deception: forgery is provenance editing. Economy: laundering costs; clean goods fetch more. Politics: contraband definitions change by regime and war. Time: items run "hot" then cool.
- **Historical grounding:** Hallmarks and assay offices; rifle serials; Bertillon and fingerprint practice; typewriter forensics; hotel police registers; customs stamps; telegraph companies retaining cable copies.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Information-gap curiosity (what does the hunter know?); dramatic irony; the thrill of a perfect laundering; regret when a careless sale resurfaces months later as a knock on the door: persistent consequence.
- **Engine implications:** Item event log with timestamps and location; hunter deduction as log queries; scalability by tiering (only significant items logged, commodities aggregated).
- **Risks / open questions:** Over-punishing; the player must be able to inspect their own exposure.
## P06.04 — Six Verbs for Every Artifact
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every artifact affords sell, trade, plant, leverage, use and destroy, plus "register" for technical secrets (file as a foreign patent). The market for secrets prices them by scarcity, verifiability and decay.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A stolen blueprint can be sold to one service for cash; sold again as a copy to a second (each copy lowers the price and raises exposure, because copies carry distinguishing marks); planted in a rival's luggage before a frontier; held as leverage over the official who lost it; used to inform a business bid; registered as a patent in another jurisdiction to turn theft into royalties (a public filing, hence traceable); or burned to end a hunt. Secrets decay: plans for a ship are worthless after launch, a mobilisation table after mobilisation. Prices come from per-faction demand curves modulated by the current crisis level.
- **Directive coverage:** Items and economy primary. Deception: planting, forging copies. Politics: demand follows the crisis state; war inflates both prices and penalties. Travel: buyers sit in specific cities, so decay makes a race on the timetable. Hunt: every verb but destroy leaves a provenance trace.
- **Historical grounding:** Naval plans and mobilisation schedules as the age's prize documents; national patent systems under the Paris Convention; commercial codebooks; services with small budgets paying in favours and passports as much as cash.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Optionality is intrinsically rewarding; double-selling is push-your-luck with variable-ratio payoff; decay timers produce goal-gradient urgency; a clever plant delivers the con-artist's superiority high.
- **Engine implications:** Affordance table per item class; demand model per faction; decay functions bound to world-clock events; copy lineage recorded in the provenance log.
- **Risks / open questions:** Dominant strategies (always double-sell) unless exposure mathematics bites.

## P06.05 — The Commission Agent
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player brokers arms and industrial contracts between manufacturers and governments or insurgents, living on commission, with espionage as the means of learning what rivals bid and what buyers secretly want.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A deal is a months-long state machine: identify a buyer (ministry, navy, insurgency), learn its specification and budget by conversation, bribery or theft, align a manufacturer, outbid rivals (or sabotage their bid by selling evidence of it to the buyer's counter-intelligence), secure the contract, finance delivery, ship the goods, collect commission on delivery. World-clock deadlines govern each stage: a crisis makes buyers desperate; a peace collapses prices; an embargo makes a delivery criminal overnight. Services also pay for bidder intelligence, so the same information sells twice — to the buyer and to a foreign service — at the risk of being seen as both dealer and spy.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and politics primary. Travel: factories, ministries and ports are far apart; deals require presence. Deception: dual-selling; fronting for a manufacturer under a legend. Items: sample weapons, contracts, bills of lading. Time: crisis windows, delivery dates. Hunt: rival agents, customs, counter-intelligence tracing shipments.
- **Historical grounding:** Vickers, Krupp, Schneider and Škoda and their commission agents; the naval race; Balkan arms purchases before 1912; Irish gun-running 1914; wartime contraband lists.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Long-horizon anticipation (large payoffs months away) interleaved with short-horizon tension; rivalry against AI agents; the Zeigarnik pull of a deal left half-closed at session end.
- **Engine implications:** Contract state machines dependent on world-clock events; rival agent AI; freight routing; demand per faction.
- **Risks / open questions:** Could collapse into spreadsheet management; each stage needs a field action.
## P06.06 — The Concession Hunter
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** Concessions — railway, oil, mining, timber and telegraph rights granted by weak or venal states — are the great assets: won by access and bribery, valued by political forecasting, flipped to syndicates on the bourses, and voided by the revolutions and wars the world clock delivers regardless.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each concession is a state machine (rumoured → petitioned → granted → financed → operating → disputed → voided) inside a granting polity whose stability is simulated. The player travels to the capital, cultivates and bribes, assembles capital from banks and syndicates by promising shares, then decides: hold for revenue, or sell while the political-risk premium is low. Espionage reveals what the rival bidder offered and what the ministry's confidential stability report says. A concession's paper value moves daily with news on the bourse, so a well-timed intercepted cable is worth more than the mine.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and politics primary. Travel: capitals at the edges of the rail network. Deception: legends as engineers or financiers. Items: the deed, maps, survey reports. Time: tender deadlines, revolution clocks. Hunt: a rival syndicate's agents and the host state's police.
- **Historical grounding:** Persian oil concession 1901 (oil struck 1908); Baghdad Railway 1903; Manchurian and Korean timber and railway concessions preceding 1904; Siberian goldfields; the Ottoman Public Debt Administration; 1917 expropriation.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Speculating on a hidden political model is a forecasting game with rich feedback; large swings exploit loss aversion and sunk cost; "sell now or hold" recurs as an agonising, meaningful choice.
- **Engine implications:** Polity stability model emitting events; asset valuation function; syndicate AI; bourse ticker fed by the news system.
- **Risks / open questions:** Abstract; needs tactile stakes in the field.

## P06.07 — Legends Have Burn Rates
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every cover identity has a lifestyle cost that must be paid to remain credible. An underfunded legend decays in credibility and attracts attention; an overfunded one attracts thieves and envy.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each legend has a social class with a monthly burn (club subscriptions, hotel grade, tailoring, servants, carriage, gifts) and a wardrobe inventory. Households in several cities each cost rent and staff and each offer a safe house, a mail drop and a cache. The player decides which legends are live (paid up, resident) and which dormant (cheap, but reactivation takes time and generates registration events). Gambling is both class behaviour and a liquidity option with variance; gambling debts are debts of honour, unenforceable at law but enforced socially by club expulsion, which kills a legend.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and life-sim primary. Deception: credibility is a function of spend matching class. Travel: ticket class must match the legend. Items: wardrobe, cards, club tokens. Politics: wartime scarcity raises burn rates; a revolution destroys a household. Time: monthly debits on the world clock. Hunt: unpaid tradesmen talk; hotel registers record who paid, and how.
- **Historical grounding:** Club culture and blackballing; visiting cards and letters of introduction; the Gaming Act 1845 (gambling debts unenforceable); Monte Carlo and racecourse betting; hotel police registration.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Several burning fuses create plate-spinning tension; income spikes relieve it, then obligations accrue again — a clean tension/relief cycle; maintaining legends breeds attachment to identities the player built.
- **Engine implications:** Recurring debit events; credibility stat per legend driven by spend versus class; household entities with locations and caches.
- **Risks / open questions:** Must not become chores; aggregate the bookkeeping.

## P06.08 — Creditors Are the Second Hunter
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Debt is a pursuit system running parallel to counter-intelligence. Every creditor — bank, moneylender, tailor, casino, or an intelligence service that advanced funds — has patience, information and an enforcement method. Default escalates to bailiffs, protest of bills and bankruptcy, which the state publishes.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player borrows to bridge deals and lifestyles. A bank discounts bills and expects payment at maturity; a protested bill is a public act; a London Gazette bankruptcy notice prints the name — a legend's name — exposing it to every hunter who reads the papers. A service that paid an advance calls the loan as a demanded mission at the worst moment. Creditors share information across cities slowly (by letter) or quickly (by cable), so fleeing a jurisdiction buys time, not freedom. Pawning artifacts raises cash but puts incriminating items into the market with the player's ticket on them.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy primary. Hunt: creditors are a second, legible pursuer. Travel: jurisdiction-hopping against information lag. Deception: debts attach to identities, so a burned identity's debts die with it, tempting deliberate fraud. Items: collateral, pawn tickets. Politics: wartime moratoria; revolutionary repudiation.
- **Historical grounding:** Notarial protest of bills; London Gazette notices; Bankruptcy Acts 1883 and 1914; the German Konkursordnung; Money-lenders Act 1900; the August 1914 moratorium.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Hyperbolic discounting makes borrowing seductive and the arriving bill dreadful; a legible countdown with escalating stakes; letting an identity die with its debts is a moral-economic choice with lasting consequences.
- **Engine implications:** Creditor agents with patience timers and information propagation across cities; identity-scoped liabilities; publication events that feed the hunter.
- **Risks / open questions:** Death spirals; insolvency must be a playable reset, not a game over.

## P06.09 — Bribery with Hidden Reservation Prices
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every official has a hidden price and a preferred form of payment. Bribery is price discovery under risk, and every paid bribe is both a relationship and a liability.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Approaching a customs officer, clerk, stationmaster, police registrar or minister, the player chooses channel (direct, intermediary, contract "commission", gift, a post for a relative), amount and timing. Too low insults; too high signals desperation and invites reporting or escalation; the wrong form fails — a clerk needs cash, a governor a decoration, a chinovnik the appearance of legality. Success yields a service (a stamp, a look at a register, a delayed arrest) and a corrupted contact with a recurring price, who may later be flipped by the hunter or, dismissed, become the one who talks. Prices are indexed to regime: baksheesh economies versus Prussian rigidity; wartime desperation lowers prices but raises penalties.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and deception primary. Travel: frontiers and stations are bribe points. Politics: corruption is a property of polity and era. Items: gifts and receipts — some bribes leave paper. Time: urgent bribes cost more. Hunt: bribe-takers are the hunter's route into the player's network.
- **Historical grounding:** Ottoman and Russian officialdom; arms-contract "commissions"; hotel register inspections; the Prevention of Corruption Act 1906 as a counter-tool.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Negotiating against a hidden value is a read-the-table skill with instant feedback; a network of corrupted contacts feels like a growing power base and, equally, a growing exposure.
- **Engine implications:** Official entities with reservation price, form preference and loyalty decay; corruption indices per polity and year.
- **Risks / open questions:** Avoid "always pay" dominance; detection risk must scale with frequency.
## P06.10 — Arbitrage on the Timetable
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** A city-level price system for goods and securities, with tariffs, luggage limits and telegraph-speed information, lets the traveller profit from movement — and turns an intercepted cable into a trading edge.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each city holds prices for tradable classes (caviar, carpets, furs, machine parts, securities). Differentials arise from distance, tariff walls (free-trade Britain versus protective Germany, France, Russia), season and crisis. The player buys within luggage weight and customs risk and sells on arrival — the 80 Days trade loop made real. The deeper layer: news moves prices and travels by cable, so a player who reads a telegram before the bourse (via a tapped cable office or a bought clerk) trades ahead of it: shorting Russian bonds before a known revolution, buying marine insurance ahead of a known submarine order. Wartime adds contraband lists, blockade and neutral transshipment via the Netherlands and Scandinavia.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and travel primary. Deception: customs declarations, smuggling compartments. Items: goods with weight and provenance. Politics: tariffs, wars and blockades reshape the price field. Time: information lag is the margin. Hunt: customs searches; a too-lucky trader gets investigated.
- **Historical grounding:** Méline (1892) and Bülow (1902) tariffs; Russian 1891 tariff; British free trade to 1915; retained cable copies; Paris bourse's role in Russian debt; Netherlands Overseas Trust (1914).
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Insider trading is the purest "I know something you don't" fantasy; frequent small trades give variable-ratio reinforcement; the luggage puzzle is tactile.
- **Engine implications:** Price vectors per city with mean reversion plus event shocks; information propagation modelled separately from the event itself (an event and its news are two things).
- **Risks / open questions:** Exploitability; must not eclipse espionage as income.
## P06.11 — The Thirty-Year Storm (Currency Regimes)
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** Across 1895–1925 money itself changes nature: fixed gold parities, then suspension, exchange controls, confiscation and hyperinflation. Stored wealth is only as safe as the player's forecast of the political clock.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Before 1914 the player holds any major currency carelessly. From 1914 the world clock introduces per-country regimes: convertibility suspended, gold exports banned, moratoria, controls on cash at exits. 1917 Russia: banks nationalised, safes opened, debts repudiated — every rouble asset, bond and household evaporates unless moved in time. 1922–23: marks and crowns run to trillions; wages must be spent the hour they are paid; hard assets, foreign currency and gold rule. A player who reads the macro-events, and whose espionage supplies early warning, converts in time; one who does not watches a fortune die. Hoarding gold is itself illegal in several regimes, so the prudent player becomes a smuggler.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and politics primary. Time: regime changes are dated events; intelligence is the counter. Travel: moving wealth is physical movement across frontiers. Deception: hidden compartments, false declarations. Items: gold, jewellery, bearer bonds. Hunt: currency police and customs; wartime censors open letters that contain drafts.
- **Historical grounding:** 1914 suspension; Soviet decrees on banks and debts 1917–18; Austrian and German hyperinflation; Rentenmark 1923; British return to gold 1925.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** A looming, known catastrophe is slow-motion loss aversion; successful foresight is deeply satisfying; high replay value as players try different hedges against the same clock.
- **Engine implications:** Per-currency regime state machines; asset classes with regime-dependent liquidity; early-warning events tied to intelligence actions.
- **Risks / open questions:** Inevitable disasters can feel unfair without strong foreshadowing; largely a late-game system.

## P06.12 — Freight Shadow: Your Cargo Travels Too
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** Contraband and trade goods move on the same network as the player but on freight schedules, with bills of lading, manifests and transshipment — a second hidden-movement game in which the hunter pursues the crate.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player consigns a crate of rifles, a case of plates or a trunk of banknotes under a false manifest. It moves at freight speed (slow versus fast goods classes), stops at customs, and is inspected with a probability shaped by the manifest's plausibility, bribes paid and the current contraband regime. The player can accompany it, meet it at transshipment, or let it run. The hunter sees waybills and customs records — trace evidence of mode, like Scotland Yard's transport tokens — and may seize it or follow it to the consignee, who may be the player's legend. Seized cargo enters the hunter's provenance record.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and items primary. Economy: freight rates, insurance, demurrage. Deception: false manifests, straw consignees. Politics: contraband lists, embargoes, wartime requisition. Time: freight timetables, port demurrage. Hunt: the crate is a second fugitive.
- **Historical grounding:** Grande vitesse and petite vitesse goods classes; bills of lading; Lloyd's marine insurance; transshipment ports (Hamburg, Antwerp, Trieste, Genoa); customs at gauge-break frontiers; 1914 gun-running.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Doubling the hidden-movement puzzle creates split-attention tension; "will the crate make it" is a cliffhanger on a real timetable; a successful rendezvous is a planning triumph.
- **Engine implications:** Freight layer over the passenger graph; cargo entities with manifest and inspection logic; hunter can target cargo as well as persons.
- **Risks / open questions:** Scope; a few key routes may be enough to deliver the fantasy.

# Round 1A Proposals — Politics & Warfare Designer (Panelist P07)

## Panelist statement
My lens is the macro-world as a machine the player can touch but never steer: intelligence services, ministries, armies and revolutionary movements as factions with budgets, agendas and beliefs; wars and crises as scheduled processes running on real timetables; and dependency chains that carry one agent's small acts into geopolitics and back into his own life. The game must get one paradox right: the Great War begins in August 1914 whatever the player does, yet everything at the player's scale — who wins the contract, which service takes the credit, whose file is opened — is genuinely his to perturb. If both halves hold, foreknowledge becomes a resource, every faction becomes a market, and the hunt becomes a political process rather than a chase.

## P07.01 — The Many-Mastered Agent
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player is a freelance asset on the books of several intelligence services and ministries at once, each a faction with a budget, standing requirements and a *belief state* about him — what it thinks he knows and whom he serves. The game is managing those beliefs for twenty-five years.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Services post requirements drawn from current worries. The player accepts tasks, travels to fulfil them, decides which service gets which fragment and what "chickenfeed" the others get, and watches per-service trust and suspicion gauges. Services cross-check: a report too good, or surfacing in a rival's hands, moves suspicion. When a belief flips to "double", the service stops paying and starts hunting — or runs him against his other patrons.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics primary. Travel: requirements are geographic; clashing deadlines force route choices. Deception/coding: one cipher and one legend per service. Items: documents with provenance. Economy: retainers, bonuses, audited expenses. Time pressure: requirements expire with crises. Hunt: counter-intelligence sections pursue, funded by the player's successes.
- **Historical grounding:** Commission agents serving several governments; Secret Service Bureau (1909), Deuxième Bureau, Evidenzbureau, Abteilung IIIb, Okhrana foreign bureau; annual secret-service votes.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Plate-spinning tension — concurrent commitments whose collisions are foreseeable but never fully preventable; dramatic irony (the player knows more than any faction); variable-ratio payoffs when a fragment sold twice pays twice; dread-and-relief at every cross-check.
- **Engine implications:** Per-faction belief models (tagged fact sets, suspicion scalars, attributed sources); requirement generators fed by the world clock; leak propagation along alliance edges; yearly budgets scaling task value and hunter capacity.
- **Risks / open questions:** Legibility of belief states without exposing them; cross-checks must bite so "sell everything to everyone" never dominates.
## P07.02 — Tramlines and Switchpoints: Three-Tier Bounded History
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every world fact is tagged Tier 0 (immovable: war dates, revolutions, treaties), Tier 1 (bounded within plausible windows: who wins the 1906 Serbian artillery contract, which firm finances a railway section) or Tier 2 (free: arrests, exposures, prices, credit). Actions push *pressure accumulators* on Tier 1 and 2 variables; saturation guarantees no pressure moves a Tier 0 rail.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player never sees tiers, only a world that answers him at human scale and stays textbook-accurate over decades. Stealing a battleship's gunnery tables changes which yard gets the follow-on order and which service gets the budget — never whether the naval race happens.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics primary. Travel: through-service inauguration days jitter within their season (Tier 1); the 1914 closures are Tier 0. Deception: exposures are Tier 2, propagating upward as pressure. Items: documents carry *lever weight* against Tier 1 variables. Economy: contract outcomes are Tier 1. Hunt: hunter budgets are Tier 1 outputs of Tier 2 events. Time pressure: windows close.
- **Historical grounding:** The 1906 Serbian artillery contract (Schneider over Škoda) and the ensuing Austro-Serbian customs war; section-by-section financing of the Baghdad railway; naval orders after the 1906 Dreadnought.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** "I mattered, and the world is still real" — agency with credibility; detective pleasure in tracing cause through the dependency graph; a bounded sandbox invites experimentation without breaking the period contract.
- **Engine implications:** Tiered variable schema; pressure accumulators with decay and saturation; weighted dependency edges; a scheduler reconciling Tier 1 windows with the Tier 0 clock. A new decade adds rows, not code.
- **Risks / open questions:** Players testing the rails may feel the designer's hand; saturation must present as institutional inertia, not a wall.
## P07.03 — Crisis Ladders
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Diplomatic crises (Fashoda 1898, Morocco 1905 and 1911, Bosnia 1908–09, July 1914) run as explicit state machines — note, counter-note, ultimatum with a literal deadline, partial mobilisation, general mobilisation — each rung altering the world's parameters for its duration, some permanently.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Days before the first rung the player reads price twitches, cable volume and cancelled leave; during the ladder, borders tighten, trains are requisitioned, bourses panic, services open emergency funds, hunters gain emergency powers. The player runs toward the crisis (best pay, most exposure) or away (asset preservation). Some rungs leave *ratchets*: laws and registrations that never revert.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics and time pressure primary. Travel: the timetable degrades rung by rung; at general mobilisation the military schedule overwrites the civilian one. Deception: emergencies check papers never checked before. Items: ultimatum texts and cipher cables are the week's prizes. Economy: short-dated plays in gold, insurance, shipping. Hunt: emergency powers raise the hunters' reveal cadence.
- **Historical grounding:** The 48-hour Austrian ultimatum of 23 July 1914; the 12-hour German ultimatum to Russia of 31 July; the Official Secrets Act 1911 passed in an afternoon during Agadir; Aliens Restriction Act of 5 August 1914; mobilisation plans as railway timetables.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Countdown dread and relief; scarcity windows creating diegetic appointments ("be in Vienna by Thursday"); ratchets reward learning the diplomatic calendar as mastery; because the ladder cannot be stopped, tension is pure.
- **Engine implications:** Ladders as data (rungs, durations, Tier 1 jitter, parameter deltas); global modifiers per rung; permanent ratchets; hooks into travel graph, price model and hunter rulesets.
- **Risks / open questions:** Too many crises numb; density must follow the real calendar, long calms included.
## P07.04 — War as Weather
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Wars are long-running world regimes, not events. When one runs (Boer, Russo-Japanese, Balkan, Great), a layered rule overlay changes travel, identity, money, communication and hunting across whole regions; fronts move on the historical schedule; neutral capitals become the game's bazaars.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player prepares before the switch — relocating households, opening neutral accounts, acquiring a neutral identity — then lives inside it. Contraband lists make trade illegal and lucrative; censorship forces coded correspondence; enemy-alien status threatens the internment of an identity's household; a cover identity of military age receives call-up papers; exit permits become mandatory. Neutral hubs (Bern, Rotterdam, Copenhagen, Stockholm, Madrid) concentrate every service's buyers in a few hotels.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics/warfare primary. Travel: requisition, blockade zones, submarine risk on sea legs. Deception: an identity's nationality becomes life-or-death. Items: contraband, passes, permits. Economy: profiteering versus expropriation and exchange controls. Time pressure: fronts move whether or not you are there. Hunt: states of siege, alien registration, censors reading your post.
- **Historical grounding:** Britain cut Germany's Atlantic cables on 5 August 1914; Defence of the Realm Act; enemy-alien internment; navicerts (1916); the Bolshevik publication of the secret treaties (November 1917).
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The pleasure of preparation meeting contact — plans survive or shatter; regime change renews a world the player had mastered (optimal-challenge reset); neutral hubs are dense social-deduction arenas.
- **Engine implications:** Regime overlays as modifier stacks on graph edges, prices, legal rules and faction powers; front lines as time-indexed polygons; identity attributes (nationality, age) checked against regime rules.
- **Risks / open questions:** Four years is a long regime; it needs internal phases (1914 mobility, 1915–16 stasis, 1917 Russian collapse, 1918 fluidity).
## P07.05 — The Requirements Market: Secrets Have Expiry Dates
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every stolen document, intercepted cable or observed fact has a value curve over time for each faction — rising as the related crisis approaches, collapsing when superseded or published — and a holding risk, because possession is evidence. The player trades on timing.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A naval construction programme is worth little in 1905, a fortune during the 1909 scare, nothing once the estimates are tabled. The player sells now, holds for the spike, splits copies across buyers, or burns. Rival agents race the same curves: a document becomes worthless the moment another seller delivers. Holding means concealment compartments, dead drops and sweating through customs stops.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and politics primary. Items: documents with provenance, copies, forgeries that pass until cross-checked. Travel: deliver before the window closes, across borders that may now demand passports. Deception: selling the same secret twice under different legends. Time pressure: explicit value curves. Hunt: holding time multiplies exposure.
- **Historical grounding:** The 1913 compromise of Austro-Hungarian deployment plans; mobilisation schedules as the prize document of the age; the 1909 naval scare; the Zimmermann telegram, whose publication destroyed its secret value while creating political value.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Timing games engage loss aversion and regret aversion at once ("sold too early", "held too long"); watching curves gives a reason to return daily; forecasting pleasure in reading the diplomatic calendar correctly.
- **Engine implications:** Value functions per item class per faction, parameterised by distance to scheduled events and by supersession events; a daily market-clearing step; forgery-detection probability by buyer competence.
- **Risks / open questions:** Must not collapse into spreadsheet play; the physical act of holding and moving must stay dangerous.

## P07.06 — Your File Travels with the Alliances
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky)
- **Central mechanical hook:** Each service keeps a dossier on each of the player's identities. Dossiers are copied or traded — partially, with delays — when states align (Entente 1904, Convention 1907, war coalitions), inherited by revolutionary successors (Cheka from Okhrana, 1917), and lost when regimes fall. The diplomatic calendar therefore decides who will know what about you, and when.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player watches approaching realignments: a legend burned in Paris is safe in St Petersburg until 1907, then not. He can race the merger — break in and thin the file, make himself indispensable to the receiving service before it reads him, or retire the identity. Successor states inherit incomplete files, opening windows of invisibility after collapses.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and politics primary. Deception: identity hygiene across alliance blocs. Travel: where you can still go is a function of where your file has not yet arrived. Items: the file as a stealable artifact (photographs, Bertillon cards). Economy: buying archivists. Time pressure: merger dates.
- **Historical grounding:** Okhrana Paris bureau cooperation with the French police; Anglo-Russian Convention 1907; wartime Allied intelligence exchange; the partial destruction and inheritance of Okhrana archives in 1917; Bertillonage and fingerprint records.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Foreseeable dread — the player knows the date his past arrives and must act first; Zeigarnik tension around unfinished identity clean-ups; catharsis when a collapse wipes a slate.
- **Engine implications:** Dossier as faction-held fact sets with provenance and confidence; alliance graph whose edge events copy facts; regime-fall events that delete or mutate stores; hunter AI reads only its own dossier.
- **Risks / open questions:** Legibility: the player needs some way to learn roughly what a service holds, perhaps only by taking risks.

## P07.07 — Doomed Projects
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player may join or sponsor political projects — coups, counter-revolutions, separatist risings, concession wars — modelled as multi-stage ventures with resources, timetables and hidden feasibility. Some are Tier 0 doomed: history forbids their success. They can still be played to the hilt, because the game scores *extraction*, not victory: get money, people and reputation out before the collapse.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A project needs funds, arms, officers' loyalty, foreign backing and timing. Each stage consumes travel, bribes and secrecy; each leak raises the counter-intelligence tempo. Feasibility is read from intelligence, never shown. At collapse the project becomes a pursuit: evacuate by scheduled routes while the new regime's service hunts.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics/warfare primary. Hunt: collapse is the Scotland Yard phase. Travel: evacuation under closing frontiers. Deception: false flags; who really funds whom. Items: arms consignments, gold. Economy: project finance as venture capital paying only in Tier 1 and 2 successes. Time pressure: the project's clock races the world's.
- **Historical grounding:** Anti-Bolshevik conspiracies sponsored by Allied missions in 1918; the Russian Civil War; émigré counter-revolution to 1925; the 1912 US arms embargo on Mexico and the gun-running it bred.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Commitment escalation under uncertainty, redeemed by mastery of exit timing; the gambler's "one more stage"; emergent tragedy from systems rather than scripts — the player's own venture becomes the story he tells afterwards.
- **Engine implications:** Project templates as stage graphs with resource gates; feasibility values hidden behind intelligence quality; collapse triggers that spawn pursuit states and city regime changes.
- **Risks / open questions:** Players may resent guaranteed failure if they detect it; extraction must read as real victory, and real Tier 1 wins must exist elsewhere.
## P07.08 — Betting on the Calendar
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky but potentially brilliant)
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player's historical foreknowledge is a resource with a price. He can act on 1914 before anyone else — but the factions model *prescience* as evidence. Being right too often, too early, about too much marks him as an insider, a plotter or a provocateur, and the hunters recalibrate.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player quietly shorts shipping, buys armament shares and moves households to Switzerland in spring 1914. Every prescient act adds to a suspicion-of-foreknowledge score read by services and police; thresholds trigger investigation, frozen accounts or a recruitment approach ("you knew — who told you?"). Tier 1 jitter keeps textbook knowledge approximate: the player knows the war is coming, not the day a given frontier closes, so bets remain bets.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and hunt primary, politics as the clock. Travel: pre-positioning before closures. Deception: laundering prescience through plausible cover reasons — a business pretext for being in Zurich. Items: gold and neutral papers bought early. Time pressure: a window before each macro-event.
- **Historical grounding:** Bourse panics and bank runs of late July 1914; moratoria and exchange controls; prewar spy fever; wartime trading-with-the-enemy prosecutions.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Forecasting pleasure (being right) tempered by risk — a built-in temptation loop; the inversion that the player's meta-knowledge becomes diegetic danger; replay value as players refine how much to exploit and how to disguise it.
- **Engine implications:** Per-faction prescience accumulator keyed on actions correlated with upcoming Tier 0 events; cover-reason tags that discount suspicion; jittered Tier 1 windows.
- **Risks / open questions:** Players without historical knowledge act on in-world signals and must not be disadvantaged; threshold tuning is delicate.

## P07.09 — Influence Chains
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Political effects are achieved only through chains of cultivated nodes — generic role-holders such as a contracts clerk, a staff officer's orderly, a bourse jobber, an agency correspondent — each with access, loyalty vector, upkeep and exposure. A desired effect (swing a contract, kill a story, delay a visa) resolves as a path search through the network; every hop costs time, money and risk.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player builds a network across cities while rival services prune it through arrests, transfers and turning. Nodes are activated by meetings, requiring travel, or by coded correspondence, slower and interceptable. Hunters read activations as trace: a cluster of meetings in Trieste is a reveal.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics primary. Travel: nodes are physical, so reach is bounded by the timetable. Deception/coding: cover addresses and a cipher per node. Items: letters of introduction and gifts as access keys. Economy: the network is a cost centre that must earn its retainers. Time pressure: effects must land before decisions are taken. Hunt: activation as footprint.
- **Historical grounding:** Letters of introduction and visiting cards as the access regime; commission agents' networks inside war and marine ministries; news-agency correspondents as information nodes; cover addresses and poste restante.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Ownership and the IKEA effect — a network built over years is beloved; loss aversion when a node is burned; the click of a long chain resolving; nurturing play between crises.
- **Engine implications:** Graph of role-nodes with attributes; path-cost resolution against desired effects on Tier 1 and 2 variables; rival pruning as scheduled faction actions; activation events feeding hunter inference.
- **Risks / open questions:** Nodes must remain roles with numbers, not characters; per-node content stays procedural.
## P07.10 — The Concession Hunter
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** A career built on commercial diplomacy in the semi-sovereign lands (Ottoman, Persian, Manchurian, Chinese) where every railway, oil or mining concession needs a grantor, a syndicate, a Great Power sponsor and a defeated rival — and where espionage is the tool, not the job.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player identifies a concession in play, travels to the grantor's capital and assembles a chain (introduction → court faction → finance house → embassy backing) while rival Powers' agents do the same. He steals the rival bid, bribes the clerk who times the decree, plants a story about the rival's insolvency. Payoff is commission plus a syndicate seat — long-dated income that becomes political exposure when the Powers fall out. Concessions are Tier 1: holders vary; that oil is struck in Persia in 1908 does not.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and politics co-primary. Travel: long legs by Trans-Siberian, Suez and the Baghdad railway's advancing railhead. Deception: bidding under cover, forging telegraphic authority. Items: decrees, firmans, share certificates. Time pressure: decrees expire, rival ultimatums land. Hunt: the embarrassed Power's service pursues the agent responsible.
- **Historical grounding:** Baghdad railway concession (1903); Persian oil concession (1901) and the Anglo-Russian spheres of 1907; Yalu timber and Manchurian railway concessions feeding the Russo-Japanese War; Ottoman Public Debt Administration as gatekeeper.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Deal-making highs on an intermittent schedule; long-horizon payoffs persisting as income; success itself breeds tension because the syndicate becomes a target.
- **Engine implications:** Concession objects with multi-party state; sponsor and rival faction AI that bids; income streams and expropriation events tied to regime changes.
- **Risks / open questions:** Pacing may be slow between deals; the hunt layer must keep sessions tense.
## P07.11 — Pressure Systems: Cities with a Regime State
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every city carries regime-state variables — police effectiveness, strike pressure, arms availability, émigré funding, curfew — driven toward the scheduled revolutions (Tier 0) but perturbable by the player (Tier 1 and 2). They are the interface between politics and everything else: papers checked, prices, trains running, who hunts you.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player runs rifles to a restive port by steamer, funds an émigré press elsewhere, sells a cell to a third city's police; each act moves dials and earns from a faction. As a scheduled revolution nears, dials converge to history, but the player decides which quarters fall first, how long the general strike halts the railways, whose arms depot is seized.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics primary. Travel: strikes and curfews as dynamic edge closures (the October 1905 railway strike closed a nation). Deception: legends judged against regime state — a bourgeois cover in a soviet-held city is a liability. Items: rifles, presses, the right currency. Economy: black markets scale with stress. Time pressure: revolutions on schedule. Hunt: police effectiveness is literally the hunter's reveal cadence.
- **Historical grounding:** 1905 general strike and Moscow rising; Young Turk Revolution 1908; Mexican Revolution and the 1912 US arms embargo; Petrograd 1917; Irish War of Independence.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Dynamic-system play — small inputs, visible local effects; presence at history with the player's fingerprints on its texture; difficulty that breathes with the world rather than a slider.
- **Engine implications:** Per-city variable vectors with convergence functions toward scheduled states; hooks into travel edges, prices, hunter rulesets and identity checks; faction actions as pressure inputs.
- **Risks / open questions:** Convergence must not feel like a leash; differentiated city trajectories are where the design earns its keep.
## P07.12 — The Scare Economy: Press, Opinion, Credit and Blame
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Public opinion and bureaucratic attribution are the conduits through which the player's small acts reach budgets and laws. A planted story raises a *scare* variable (naval, spy, invasion) pressuring ministries' estimates; every political outcome distributes credit and blame tokens among services — credit buys budget, blame buys inquiries and purges.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player leaks to an agency correspondent, times the story to a budget debate, profits from armament shares, collects a bonus from the service that gets the credit — or engineers blame onto the rival service hunting him. Public credit builds reputation (letters open, fees rise) but fattens his dossier; anonymous credit is safer and poorer.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics primary. Economy: scares move shares and orders. Deception: forged provenance. Travel: editions are city-timed; a story must reach the right capital before the evening edition. Items: the planted document. Time pressure: debate dates, estimates seasons. Hunt: blame sharpens a service's zeal; reputation raises visibility.
- **Historical grounding:** The 1909 naval scare driving dreadnought estimates; spy fever preceding the Secret Service Bureau (1909); multi-edition mass dailies; the Admiralty, War Office and Press Committee (1912) as censorship ratchet; Reuters, Havas and Wolff as the wire.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Visible macro feedback from micro acts; status set against safety, so the fame-versus-anonymity dilemma recurs rather than resolving once; schadenfreude when a hunting service is defunded.
- **Engine implications:** Scare variables per state and topic with decay; attribution ledger keyed to outcomes and visible actors; budget functions reading credit; press as propagation medium with edition timing.
- **Risks / open questions:** Must stay within Tier 1 bounds (scares cannot build fleets that never existed); the press must not become a cheap win-button.

# Round 1A Proposals — Pursuit & Hidden-Information Designer (Panelist P08)

## Panelist statement
I design the hunt. The game's promise is that for forty hours the player is somebody's quarry, and that this never degenerates into either an invisible random death or a nagging meter. That requires three things: hunters who reason only from evidence the player actually left (so every capture is auditable and fair), period institutions that act as forced reveals (registers, frontiers, banks, cables) so exposure is a *choice* the player trades for comfort and speed, and a reveal cadence legible enough that the player can feel the net tighten and slip it. Everything below is built so the hunt and the life sim are one system: the register you sign is the bed you sleep in.

## P08.01 — The Ledger of Traces
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player is never observed directly. Every act writes a record into an institutional ledger (hotel register, clerk's memory, telegraph copy, bank counterfoil, passport stamp); hunters can only read ledgers.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each record carries place, time, identity used, fidelity (a signed Meldezettel versus a porter's memory), persistence (a Prussian sheet collected nightly versus an inn book nobody reads) and access (which services may query it, at what delay and cost). The player sees their trail as a map and may spend time, money and risk cleaning it — bribe a clerk, steal a register page — knowing cleaning writes a record too. Minute-to-minute: the convenient act that writes a record, or the awkward one that writes none. Session-to-session: watch the trail age.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel (tickets, stamps) and economy (drafts, letters of credit) write most records; deception chooses which identity signs; items (a labelled trunk) are records on legs; politics sets who may read which ledger; time pressure from records propagating on institutional clocks.
- **Historical grounding:** Compulsory hotel registration in Germany, Austria, France and Russia, none in Britain until the 1914 Aliens Restriction Act; retained telegraph copies; passport-free Western Europe before 1914; named drafts and circular notes.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Mundane verbs become tense decisions (loss aversion over a clean trail); a legible map of one's exposure sustains fear without helplessness; "it was the laundry mark" stories emerge unauthored.
- **Engine implications:** Append-only event log indexed by place, identity and time; cost-bounded hunter queries; lazy materialisation — only queried ledgers expand into detail, the rest remain counts. Continent-scale: the world simulates books, not clerks.
- **Risks / open questions:** Trail display without a spreadsheet; tuning persistence so hunts neither evaporate nor become inescapable.

## P08.02 — The Quiet Route: Forced Reveals at Register, Frontier, Bank and Cable
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** Scotland Yard's periodic reveal becomes the period's four compulsory disclosure points. A registered hotel, a passport frontier, a named bank instrument and a cable each reveal identity-plus-location to whoever reads that institution's records, on its clock. Routes are planned on two axes: fastest and quietest.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each day the player buys a comfort/exposure bundle. Wagons-Lits and a grand hotel reveal nightly; a third-class waiting room, gold coin and a letter by post reveal nothing but cost sleep, health and time. An exposure forecast for any route (how many reveals, to which services, with what delay) sits beside the timetable. Hardship accumulates as life-sim debt — illness, missed appointments, a shabby appearance that itself draws police attention.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and hunt primary; economy (cash versus instruments); deception (which identity reveals); politics (border regimes flip in 1914, exit permits, 1920 passport standardisation); items (luggage class as signal); time (reveals propagate on institutional schedules).
- **Historical grounding:** Meldepflicht in German-speaking lands; French hotel registers; Russian internal passports and hotel reporting; British absence of controls until 1914; universal passport and visa controls 1914–1920; named drafts; retained telegraph forms.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** A clean trade-off loop — pay in suffering for silence — felt every night; variable-ratio tension because a reveal only matters if a hunter is reading that ledger; mastery as the player learns each country's disclosure texture; 80 Days' "one more leg" compulsion with a second axis.
- **Engine implications:** Each institution is a reveal channel with latency and a reader list; the route planner queries channels for an exposure estimate; the world clock mutates channel parameters (1914, 1917, 1920).
- **Risks / open questions:** A degenerate "always sleep rough" strategy; hardship must bite.

## P08.03 — Heat Belongs to the Legend, Not the Player
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Hunters hold a dossier per cover identity, not per player. The hunt advances through *linkage events* — moments when evidence ties two legends together — and the player's craft is keeping legends unlinked.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each legend has its own papers, bank, luggage, tailor, cable address, handwriting and habitual cities. Links form when a witness sees both faces, two registers show one hand, a draft is endorsed from one name to another, the same trunk arrives under two names, or a photograph is matched across files. The player sees legends as separate heat columns and a linkage-risk indicator for any planned action ("arriving as A on the train B's ticket was bought for"). Burning a legend discards its heat but also its credit, contracts, friendships and households. Destroying the specific linking record can sever a link.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception primary; economy (each legend's finances separately funded); travel (never change identity mid-journey without a gap); items (handwriting, typewriter, luggage as linking artifacts); politics (a legend's nationality decides which frontiers open); time (links age; witnesses forget).
- **Historical grounding:** Multiple-passport careers among freelance agents; portrait-parlé description cards that made face-matching a filing operation; handwriting and typewriter typeface comparison; endorsement chains on bills and drafts.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Compartmentalisation is a satisfying puzzle; the endowment effect on a carefully built legend makes linkage genuinely dreadful; failure is granular — lose a name, not the game — so the player keeps playing after disasters.
- **Engine implications:** Evidence records carry identity tags; hunter inference includes a link-detection pass over co-occurring tags; legends are first-class entities owning assets and relationships.
- **Risks / open questions:** Bookkeeping burden; how many legends a player can truly manage.

## P08.04 — The Patchwork Dragnet: Jurisdictions and Liaison
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** There is no single hunter. Each service and private agency pursues with its own belief, reads only its own jurisdiction's ledgers freely, and learns from others through liaison channels whose existence, delay and honesty are set by the political world clock.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player routes through the *seams*: a Sûreté lead dies at the Belgian frontier unless Paris cables Brussels and Brussels agrees; the Okhrana's Paris bureau shadows in France but must ask Special Branch for London. Channels open and close historically — anti-anarchist police cooperation after 1898 and 1904, Entente sharing from 1914, the Okhrana's extinction in 1917, the international police commission of 1923. The player can poison a channel (feed one service a claim about another), buy a liaison clerk, or go where the dominant hunter has no friends. Private hunters — creditors' inquiry agents, Pinkertons — ignore borders but have small budgets.
- **Directive coverage:** Politics/warfare primary (alliances literally are the hunt map); travel (the seam is a frontier); economy (private hunters hired by your debts and lawsuits); deception (forged liaison traffic); time (channels mutate on dates).
- **Historical grounding:** Okhrana foreign Agentura in Paris; Special Branch–Okhrana contacts over anarchists; Rome 1898 and St Petersburg 1904 anti-anarchist protocols; wartime allied bureaux; Vienna 1923 commission.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** A readable geography of safety that shifts with history, producing fresh strategic puzzles every few game-years; theory-of-mind pleasure from setting hunters against each other.
- **Engine implications:** N independent belief states; a liaison graph with latency, fidelity and political gating; inter-hunter messages as scheduled, interceptable events. Dormant hunters hold only a shelved dossier, so cost scales with active hunts.
- **Risks / open questions:** Legibility of liaison state; avoiding a single safe-country exploit.

## P08.05 — The Honest Hunter: Hypothesis-Set Belief Engine (risky)
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Hunters never read the player's true position. Each maintains a bounded set of weighted hypotheses (identity, place, time, mode, habit) propagated along the real timetable graph and reweighted only by evidence records. Whatever the hunter "knows" is derivable, and the player can learn to shape it.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A quarry recorded in Vienna at 20:00 cannot reach Paris before the earliest arrival, so hypotheses advance along Bradshaw's; a river steamer outside the hunter's profile leaves the player outside the set until a record reveals it. Hunters profile you (class, hotels, banks, cable addresses), query ledgers where weight concentrates, or sit on fixed points — households, offices — that hypotheses must eventually revisit, within a budget. The player, modelling the model, exploits gaps: travel out of profile, cross modes, hold still where hypotheses are thin. When weight collapses onto one city, a cordon (P08.07) fires.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and travel primary; deception (profile-breaking as behavioural disguise); economy (hunters spend on cables and watchers); politics (jurisdiction gates ledger access); time (propagation in schedule time).
- **Historical grounding:** Period detection was ledger-and-timetable work — police circulars, Bradshaw's consulted to test alibis, description cards, telegraphed requests to stations ahead.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The hunter feels like a mind, not a meter; outwitting a legible reasoner produces genuine competence satisfaction; every capture is explicable, which lets forty hours of pursuit feel fair rather than cruel.
- **Engine implications:** Particle-filter-like propagation on a hierarchical graph (city → region → country), expanding resolution only where weight concentrates; deterministic seeds for replay and the autopsy (P08.09); hypotheses × hunters must fit a frame budget.
- **Risks / open questions:** Tuning; hunters too dumb or too lucky; conveying the model without exposing it.

## P08.06 — Shadows and Dry-Cleaning
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Physical surveillance is never announced. When a hunter assigns watchers, the world emits ambient anomalies (a repeated face, a cab that waits, a porter with questions), and the player decides whether to spend time on counter-surveillance routes that either expose or lose the shadow.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Anomalies are generated with a false-positive rate that falls with experience and rises with fatigue, drink and unfamiliar cities. Dry-cleaning uses real urban texture: department stores with several exits, a Métro or U-Bahn interchange, boarding a train and alighting before departure. Each costs time against appointments and the timetable. A lost shadow hands the hunter a last-seen fix and widens their hypotheses; an undetected shadow follows you to a meeting or a household, converting a location into a linkage (P08.03).
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt primary; travel (urban transit as counter-surveillance terrain); deception (behaving like your legend while checking your tail); items (a change of hat and coat in a trunk at left-luggage); economy (fares, lost business hours); politics (shadowing intensity varies by regime — Okhrana Russia against pre-war London).
- **Historical grounding:** Okhrana watcher teams and Special Branch shadowing; motor taxis from c. 1905–10; metropolitan railways in London, Paris, Berlin, Budapest and Vienna.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Calibrated paranoia — pattern detection with real costs for both false alarms and complacency — produces the "I knew it" peak; the skill is learnable, so veterans feel growth; it makes the hunt visible without a meter.
- **Engine implications:** Watchers as cheap local processes attached to a hunter's lead; anomaly emission as a signal-plus-noise channel; city templates listing dry-cleaning affordances.
- **Risks / open questions:** Players distrusting all signals; ground truth must be recoverable afterwards (P08.09).

## P08.07 — The Cordon Clock
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** When a hunter's belief concentrates on a city, a cordon forms in real time — cables to outlying stations, men to piers and frontier posts — with a formation curve, gaps and decay. Escape is a race against a fuse the player can partially read.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Formation takes hours to days depending on service reach and city size; coverage climbs unevenly — main termini first, river piers and branch halts last; night shifts thinner. The player reads warning signs (a plainclothesman on the concourse, papers demanded at the hotel, a paragraph in the evening edition) and consults Bradshaw's for the pre-dawn departure from the unwatched suburban station. Or hide inside the ring: cordons cost money and men and collapse after days without fresh evidence, but hiding needs a safe address and total silence — no cables, no bank.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and hunt primary; economy (a budget contest; hunter funds are finite); deception (passing a checkpoint on a cold legend); politics (wartime cordons are denser — requisitioned stations, military police); items (what fits in one bag); time (the fuse).
- **Historical grounding:** Telegraphed descriptions to stations along a line, frontier posts and ports; wartime exit controls; the practical porosity of pre-1914 continental borders.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The burning fuse is the purest tension/relief cycle in games; slipping the net is a near-miss the brain rewards disproportionately; going to ground is a slow-burn alternative with its own payoff.
- **Engine implications:** Cordon as a scheduled process over a city's exit set with per-exit coverage curves; joined with the timetable to compute escape windows; decay tied to hunter budget.
- **Risks / open questions:** Over-frequent cordons numb; they must be rare, earned and memorable.

## P08.08 — The Newspaper Mirror
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Wanted notices, reward adverts and "information wanted" columns make every NPC within a paper's circulation a potential witness — and because the player reads the same papers, the press becomes the one window into what hunters believe.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Multiple daily editions deliver the hunt's public state: a portrait-parlé description, a name, increasingly after the early 1900s a half-tone photograph. Publication raises witness recall and spontaneous denunciation inside the circulation area and propagates via agencies to other countries with delay. The player answers through the same medium: agony-column coded messages to accomplices, a planted sighting via a stringer, a paid paragraph announcing a departure that never happens. A photograph in print is catastrophic for a legend and forces a change of appearance that alters the player's social reach (a shaven agent cannot resume the bearded legend's club).
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and deception primary; economy (buying paragraphs; a share in a paper as long-term asset); politics (wartime censorship suppresses or amplifies notices); travel (circulation areas as risk maps); coding (agony-column ciphers); time (editions as a daily reveal rhythm).
- **Historical grounding:** Police Gazette and continental Steckbrief traditions; reward advertising; the spread of half-tone photography in dailies; Reuters, Havas and Wolff syndication; wartime press censorship; the agony column as a real covert channel.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The player voluntarily seeks dread (information-gap curiosity) on a daily cadence; relief when the edition is silent; the inversion of turning the hunter's megaphone against them.
- **Engine implications:** Publications as broadcast events with circulation geometry and recall multipliers; agency propagation as delayed re-broadcast; the witness model subscribes to broadcasts.
- **Risks / open questions:** Notice text must be procedural, never authored; the photo-era transition needs tuning.

## P08.09 — The Trail Autopsy
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Capture is never game over and never a mystery. Every arrest, near-miss or exposed legend produces a replayable reconstruction of the exact evidence chain and inference the hunter used, with the branch points where the player could have broken it.
- **How it plays (core loop):** After an arrest the player sees the hunter's dossier as it was built: the register in one city, the cable copy in another, the teller who remembered a face, the timetable deduction that excluded other routes. Then the life sim continues — interrogation (the deception system), remand, trial, expulsion, exchange, escape, or a sentence that ages the character and advances the world clock. A redacted autopsy is also offered for close calls the player survived, so learning is not gated behind failure.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt primary; deception (interrogation as the next act); economy (bail, bribes, counsel, lost contracts); politics (who you are exchanged to depends on current alignments; a wartime arrest is a different fate from a peacetime one); travel (expulsion routes); time (sentences consume years).
- **Historical grounding:** Expulsion or short sentences as the common fate of foreign agents before 1914; wartime courts-martial; prisoner exchanges; archives that enabled later re-arrest.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Fairness attribution is the strongest predictor of persistence after loss; the autopsy converts loss into mastery ("never endorse a draft across legends again"); the chain reads as a story the player authored by accident; it makes the hunter's intelligence credible.
- **Engine implications:** Deterministic, seed-replayable simulation with provenance on every inference (record → reweight → action); this doubles as the balancing and debugging tool for the whole engine.
- **Risks / open questions:** Reconstructions must read as narrative, not log dumps; capture consequences need variety across a career.

## P08.10 — Feeding the Hunter: The False-Evidence Economy (risky)
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Because hunters reason only from records, the player can manufacture records. Deception and the hunt become one system: buy a through-ticket you never use, have an accomplice sign your name into a distant register, post letters with distant postmarks, cable from a station you leave by cab.
- **How it plays (core loop):** False evidence must fit the timetable and the hunter's profile of you, because the hunter checks Bradshaw's too; an impossible journey is discarded and raises the hunter's scepticism against that evidence class. Fabrication costs money, accomplices (each a liability with their own trail) and time. The payoff is steering hypotheses into a cordon on the wrong city while you do business in the right one. Hunters that detect a fabrication learn, and a service burned twice begins to read inversions.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception primary; travel (fabrications are routes); economy (decoy itineraries; accomplices on retainer); items (a monogrammed trunk sent ahead as bait); politics (planting evidence that implicates a rival service); time (decoys sequenced against real movements).
- **Historical grounding:** Documented agent use of decoy bookings, mail drops and cover addresses; telegraph-office provenance on every cable; anonymous continental third-class travel.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Deceiving a visible reasoner is the richest form of agency; a cordon forming on the decoy is a theory-of-mind payoff; the learning hunter sustains the arms race across a career, not one exploit; decoys that can backfire deliver gambling-grade anticipation.
- **Engine implications:** Records carry an authorship field hidden from hunters but used by the scepticism learner; plausibility checks against the timetable graph; accomplices as agents with their own traces.
- **Risks / open questions:** Cheap decoys would trivialise the hunt; the scepticism learner must punish overuse without punishing cleverness.

## P08.11 — Poacher and Gamekeeper: The Hunter's Board
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** One career, both roles. Between fugitive stretches the player takes counter-espionage or inquiry commissions and hunts an AI quarry across the ledgers, timetables and liaison channels used against them — learning the hunter's mind by being it.
- **How it plays (core loop):** As hunter the player receives delayed ledger extracts, allocates watchers to termini, piers and frontier posts, cables foreign police, who may refuse, prunes impossible hypotheses with Bradshaw's, and chooses when to spring a cordon — early and leaky, or late and expensive. The quarry runs on the inverted belief engine (P08.05): it leaves traces, buys decoys, dry-cleans. Commissions pay and build standing with a service — later the liaison channel you most fear as quarry. Hunting writes your own records: expense claims and cables show where you were.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt primary both ways; politics (your employer decides which ledgers you may read); economy (commission, expenses, reward money); travel (the hunter must physically go to question a clerk); deception (quarries and witnesses lie; a double agent may hunt their own network); time (the quarry moves regardless).
- **Historical grounding:** Freelance agents served several services in sequence; private inquiry agencies took pursuit contracts; police circulars and telegraphed descriptions were the working tools.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Deduction pleasure (hypothesis elimination) alternates with evasion pleasure across sessions; skill transfer is real — a good hunter becomes a better fugitive, felt as mastery; symmetry makes the AI's later pursuit credible and legible.
- **Engine implications:** The belief engine must be human-usable: hypotheses exposed as a weighted board; the quarry agent reuses the player-facing trace systems. Little extra content cost: both roles share one simulation.
- **Risks / open questions:** Hunter sessions must stay embodied, not become a spreadsheet.

## P08.12 — The Long Hunt: Dossiers That Never Close (risky)
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** A 1895–1925 career in which no evidence is ever deleted. Fingerprint cards, photographs and cable copies persist in archives, shelved when a hunt stalls, reopened when new evidence, decryption or political priority arrives. The endgame is the convergence of shelved dossiers.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Early exposures feel cheap, but a 1902 arrest leaves prints at the Fingerprint Bureau; a 1916 cable copy becomes readable in 1919 when its code breaks; the service you served in 1912 hunts you in 1918 after a revolution inherits its files. Dormant dossiers wake when a linkage (P08.03) touches them or the world clock changes who wants you. Archives can be attacked — fire, theft, bribery, lawsuit — each a high-stakes operation. Ageing matters: the face of 1905 diverges from that of 1920, but portrait-parlé fields that never change (height, ears, scars) stay dangerous.
- **Directive coverage:** All six: travel (where archives are), deception (identity drift), items (the single incriminating artifact), economy (old debts as reopeners), politics/warfare (regime change as principal reopener), time (decades), the hunt as the career's shape.
- **Historical grounding:** Fingerprinting at Scotland Yard from 1901; Pinkerton's photographic rogues' gallery; revolutionary successors inheriting old police files in 1917; Room 40 and post-war decryption; the 1923 international police commission pooling records.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Long-horizon consequence is the rarest, most prized feeling in games; dread of the archive gives every early success a sweet-and-sour flavour; systems, not an author, write the ending.
- **Engine implications:** Dossiers as cold storage with wake triggers; the world clock as reopener; cheap at scale because sleeping dossiers consume no cycles; needs stable record identity across decades.
- **Risks / open questions:** Inevitability could feel oppressive — real paths to retire clean are essential.

# Round 1A Proposals — Player Psychology & Retention Designer (Panelist P09)

## Panelist statement
I judge every mechanic by two questions: what pulls a player in within the first minute, and what makes them refuse the natural stopping point an hour later. The compulsion must come from honest sources — tension/relief cycles anchored to the period's real clocks, loss aversion over things the player actually built, mastery that lives in the player's head, and choices that visibly close doors. No variable-ratio reward schedules, no appointment nagging, no manufactured scarcity: players should keep playing because the world moves without them and because what they hold is worth protecting.

---

## P09.01 — The Next Departure
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The timetable, not a menu, structures the session: every pause the game offers (hotel, sleeper berth, safe house) sits a few in-game hours before a departure the player can already see.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player plans a leg from a Bradshaw-style timetable (departures, durations, fares, classes). Arrival opens a city window — the hours until the next useful departure — for errands: bank, poste restante, contacts, market. Rest-and-save is offered at the hotel register, but the board shows a 23:40 through-train reaching Berlin before the bourse opens. Night trains turn "I'll stop here" into "I'll sleep on the train"; the leg resolves on next load, world and hunt advanced.
- **Directive coverage:** Travel and time pressure primary (the window is a countdown); deception, economy and politics fill it. Hunt: a hotel stop means police registration, so saving costs cover; a sleeper leaves a ticket-class trace.
- **Historical grounding:** Bradshaw's Continental Guide; through-trains (Orient Express, Nord-Express); Wagons-Lits sleepers; compulsory hotel registration in Germany, Austria, France and Russia.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Zeigarnik effect (unfinished plans stay mentally active) plus goal-gradient effect (effort rises as a visible goal nears): the player always pauses within sight of a connection and so seldom pauses. Sessions end aboard a train, converting stopping into anticipation — a cliffhanger generated by the schedule, never authored. The hotel pause carries an honest cover cost, so continuing is a real choice, not a nag.
- **Engine implications:** Timetable as data rows; the city window as a scheduled-event queue; deferred, deterministic leg resolution on load; saves keyed to world time. Scales linearly.
- **Risks / open questions:** Stopping must not be punished hard; off-screen resolution must never kill without warnings visible before boarding.

## P09.02 — The Reveal Calendar
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The hunt runs on a known cadence: at scheduled intervals the pursuing services consolidate the player's traces into a "fix". The player knows exactly when, never what: the fix's quality depends on their own prior behaviour.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A calendar strip shows the next fix. Between fixes the player trades, travels and meets, each act leaving a typed trace (ticket class, hotel register, telegram copy). On fix day an abstract readout arrives: which services narrowed to which region, and which trace type gave them most. A short window follows to move, swap legend or go quiet. Cadence tightens with the world: weekly before 1914, near-daily in wartime.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and time pressure primary. Travel is the escape between fixes; deception (legend swap) breaks a fix; items (forged papers) enable it; going quiet forfeits income; politics decides who hunts and who shares.
- **Historical grounding:** Scotland Yard's reveal cadence mapped onto wanted notices, police gazettes, telegraphed circulars to frontier posts, 1914 passport controls, wartime censorship.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Scheduled uncertainty — a fixed interval with uncertain content — is the strongest honest anticipation structure: tension climbs toward fix day, relief or alarm follows, then reset. Unlike random reveals it is fully causal, so outcomes feel earned (internal locus of control). Reading trace types is a mastery curve; the quiet week before a fix sets a tempo of boldness and retreat, the core tension/relief cycle.
- **Engine implications:** Typed, timestamped trace log; per-service inference at scheduled ticks; cordon generation as an event; cadence as a world-state parameter altered by macro-events. Cheap, deterministic batch inference.
- **Risks / open questions:** Readout abstraction: too legible is exploitable, too opaque feels unfair. Cadence tuning per era.

## P09.03 — Legends You Can Burn
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Cover identities are long-lived assets with their own ledgers — account, address, references, registration history. They appreciate with use and can be burned to collapse a hunter's fix. The game makes the player love them first.
- **How it plays (core loop):** A legend is built over months: registrations, an account, references, trades under its name. Each use adds credibility and exposure (trace density). When the hunt closes, the player chooses which legend to burn: its account, household and contacts become inaccessible and the fix collapses into pursuit of a ghost. Legends can be rehabilitated at cost, or sold — a clean legend is an item with provenance.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception and hunt primary. Economy: name-bound letters of credit split wealth across legends. Travel: after 1914 passports are per-legend. Items: forged papers. Politics: a legend's nationality decides which wartime frontiers open.
- **Historical grounding:** Letters of introduction as credentials; name-bound letters of credit and circular notes; hotel police registration; the 1914 passport regime, standardised 1920; parallel households.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Endowment effect: people value what they built far above market worth, so burning a legend nurtured for twenty hours is a genuine loss — the hunt bites without killing. Loss aversion makes the player ration exposure, so every registration is a meaningful choice. Choosing which legend dies is rich in anticipated regret, and a new legend offers the clean-slate appeal that makes roguelikes compulsive, inside one continuous career.
- **Engine implications:** Identity as an entity with its own trace log, accounts and relationship edges; a dependency graph so burning cascades correctly (contacts bind to the name); provenance for sold legends.
- **Risks / open questions:** Interface load; cascade correctness; legend hoarding, countered by upkeep costs.

## P09.04 — The Second Agent
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** One persistent world runs 1895–1925. When the player's agent is caught, killed or ruined, the world does not reset: a new agent begins at the current date with nothing but what the player knows, in a world that remembers the predecessor.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Live a career; fail; begin another with the clock still running — 1912 now, Balkan war looming, the predecessor's codebook in a Vienna evidence vault, their Paris account frozen. Routes, timetables, codes, which markets move in which crises: this knowledge is the only progression, and it lives in the player's head. Recovering those assets is an optional objective with its own trace cost.
- **Directive coverage:** All five: politics/warfare drive the clock; the hunt persists as institutional memory; economy as frozen or recoverable assets; items as persistent artifacts; travel knowledge as the mastery axis; deception restarts from zero legends.
- **Historical grounding:** The thirty-year macro-event calendar; institutional continuity of services and archives; frozen accounts and expropriation after 1917; Bradshaw-literate expertise.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Dissolves the sunk-cost versus fresh-start conflict that kills retention in permadeath games: failure costs the agent, not the world or the player's competence. Mastery is honest and visible (a veteran plans a Constantinople escape in seconds): competence in the self-determination sense, the most durable motivator. The predecessor's trail opens a curiosity gap. The hard 1925 horizon keeps the campaign finite; each agent's life is a chapter written by play.
- **Engine implications:** Single persistent world with multiple agent lifespans; entity memory across agents; fully data-driven content; deterministic macro-event schedule with perturbation flags.
- **Risks / open questions:** Late-start agents miss the pre-war era and face a harder wartime world — perhaps a feature; needs a world-state difficulty curve.

## P09.05 — Stale News
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player sees the world only through period channels with realistic lag: newspapers (editions vary by city), telegrams (fast, costly, interceptable), letters (slow, private), rumour (free, unreliable). Everything acted upon is hours to weeks old.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each morning the player chooses which editions and wires to buy. Headlines reveal that something happened — a mobilisation, a bourse panic — but details wait for the next edition or arrival. Acting on stale information is the core risk: a concession buyable three days ago is gone; a frontier has closed. Cables cost money and leave a copy at the telegraph office; commercial code obscures. News can be planted via an agency stringer.
- **Directive coverage:** Time pressure primary: the lag is the pressure. Coding (cable codes); economy (information arbitrage); politics (macro-events arrive late); travel (lag depends on location); hunt (cable copies are traces; wanted notices are news).
- **Historical grounding:** Multi-edition dailies; Reuters, Havas, Wolff; telegraph companies retaining copies; commercial codebooks; the All Red Line (1902); censorship from 1914.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Loewenstein's information-gap theory of curiosity: a headline without detail is a gap the mind must close, so "buy the evening edition" is irresistible. Need for closure pulls the player toward events. Counterfactual feedback arrives through the world's own channels — the paper reports what happened where you were not — producing regret and anticipation without authored voice. Knowing which city hears what first becomes mastery.
- **Engine implications:** An event-visibility layer: events carry propagation profiles per channel and location; player knowledge stored apart from world truth, diffed lazily; cable interception hooks.
- **Risks / open questions:** Lag must be legible (edition dates shown) or feels unfair; planted news must not trivialise markets.

## P09.06 — The Obligation Calendar
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The spy's life is a calendar of dated promises — deliveries, meetings, bill maturities, household upkeep, visa expiries, reports due. The calendar is the main planning surface, and the hunter reads it too, because obligations predict location.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Every accepted commission, loan, lease or rendezvous places a dated entry. The player routes travel to satisfy them; conflicts are visible and resolved by renegotiation (trust loss), proxies (cut-outs) or default (debt and reputation consequences). The hunter's inference uses observable obligations — a bill maturing in Vienna, a registered lease — to predict presence, so reliability is the player's vulnerability. Breaking pattern costs money and trust.
- **Directive coverage:** Time pressure primary. Economy (bills, debts, rents); hunt (predictability as evidence); travel (the calendar is a routing problem); deception (proxies; false appointments as lures); politics (faction retainers demand dated reports).
- **Historical grounding:** Bills of exchange with fixed maturities; letters of credit; households in several cities; visa validity after 1914; telegraphed appointments; poste restante holding limits.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Commitment devices: people honour self-set deadlines far more than imposed ones, so every promise becomes a deadline the player owns. The planning fallacy becomes a learnable skill — veterans leave slack. Reliability versus predictability is a recurring meaningful choice, not a one-off. Dated obligations also create natural sessions: the player knows the 14th is the Vienna maturity and plays toward it — goal-gradient at the scale of a week.
- **Engine implications:** Dated obligation entities with dependencies; conflict detection; the hunter sees only observable obligations; default cascades through debt and reputation. A priority queue.
- **Risks / open questions:** The calendar can become a spreadsheet; obligation types must be few and legible.

## P09.07 — The Commission Board
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Opportunities — commissions from services, firms and factions; trades; meetings — arrive as time-windowed offers tied to the world clock, made mutually exclusive by geography: accepting one in Baku forecloses one in Tangier by travel time alone.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The board fills from world state: an arms commission after a crisis, a concession when a railway reaches a province, a document job when a fleet sails. Each offer carries a window, location, payoff and trace cost. The player evaluates by timetable (can I get there?), hunt state (does it cross a cordon?) and politics (who is angered). Lapsed offers are reported with outcomes: taken by a rival, or gone as the crisis cooled.
- **Directive coverage:** Time pressure primary. Travel (feasibility is a timetable query); economy (payoffs, trades); politics (offers generated by macro-events); deception (the jobs); hunt (trace cost); items (deliverables with provenance).
- **Historical grounding:** Commission agents for Vickers, Krupp and Schneider; concession brokering in Manchuria, Persia and Ottoman lands; Baku oil; naval plans as prize documents; the crisis calendar (Morocco 1905/1911, Bosnia 1908, Balkan Wars).
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Meaningful choice requires mutual exclusivity; here every acceptance is a visible refusal. Scarcity is honest, arising from geometry and the world clock, not timers. Anticipated regret sharpens each decision, and reported outcomes of lapsed offers deliver counterfactual feedback that fuels "next time". Because offers follow foreseeable macro-events, the board produces scheduled anticipation: Morocco is heating up; offers will come.
- **Engine implications:** Offer generator reading world state; expiry queue; feasibility checks against timetable and cordons; outcome resolution for unaccepted offers. Lightweight; generated lazily per region.
- **Risks / open questions:** Must not degrade into a quest log — keep offers few and consequential.

## P09.08 — Cipher Hands
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player encodes and decodes with period methods — commercial codebooks, Vigenère, Playfair, columnar transposition, book cipher — taught incrementally. Skill lives in the player's head; in-world aids trade effort for cost and trace.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Messages arrive as ciphertext. The player chooses: decode by hand (free, private, slow), use a codebook item (fast, but an incriminating artifact that can be found or stolen), or pay a commercial decoding bureau (fast, costly, leaves a copy). Sending mirrors this. Mastery compounds: a player who knows Playfair reads a captured dispatch in minutes while the hunt barely advances.
- **Directive coverage:** Coding primary. Items (codebooks with provenance); economy (bureau fees; commercial codes cut telegram costs); hunt (plaintext in luggage is evidence; cable copies are traces); politics (systems change with the war: ADFGVX 1918, one-time pads 1917–19); time (decoding consumes hours).
- **Historical grounding:** Playfair in British field use; Vigenère and transposition; book ciphers; ABC and Bentley's commercial codes; Room 40; the Zimmermann telegram.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Competence, in self-determination theory, is the most durable motivator; a skill genuinely acquired rather than a stat raised produces flow and pride. The staircase from commercial code to Playfair to transposition is a mastery curve with visible payoff. The three-way choice keeps novice and expert engaged while making expertise strictly better for concealment. Decoding a captured message is a curiosity gap closed by effort, the most satisfying closure there is.
- **Engine implications:** Cipher routines as pure functions; messages carry system and key; key distribution as world events; a difficulty ramp and optional assist layer. Trivially scalable.
- **Risks / open questions:** Friction for some players; core progress must never gate on hand-decoding — the paid route must always exist.

## P09.09 — Comfort or Cover
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every convenience of the age — first class, a Wagons-Lits berth, a grand hotel, a telegram, a letter of credit — is faster, healthier, better for standing, and registered. Every anonymous alternative — third class, boarding house, gold coin — is slower, unhealthier and nearly traceless. One continuous dial, tuned daily.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each purchase sets the dial: class, lodging, payment instrument, channel. Health is a resource worn down by cheap choices (fatigue, illness, the 1918 pandemic); standing — doors opened by apparent means — erodes too. Trace density rises with expensive choices and is harvested at frontiers and hotel registers. A wealthy legend in third class is itself an anomaly.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and travel primary. Economy (the price of anonymity); deception (legend consistent with mode); items (coin versus instruments); politics (wartime requisition removes first class; passport checks move the dial).
- **Historical grounding:** Three classes of carriage; Wagons-Lits; hotel police registration; telegraph copies; name-bound credit instruments versus gold coin; the influenza pandemic; wartime requisition of rolling stock.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Approach-avoidance conflict is the engine of tension: the player wants the sleeper and fears the register. Because the dial is continuous and daily, it yields micro-dilemmas at a rhythm that sustains attention without fatigue: not every choice is high-stakes, but all are consequential. Loss aversion operates on two fronts (health, cover), so no dominant strategy exists; the player's style emerges and becomes identity, which a modelling hunter exploits (P09.10).
- **Engine implications:** Every transaction carries a cost vector (money, time, health, standing, trace); frontier and register events harvest traces; a legend-consistency check.
- **Risks / open questions:** The dial must be visible without becoming a spreadsheet; health spirals need a floor.

## P09.10 — The Dossier Mirror
- **Type:** game-defining system (risky)
- **Central mechanical hook:** The hunter keeps a dossier on the player built only from traces: habits (always first class, the same cable address, Thursday bank visits), known legends, associated institutions. The player can steal, buy or glimpse it and must decide which habits to break at efficiency cost.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Prediction is pattern-based, not omniscient: the hunter forecasts where a creature of habit will be. Periodically the player can read the dossier — intercept a circular, bribe a registry, burgle a bureau — as an honest list of inferred regularities with confidence levels, showing precisely which conveniences betray them. Breaking a pattern costs time, money or health (P09.09); a sudden break is itself an anomaly.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and deception primary — counter-surveillance as self-knowledge. Travel (routes as habits); economy (pattern-breaking costs); items (the dossier is a stealable artifact); politics (dossier-pooling follows alliances: the 1907 Convention, wartime Entente sharing).
- **Historical grounding:** Okhrana Paris bureau files; Special Branch; Bertillonage and fingerprinting (1901); mail perlustration; informant networks; inter-allied sharing.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Being modelled is uncanny and compelling: the game reflects the player's own behaviour back as evidence, producing self-recognition and learning. It converts the hunt from "avoid the red dot" into "know thyself"; pattern-breaking is agency at its purest. The dossier externalises the mastery curve — a veteran's dossier is thin. Risky because inference must be legible and never feel like cheating; brilliant if the hunter becomes a mirror, not a monster.
- **Engine implications:** Per-service inference over the trace log (frequency, recency, co-occurrence); confidence scoring; dossier serialised as a readable artifact; inter-service sharing graph. Inference at ticks, cached.
- **Risks / open questions:** Inference quality; players seeding deliberate noise (acceptable if costly); readability of probabilistic output.

## P09.11 — Three Masters
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** The player may hold retainers from several services and firms at once. Each maintains a trust/suspicion state; each transaction with one is a liability with the others. Play is keeping several plausible accounts of yourself alive at once.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Accept retainers from, say, two services and a trading house. Each demands exclusivity, periodic reports and proofs of loyalty. Information sold to one can leak through the very channels the player exploits — telegraph copies, perlustration, informants — raising suspicion elsewhere. The player feeds each patron something true, something stale (P09.05) and something fabricated; patrons verify with lag. A crossed threshold turns a patron into a pursuer (P09.02).
- **Directive coverage:** Deception and politics primary; alliance shifts (1907 Convention, 1914 alignments) make some combinations impossible overnight. Economy (stacked retainers); hunt (patrons become hunters); coding (reports in cipher); travel (patrons in different capitals); time (dated reports).
- **Historical grounding:** Semi-official agents serving several services; the Secret Service Bureau (1909), Deuxième Bureau, Evidenzbureau, Abteilung IIIb, Okhrana; arms-commission double-dealing; wartime censorship raising leak risk.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Plate-spinning produces sustained cognitive engagement — the pleasurable load of keeping accounts consistent — punctuated by near-misses (a leak that almost crossed a threshold). Near-misses motivate strongly when the outcome is under the player's control, which separates this from gambling. Each added master raises income and tension together: a self-set difficulty slider. Betrayal emerges from systems, and a patron turning hunter is a reversal the player authored.
- **Engine implications:** Per-faction belief state; information objects with provenance and per-channel leak probability; threshold events; an alliance graph with scheduled changes. Lightweight.
- **Risks / open questions:** Legibility of suspicion; preventing "lie to everyone" — fabricated reports must be verifiable with delay.

## P09.12 — The Retirement Clause
- **Type:** whole-game concept (risky)
- **Central mechanical hook:** On any day the player may walk away: liquidate, choose a legend and a city, retire. The game ends and scores the life kept: wealth at that day's exchange, legend cleanliness, debts, who still hunts. The board always has one more job; the question is when to stop.
- **How it plays (core loop):** All other systems run. A live estimate of what would be kept is always visible: wealth in gold-standard terms (after 1914 exposed to exchange controls and collapse: retire on roubles in 1916 and watch it vanish), legend safety, debts, enemies. Playing on risks all; death or capture scores far below any retirement.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy primary (what you keep). Politics (safe havens change: not Russia after 1917); hunt (retirement holds only if the legend is unknown); travel (reaching the haven); time (1925 horizon; currency events); deception (the clean legend).
- **Historical grounding:** Gold-standard parities until 1914; wartime exchange controls; rouble collapse and expropriation from 1917; mark hyperinflation 1923; neutral havens; the period agent who never retired.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The honest heart of "one more job": sunk cost and loss aversion pull the player to protect accumulated wealth; the escalating board pulls them to risk it. Making quitting-while-ahead a real, scored choice gives the game a meaningful endgame decision, not a final confrontation. The live estimate turns every risky act into a visible bet on something the player owns, the endowment effect made explicit. Risky: players may retire early; mitigated by scoring legend quality and the persistent world (P09.04).
- **Engine implications:** Continuous valuation over assets, legends, debts and pursuit; a currency-event schedule; end-state generation. Cheap.
- **Risks / open questions:** Early retirement; scoring that rewards greed; the board must escalate honestly.

# Round 1A Proposals — Life-Sim & Character Progression Designer (Panelist P10)

## Panelist statement
My lens is the spy as a whole life lived across up to thirty years: a body that ages and falls ill, skills that grow and rust, names that must be fed and can die, households and dependants that eat money and leak information, debts that keep their own calendar, and an ending that the life itself earns. The game must get one thing right: everything mundane (rent, a cough, a birthday) must run on the same resource system as everything operational (a stolen blueprint, a frontier, a tail), so that the private life is never a side-menu but the very record the hunters eventually read. I propose states, resources and rules only; whatever people and plots appear are what the systems generate.

## P10.01 — Thirty Winters: The Whole-Life Arc as the Game
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** One life, 1895–1925, the agent ageing from early twenties to early fifties while three unsynchronised clocks run: the world clock (macro-events), the life clock (ageing, health, skill rust) and the hunt clock (accumulated exposure). The game is designed to end; the player's craft is choosing how.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Minutes: operations, conversations, travel legs, trades. Days: routing, correspondence, feeding names and households. Yearly: an audit (P10.11) applies age curves, compounds debts, decays unmaintained standings and raises the hunt's baseline from every exposure the life has generated. Early years are cheap and forgiving (passport-free Europe, gold-standard money, a body that heals); middle years build an empire of households, concessions and retainers; late years are the hunt, when wartime border regimes, photographic passports and thickening files make every past choice load-bearing.
- **Directive coverage:** Life-sim primary; travel, deception, items, economy and politics are the verbs by which the life accumulates; time pressure comes from three unaligned clocks; the hunt is the life's integral.
- **Historical grounding:** The window spans the shift from a passport-free, gold-standard Europe before systematic fingerprinting (Scotland Yard, 1901) to one of visas, exit permits, counter-espionage bureaux and currency collapse.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Mortality salience plus the endowment effect: the player has built things that can be lost, and the drift from freedom to vulnerability generates unscripted dread. Every mundane decision has a thirty-year tail: the "one more year" pull of grand-strategy campaigns.
- **Engine implications:** Append-only, event-sourced life record; decay functions evaluated lazily on access, not per tick; year-granular compaction of stale state; the hunter AI mines the same record.
- **Risks / open questions:** Pacing thirty years into sessions; many players will never finish a life, so fast-forward and audit cadence must carry it.

## P10.02 — Legends as Maintained Assets
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** A cover biography is an owned asset: a fact-graph (name, nationality, profession, residence, bank, references, papers) whose facts carry evidentiary weights that decay unless fed and can be contradicted by the player's other legends.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player builds a legend fact by fact: a hotel-registration trail, a bank account, an accommodation address receiving real letters, a club membership, a visible means of support. Each fact costs money and time to maintain, because the name must appear somewhere periodically. The engine records where each legend was witnessed (city, circle, date); legends witnessed in overlapping circles accumulate collision risk, and a collision becomes an inconsistency record hunters can find. Legends may be rested (cheap, decays), retired cleanly (slow, expensive) or burnt (instant; every fact becomes hunter evidence).
- **Directive coverage:** Deception primary. Travel: frontier regimes test papers; passport-free pre-1914 Europe demands social proof instead. Items: cards, letters and papers with forgery grades. Economy: legends cost money and their cover occupation must produce real transactions. Politics: nationality becomes enemy-alien status in 1914. Hunt: legends are the deduction target.
- **Historical grounding:** Letters of introduction, visiting cards, hotel police registration, residence permits, accommodation addresses, enemy-alien registration from 1914, photographic passports from 1914–15, the 1920 League standardisation.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Plate-spinning cognition: several fragile constructs each demanding attention is a reliable flow generator. The endowment effect makes a well-fed legend feel like property; dread of collision supplies tension with no authored content.
- **Engine implications:** Per-legend weighted, timestamped fact graphs; witness records as a sparse legend × circle × city matrix; collision detection by graph intersection on access.
- **Risks / open questions:** Upkeep must not become chores; needs a legible legend-health view and delegable routines that leak.

## P10.03 — Households as Resource Engines
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** A household (flat, house, long-let hotel suite, room above a shop) is a persistent state machine anchored to one city and one legend: it produces plausibility, storage, an address and local standing; consumes rent, wages and attention; and leaks information through everyone who serves it.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player leases under a name, staffs abstract roles (housekeeper, valet, cook, porter) carrying wage, discretion and loyalty values, stores items and papers at graded concealment levels, and anchors the legend to the address. Each household has a quarter-day rent calendar, a condition stock, a talk rate (staff and neighbours emit witness records) and standing in local circles. Neglect cascades: unpaid rent, distraint on goods, an auction notice, a court record, documents in hunters' hands. Several households in several cities multiply plausibility and refuges but divide money and attention. External regimes can delete a household: wartime requisition, enemy-property custodians from 1914, expropriation from 1917.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and deception primary. Travel: a household is where luggage, caches and post wait between legs. Items: graded storage. Politics: requisition and seizure regimes. Hunt: every household is a hiding place and a known address at once.
- **Historical grounding:** Quarterly rent terms, servant-keeping as ordinary middle-class practice, concierges and Russian yard-keepers reporting to police, distraint for rent, trading-with-the-enemy custodianship, Soviet expropriation.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Nest-building compulsion; the endowment effect turns every household into a hostage to fortune; "spread or consolidate" is a renewable strategic dilemma.
- **Engine implications:** Household entities depending on city rent calendars, legal regimes and the local circle graph; cascade rules fired on missed payments; lazy evaluation while the player is absent.
- **Risks / open questions:** Micro-management load; delegated management must exist and must leak.

## P10.04 — Circles of Repute: Reputation Attached to Names, Not to the Player
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Reputation is not one bar but a set of circles (a diplomatic set, an officers' mess, a bourse, a dockside underworld, an émigré colony, a service's desk), each with memory, gossip rate and bridges to other circles, each remembering a name rather than the player.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Actions under a legend write to that legend's standing in the circle that witnessed them: trusted, dangerous, rich, ridiculous, foreign, wanted. Standing decays toward neutral with time and distance. Circles connect through bridging institutions (a bank, a club, a consulate); gossip crosses bridges at a rate, so a scandal surfaces elsewhere weeks later, possibly linking two names if someone sits in both circles. High standing opens gates (introductions, credit, invitations); low standing in the right circle is an asset. Hunters read circles too: notoriety raises ambient awareness.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception and conversation primary (conversation moves are reputation moves). Economy: credit is reputation. Politics: circles align with factions and flip in 1914 and 1917. Travel: circles are place-bound; arriving with no standing differs from returning with too much. Hunt: notoriety propagation.
- **Historical grounding:** Club and mess culture, blackballing, letters of introduction, bourse rumour, consular networks, émigré colonies watched by the Okhrana's Paris bureau, society columns.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The double-life fantasy made mechanical: being a different person in each circle is legible juggling, and gossip propagation yields delayed, deducible consequences: the dread of "when will that catch up with me".
- **Engine implications:** Circle graph per city; sparse name × circle standing vectors; propagation along bridges with rate and decay, capped breadth per tick.
- **Risks / open questions:** Too many circles become noise; keep five to eight active per city with a legible map.

## P10.05 — The Rust Model: Skills Grow by Use and Decay by Disuse
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Every skill (languages by social register, cipher work, forgery, marksmanship, riding, motoring, wireless operation, bookkeeping, dancing, lock work) is a learning curve with use-based growth, time-based forgetting and age-modified rates. Rusty skills return cheaply but produce tells meanwhile.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Skills are exercised by actions, never bought. Growth is fast at low levels and slows toward mastery; disuse decays effective level toward a retained floor, so mastery returns at a discount but costs time at the wrong moment. Age bends the curves: physical skills decline early, cognitive late, social grow longest. Period technology moves the skill map: motoring is rare in 1900 and expected of a class by 1920; wireless operation is a trade born inside the game's span; typewriting becomes ordinary clerical work, and typefaces identify machines.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception (a rusted accent register is a tell) and coding (cipher speed and error rate) primary. Travel: riding, motoring, reading a foreign timetable. Economy: bookkeeping and languages gate business. Politics: military skills matter 1914–22. Hunt: a tell is a witness record.
- **Historical grounding:** Motor licensing (France from 1893, Britain 1903, Germany nationally 1909), Marconi operators, typewriter typeface identification, French as the diplomatic language, class-coded accomplishments (riding, dancing, cards).
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Mastery and the pain of letting go: the player curates a portfolio and chooses which selves to keep sharp. "Use it or lose it" is a renewable prompt to act, and rust-induced near-misses create memorable moments.
- **Engine implications:** Skill stored as (level, last-used, floor) evaluated lazily; age modifiers as functions of birth year; tells emitted as witness records.
- **Risks / open questions:** Decay can feel punitive; tune half-lives generously and surface rust before it bites.

## P10.06 — Vices as Compound Interest (risky)
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Gambling, drink, opiates and cocaine, spending and liaisons are habit systems with craving, tolerance and withdrawal. Each gives real short-term benefits (access, nerve, liquidity, information) and compounding long-term costs (health, debt, blackmail, predictability), so the player manages the agent's addiction while the game works on their own.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Each vice has a craving stock rising with stress and time, a tolerance level raising the dose needed, and a withdrawal penalty on skills if unmet. Gambling is a true variable-ratio system (tables, racecourses, private clubs) that also opens society and underworld circles and liquidates assets. Drink lubricates conversation and dulls fear at the cost of tells. Opiates manage wound pain (P10.07) and become a dependency whose supply tightens with the law. Liaisons are abstract entanglement entries (P10.08) granting access and generating letters, i.e. physical evidence. Habitual consumption makes the agent predictable (P10.10).
- **Directive coverage:** Economy (debt, liquidity) and deception (tells, blackmail) primary. Items: supply and letters. Politics: legal regimes shift (Hague Opium Convention 1912, Harrison Act 1914, Dangerous Drugs Act 1920; Russian prohibition 1914, US Prohibition 1920, absinthe bans). Travel: where supply is legal. Hunt: predictability.
- **Historical grounding:** Over-the-counter morphine and cocaine before regulation, Monte Carlo and private clubs, wartime prohibitions.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The meta-mirror: the player feels variable-ratio reinforcement at the table while watching its cost on the ledger, producing self-aware, remembered play. Vices are also the game's pressure valves: the tension/relief cycle is built in.
- **Engine implications:** Per-vice state (craving, tolerance, supply); law-regime tables by jurisdiction and date; coupling hooks into health, debt and hunt.
- **Risks / open questions:** Taste and ratings; must stay mechanical, never glamorise; players may optimise vices away, so benefits must genuinely tempt.

## P10.07 — The Body Keeps the Books: Health, Injury and Period Medicine
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Health is several stocks (constitution, acute injuries, chronic conditions, infection exposures) with recovery measured in weeks during which neither the world nor the hunt pauses, and period medicine that is slow, expensive and leaves records.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Wounds heal on timers modified by age, care and rest; infection is a probabilistic complication without antibiotics. Chronic conditions (tuberculosis, syphilis, gas-damaged lungs from 1915, an old fracture) are permanent modifiers with flare rules. Cities carry seasonal disease exposure (typhoid; cholera in Russia 1908–10; influenza 1918–20), so routing is also health routing. Treatment is logistics: a sanatorium season buys recovery and concealment for months, money and a registration trail; Salvarsan from 1910 changes the syphilis calculus; X-rays from 1895 reveal concealed injuries; morphine manages pain and starts a habit (P10.06). Prescriptions, bills and case notes are stealable items. Ageing lowers ceilings and lengthens every timer.
- **Directive coverage:** Time pressure primary: convalescence is a forced wait against two other clocks. Travel: health gates class and speed; a sick agent cannot manage third class or a Siberian winter. Economy: medicine costs money. Deception: a limp or scar is an identifier. Politics: war makes wounds likely. Hunt: medical registration.
- **Historical grounding:** No antibiotics until the mid-1930s, Salvarsan 1910, X-rays 1895, sanatorium culture, typhoid inoculation from 1896, the influenza pandemic.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Vulnerability raises stakes; convalescence creates planning tension because the world moves while you lie still; permanent scars give the life a texture the player authored.
- **Engine implications:** Health stocks with timers; per-city seasonal exposure tables; treatment as scheduled multi-week actions coupled to the hunt and travel graphs.
- **Risks / open questions:** Long waits frustrate; design convalescence as a planning phase (correspondence, cipher work, delegated business).

## P10.08 — The Obligations Ledger: Dependants, Pensioners and Creditors as Leverage Lines
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Everyone bound to the player is an abstract obligation entry (demand frequency, cost, loyalty, leverage, failure consequence), and every obligation is at once an asset (access, cover, labour) and a lever the hunters can pull.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Obligations accrue from play: household staff (P10.03), a burnt sub-agent's dependants, a patron expecting favours, a liaison (P10.06), an informant on retainer. Each has a calendar (monthly remittances, school fees, quarterly visits) and a loyalty state that decays when demands are missed. Missed obligations convert into leverage: letters, denunciations, saleable debts of honour. Hunters target the web: pressure on a dependant produces an approach event the player may not detect. The player can discharge an obligation (expensive, final), hide it, or cut it (loyalty turns hostile and leverage activates). No entry is a character: each is a tuple given institutional context.
- **Directive coverage:** Deception (every obligation knows one of your names) and hunt (the web is the hunters' map) primary. Economy: remittances are a fixed cost base. Politics: obligations in enemy territory after 1914 become unreachable or treasonous. Travel: visiting placed obligations is routing. Items: letters as evidence.
- **Historical grounding:** Remittances by postal order and bank draft, boarding schools, secret-service funds, third-party cover addresses, the Okhrana's and Cheka's practice of pressuring relatives.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Sunk cost and guilt expressed as resources; moral weight emerges from the ledger rather than a script; fear of an approach makes the player protective of abstractions: attachment.
- **Engine implications:** Obligation entities with calendars, loyalty decay and leverage payloads referencing legends; hunter target selection over the obligation graph.
- **Risks / open questions:** Must stay humane in presentation without becoming narrative; needs a clear ledger view.

## P10.09 — Debt as the Second Clock
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Debt is a time system, not a negative balance: every borrowing has an instrument (bill of exchange, overdraft, moneylender's note, debt of honour, tradesman's account, a service's advance), a maturity, an interest rule and a jurisdictional enforcement regime, filling the calendar with due-dates independent of the world clock.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player raises money against standing (P10.04) and legends (P10.02). Each instrument has a rollover at rising cost and a default consequence: a protested bill becomes a public record; a gazetted bankruptcy burns the name it was incurred under, expelling it from clubs and credit; an unpaid debt of honour destroys standing in the witnessing circle; an unredeemed service advance converts into coercive tasking. Debts are tradable paper, buyable as leverage by or against the player. Currency regimes are debt events: gold parities make multi-currency debt safe until 1914; exchange controls, rouble collapse and mark hyperinflation then wipe or multiply debts depending on which side the player stands.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy and time pressure primary. Deception: debt under which name. Politics: currency regimes and moratoria. Travel: enforcement is jurisdictional, so fleeing a debt is routing. Hunt: bankruptcy gazettes are public documents.
- **Historical grounding:** Three-month usance bills, quarter-day rents, bankruptcy gazettes, the abolition of imprisonment for debt across Western Europe while bankruptcy remained social ruin, the 1914 moratoria, the 1923 hyperinflation.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** The quarter-day as a dread appointment; leverage as thrill; "making the payment" releases tension on a reliable cadence and teaches deliberate risk.
- **Engine implications:** Debt instruments as scheduled events with rollover branches; jurisdiction tables; a currency index by date.
- **Risks / open questions:** Debt spirals ending runs early; give early warning and make bankruptcy playable, not terminal.

## P10.10 — Routine Is a Trail: The Personal Calendar the Hunters Read (risky)
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** The mundane calendar (rent days, club dinners, doctors' appointments, school terms, letters to a cover address) shares one scheduler with operations and travel, and the hunter AI deduces from habits: regularity makes a legend plausible and the agent catchable; irregularity preserves freedom and thins the legend.
- **How it plays (core loop):** The player keeps appointments to feed legends (P10.02), circles (P10.04) and obligations (P10.08); every kept appointment writes a timestamped witness record. Hunters see, Scotland Yard–style, transport types and periodic reveals, but also mine the pattern: a name dining at the same club every Thursday is an ambush point; a remittance posted on the first of each month is a trace. The player can break pattern (costly to plausibility), delegate (leaky) or lay false routine (expensive, but a trap). Pursuer inference power varies by service and date.
- **Directive coverage:** Hunt and time pressure primary. Deception: plausibility versus predictability is the core dial. Travel: a routine is a route. Economy: standing orders and remittances. Politics: which service hunts, with what powers. Items: letters and tickets as pattern evidence.
- **Historical grounding:** Okhrana external-surveillance logs, hotel registration cadence, postal perlustration, the regularity of bourgeois life (quarter-days, club nights, spa seasons).
- **Why captivating & addictive:** A dilemma that never resolves: what makes you safe socially makes you vulnerable physically. It converts life-sim upkeep into stealth decisions, so nothing is ever "just a chore", and successful misdirection yields unauthored triumphs.
- **Engine implications:** Unified scheduler for personal and operational events; pattern mining over the witness log (periodicity detection, place clustering); pursuer models with varying inference power.
- **Risks / open questions:** Opacity: the player must see their own pattern roughly as hunters might, through an instrument that does not trivialise it.

## P10.11 — The Annual Audit: Life at Three Time Scales
- **Type:** game-defining system
- **Central mechanical hook:** Time runs at three granularities, minutes (conversation, pursuit), days (legs, letters, business) and years, and the year boundary is a ritual audit that batch-applies ageing, rust, compounding, standing decay, legend review, obligation demands and the hunt's baseline rise, and stamps the coming year's known macro-events onto the calendar.
- **How it plays (core loop):** Play proceeds in days and minutes. At each year-end the game pauses into an audit: a ledger showing age and health changes, what rusted, what matured, which names are thin, which circles have forgotten you, what the hunters are believed to hold, what fell due. Here the player decides where to winter, which household to close, which legend to retire, which skill to retrain, which currency to hold, whether to take a sanatorium season. The audit also presents the coming year's known schedule (seasonal closures, conferences, treaty dates, mobilisation risk); unknown events still arrive mid-year.
- **Directive coverage:** Time pressure primary. Travel: seasonal planning around ice closures. Economy: compounding and currency choices. Deception: legend review. Politics: the macro-event calendar. Items: depreciation. Hunt: baseline exposure.
- **Historical grounding:** The social year (spa seasons, parliamentary sessions, trade fairs), annual accounts and quarter-days, the fixed dates of the period's world clock.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Ritual cadence creates natural session boundaries and the strongest "one more year" pull in strategy design; the audit converts diffuse decay into a legible report the player owns; it is also the natural save point.
- **Engine implications:** Batched lazy evaluation at the year boundary; event compaction; deterministic replay from audit snapshots; sparse day-level simulation between audits.
- **Risks / open questions:** The audit must not feel like a tax return; limit it to a few consequential decisions with sharp feedback.

## P10.12 — Exits: Retirement, Ruin, Exile, Capture and Succession (risky)
- **Type:** whole-game concept
- **Central mechanical hook:** Endings are systems of visible preconditions the player steers toward: retirement (unencumbered wealth in a stable currency, a clean name, a non-extraditing jurisdiction), exile, ruin, capture, death, turning (joining a service as its hunter), plus succession: play continues as a protégé inheriting households, debts, standings and the hunters' file.
- **How it plays (core loop):** An exit dashboard shows how near each ending is: wealth by currency and jurisdiction, clean names, the hunters' file size. Retirement requires converting the life over game-years while the hunt continues: selling concessions, closing households, discharging obligations, buying an annuity. Ruin and capture are survivable system states. Succession, chosen or forced by death, resets body and skills but not the world: a system-generated successor inherits a configurable share of assets, every debt and household, standings in circles that knew the predecessor's organisation, and a two-subject hunters' file.
- **Directive coverage:** Economy (liquidating a life) and time pressure (exit windows open and close with the world clock; 1914 and 1917 slam doors) primary. Politics: extradition regimes. Travel: reaching the refuge. Deception: clean names. Items: what persists. Hunt: the inherited file.
- **Historical grounding:** Annuities as retirement instruments, the extradition-treaty patchwork with political-offence exceptions, émigré destinations, the 1917 expropriations and 1923 hyperinflation that destroyed retirements in the wrong currency.
- **Why captivating & addictive:** Goal-gradient on a life scale; legacy and generativity make death a continuation, not a loss, and support roguelike replay; steering toward a chosen exit gives the long game a destination without a story.
- **Engine implications:** Exit conditions as declarative queries over the life record; succession as state transfer under inheritance rules; multi-subject hunter files.
- **Risks / open questions:** Succession must stay abstract; players may game visible thresholds, so some should remain hidden.
