# Finalist Pitch — C17 The Paper Hunter: One Evidence Engine

*Rank 4 of 20 · Borda 155/200 · mean score 7.80 · Lead author P08, audited by P03 (history), P02 (engine) and P09 (hook honesty)*

## One-line pitch
You are never observed, only recorded: every act of a spy's life writes a record into some institution's book — hotel slip, berth sheet, cable copy, bank counterfoil, frontier stamp — and the services hunting you read only what reached their desks, so the game is deciding what the paper may say.

## The hook — what is new
Pursuit games show the hunter a position; this one shows the hunter a filing cabinet. Every record is one tuple — subject, predicate, value, confidence, source institution, time, authorship — and who may read it is derived on the day of asking from C03's cooperation matrix and the archive's survival, never stored; a service's dossier on you is only the records that reached it through its own jurisdiction and liaison channels. Lies, reports and forged fields are the same tuple with a different authorship, so deception and the hunt are one system; every deduction is derived from the log, so every capture can be replayed and learned from. The craft is bookkeeping under fear: which name signs, which book it lands in, how long it lives there, who will open it.

## Core loop
- **Every 30 seconds:** The convenient act writes a record; the awkward one writes none. First class and a grand hotel put your name on a berth sheet and a police slip; third class and gold coin write nothing and cost sleep. A cable leaves a copy at the office for months; a letter takes three days and in pre-war Western Europe leaves nothing — but Russia's black cabinets open watch-listed mail, and from August 1914 every belligerent's censor opens, stamps and logs it: a letter's silence is a row of the dated layer, not a constant. Beside every fare the planner shows a self-forecast — from your own acts and the published lags, never from the hunters' files — of where each record will be, and when.
- **Every 10 minutes:** A leg is planned against the hunters' probable knowledge: the Munich slip reaches the Polizeidirektion tomorrow; the Zurich drawing sits behind a warrant; the trunk still wears the Vienna label. Thin the trail (a bribed clerk, a stolen page — records in themselves), choose which legend signs next, or lay a decoy: a through-ticket never used, an accomplice signing a distant register, a cable from a station you leave by cab.
- **Every session (1–2 hours):** A commission across two or three cities on C07's timetable; a reveal rhythm you can hear — registers nightly, gazettes weekly, circulars monthly; a near-miss when a description lands one node behind you; perhaps a dossier burgled; perhaps an arrest and the autopsy that shows which record did it.
- **Over a campaign (years of game time):** The log never shrinks. Dossiers copy along alliance edges in 1907 and 1914, pass with losses to successor regimes in 1917, reopen under archive reviews; registration regimes thicken from 1914 to 1920, so the same verbs write more paper each year; early carelessness is still filed when you are fifty. A veteran's dossier is thin because the veteran learned which books to stay out of.

## The systems and how they interlock
- **Travel** (network, schedules, fares, classes, traces): Every mode leaves a different mark (P04.05): a named sleeping-car berth is high-fidelity and persistent; a third-class day ticket reveals mode and direction only; a steamer files a passenger list, but Britain's Aliens Act 1905 lists were required only of immigrant ships carrying more than twenty alien steerage passengers, so a Harwich packet filed none and before 1914 the Hook route writes nothing at either end; a cab is remembered for a day. A missed connection (C07) forces an overnight stop and a register. Frontier requirements are rows of C10's dated layer: none in Western Europe before 1914, passports and visas in Russia and Ottoman lands, everywhere afterwards. A record's lag is a delivery on the same clock as the trains, scheduled for subscribed readers and computed on query for everyone else, and the hunters ride the same graph (C05).
- **Deception / conversation / coding**: Which legend signs is the deception verb (C04); dossiers are kept per name, so the hunt advances only by linking two — a witness who saw both faces, one hand in two registers, an endorsed draft, one trunk under two names, a shared cable address, a timetable that could not have produced both records, two services comparing files at liaison. An Assert (C12), a report token (C01) and a forged passport field (C06) are records with authorship set to you; a lie's truth-distance is its gap from the world log, which the clock will test. Cables are copied and letters censored (C20): records with readers.
- **Items / artifacts**: Provenance is the log filtered by item id — issued, bought, stolen, copied, crossed a frontier, pawned, planted — and an inspector knows its institution's view widened by liaison (C06). A labelled trunk is a record on legs; a stolen dossier is an item whose pages are records, and removing one makes a deduction regress.
- **Economy / business**: Money has a signature (C08): a letter of credit draws only at listed correspondents, by the signing legend, against a specimen; a telegraphic transfer leaves a cable copy; gold leaves nothing and weighs. Protested bills and gazetted bankruptcies are public records in the same store, which is how a burned legend's creditor becomes a budgeted private hunter. Decoys cost money and accomplices, who have trails; hunters' ledger queries are funded by a service's annual vote (C01), so your successes pay the clerks who will read you.
- **Politics / warfare**: Readers are political. A record's reader set comes from the cooperation and extradition matrix (service × jurisdiction × offence class × date, C03): a Sûreté lead dies at the Belgian frontier unless Brussels agrees; London shelters offences framed as political under the Extradition Act 1870's exception while ordinary crimes are extraditable to Russia under the 1886 treaty, so how a service characterises your act decides who may read your file. War rewrites readers overnight: censors read current cables as routine, enemy archives close, allied ones open, a fallen regime's files pass on with a destruction fraction.
- **Time pressure & the world clock**: Lag is pressure: a Berlin or Vienna slip reaches the police within about a day; a consular inquiry takes weeks; a cable copy sits for months; a review of retained copies is a rare event the war regime schedules. Scotland Yard's reveal cadence becomes institutional rhythm — hypotheses reweight when registers are collected, gazettes printed, circulars issued — never a visible countdown. The world clock mutates the tables: 31 July 1914 (German passports), 5 August 1914 (British alien registration), February and November 1915 (photographs; compulsory passports), 1920 (the League booklet; general hotel registers in Britain).
- **The hunt (hidden movement, evidence, hunters)**: C05 reads this store and nothing else: weighted hypotheses over each legend's place, time, mode and habit, propagated along the real timetable and reweighted only by delivered records; the hunter profiles you (C14's habit model), queries ledgers where weight concentrates, sits on fixed points, and cordons a city's exits when weight collapses. A fabrication the timetable makes impossible is discarded and raises that service's scepticism against the evidence class. What you see is governed by C16: the own-trail view, always available and silent about what was read; and the windows onto inference — institutional cadence, the press, the stolen dossier, the interrogation room, the autopsy replaying C05's reasoning over these records.
- **The life (ageing, health, households, reputation, legends)**: The life writes paper: a sanatorium hides you and registers you at once; a limp or a scar is a portrait-parlé field that never changes; servants are witnesses with a talk rate; standing per circle crosses bridging institutions as gossip and can link names (C04). Age thins the face in the 1905 photograph, not the height on the card. Capture never ends the life (C11): remand, expulsion, exchange or a sentence that spends years, and the file follows a successor as a two-subject dossier.

## A worked example of play
March 1912, pre-war rules. Under legend A — a British machine-tool traveller — the player copies a shipyard tender in Hamburg during a window posted by an arms firm (C18). The act writes nothing; the week around it writes plenty: a hotel registration form (high fidelity; with the Hamburg police within a day; readable, since the tender is naval, by the Admiralty Staff's intelligence section working with the Hamburg political police), a commercial-code cable to a London cable address from the Hauptbahnhof office (copy retained for months, readable under warrant), a second-class berth on the night boat-train to the Hook of Holland (berth sheet under A), and a first-class landing at Harwich that no Aliens Act list records. In London nobody registers A: in 1912 Britain writes nothing. Ten days later the theft is noticed (a Tier 2 event); the naval section's dossier holds a porter's fading memory and, after a budgeted query of the Hamburg registers, A's name and the hotel's luggage label. Hypotheses propagate along the March timetable to the Hook, Antwerp and Copenhagen; a second query returns the berth sheet and weight collapses onto the Harwich route. A description reaches London by consular channel, but with no register to query and no address it yields a note; the dossier is shelved. The own-trail view shows a danger of the player's own making: the London cable address is also used by legend B, a Swiss national, for bank cables — one tag under two names (C04). They retire the address and move B to letters. On 5 August 1914 every alien must register: A, British, writes nothing until the 1915 Act; B is issued an alien's registration certificate at a police station, to which the wartime Aliens Restriction Orders (consolidated 1916) add a photograph — the February 1915 photograph rule is for British passports, A's document. In 1916 a rare wartime review of retained copies, run by a British service now curious about A for its own reasons (C01), surfaces the 1912 form: A's name above the address B's 1913 bank cables carried. The autopsy, when it comes, shows that page, the two names it joined, and the letter that would have kept them apart.

## Why it is captivating and addictive
The argument is honesty, not stimulation. Attributable consequence: hunters act only on records, so the player always knows which comfort betrayed them, and fairness attribution with controllability is the strongest predictor of persisting after loss: the autopsy shows not only the record that caused the capture but the branch point — the letter that would have kept the names apart — so the player leaves with "I could have", not only "I see why" (P09: "every deduction derivable from one log makes forty hours of pursuit auditable"). Loss aversion over something built and visible: the possession is the own-trail view — a thin, ageing trail the player can see — and every register spends it; the dossier is only inferred through windows, so the loss felt is of what is seen. An information gap without a minimap: what the hunters concluded is visible only through costly windows, so the evening edition is sought voluntarily and relief when it is silent is real; dread is resolved only by priced acts — an edition, a bought clerk, a stolen page — each a relief event on an institutional cadence, so tension and relief cycle for forty hours without going chronic. Zeigarnik tension on a period clock: a slip in transit to the Polizeidirektion is an open loop between sessions, and the own-trail view's lag timers turn that anxiety into anticipation — each record's arrival is a known future event, a date awaited rather than a fog feared; this is the strongest honest hook the design owns. Theory of mind: feeding the hunter a through-ticket you never used and watching a cordon form on the wrong city is the richest agency in the pool, kept an arms race by a scepticism learner whose state is readable through a C16 window — discarded fabrications appear in the autopsy — so the hunter adapts in the open rather than cheating. Mastery in the head: knowing a German inn's Fremdenbuch is collected nightly while a British boarding house keeps none. The stopping points are the hotel register (saving costs cover) and the quiet week before a gazette; players push through because a record is in transit, an edition is hours away, and the night train that outruns tomorrow's slip is on the board.

## Historical grounding
The parameters are the period's procedures. Registration: German and Austrian hotels delivered a Meldezettel or Fremdenzettel to the police, in big cities within about a day; French innkeepers kept registers for police inspection; Russian hotels carried the passport to the police desk; Britain registered nobody before August 1914 — aliens from the Aliens Restriction Act and Order of 5 August 1914, British subjects under the National Registration Act 1915, general hotel registers only under the Aliens Order 1920 — so a British legend can be invented until 1915, a foreign one only until August 1914. Travel paper: named berths, printed passenger lists, Aliens Act 1905 lists only for immigrant ships with more than twenty alien steerage passengers, customs declarations; a change of train, not a bogie exchange, at the Russian gauge break; the Trans-Siberian through from 1903–04. Communications: telegraph offices retained copies, obtainable under warrant; wartime censors read current traffic routinely and retained copies only in rare reviews. Documents: passport-free Western and Central Europe against Russian and Ottoman regimes; the German decree of 31 July 1914 and French measures of early August; British photographs from February 1915 and compulsory passports from November 1915; the League's 1920 booklet. Identification: Bertillonage and the portrait parlé, Scotland Yard's Fingerprint Bureau from 1901, Police Gazette and Steckbrief circulars, half-tone photographs in dailies from the 1900s. Readers: the Okhrana's Paris bureau beside the Sûreté; the Rome 1898 and St Petersburg 1904 protocols; the Extradition Act 1870's political-offence exception beside the Anglo-Russian treaty of 24 November 1886; the Okhrana's abolition in March 1917 with partial archive survival; the Vienna commission of 1923. Clocks: Russian railways on St Petersburg time and the Julian calendar to 31 January 1918; Paris time in France to 1911; Ottoman clocks from sunset — every displayed time passes through a local regime, and a forged paper in the wrong calendar is a tell.

## Engine: dynamic events, decisions, actions, dependencies
Data model: one append-only record store of canonical tuples; legends, institutions, services, places, items and messages are keys into it, and every other candidate's store (C01, C03, C04, C06, C08, C12, C14, C19) is a filtered view, never a copy. The tuple carries its source institution, not a reader list: readers are derived at query time from C03's matrix and archive survival on that date, which lets the 1914 fusion and the 1916 review above open 1912 records to readers that did not exist when they were written. Emission: each act type carries a data-driven rule keyed by jurisdiction and date (coverage, lag distribution, fidelity, persistence, source institution), the dated rows living in C10's parameter layer; delivery events are scheduled only for subscribed readers — active hunters, bourses, censors on gated channels — on C07's global priority queue, so a slip arrives under the same total ordering as a train; every other reader evaluates arrival lazily on query from the same lag rule (C13's form), so circulars and gazettes never post thousands of events each. Reasoning: a dossier is an indexed filter, essentially free (P02.05); C05's hunters propagate bounded, weighted hypotheses on a hierarchical graph that refines only where weight concentrates, reweight at institutional cadence rather than per tick, and spend a budget on queries; bitset reachability keeps dozens of hunters at microseconds (P02.08); link detection is a declarative pass over co-occurring tags; C14's mining runs at ticks, cached; a per-service scepticism learner weights evidence classes. Lazy materialisation applies to the background population only: records tagged to the player and other entities of interest are always written, so the own-trail view and self-forecast read them before any hunter; ledgers nobody queries remain counts — books, not clerks. Determinism: each lag draw is seeded by hashing (seed, record id, reader id, edge id), never from a sequential stream, so lazy and eager evaluation agree and replay is order-independent; with total ordering a save is seed plus input log and replay exact, which lets C16's autopsy replay inference with provenance (record id → reweight → action) — also the designers' balancing instrument. Persistence: archive nodes store event ids; macro-events schedule reviews with per-archive destruction fractions; the record is never pruned, compaction touching derived state only (A-P02-1). Authoring is declarative: emission tables, coverage tables, liaison matrices, cadences, link detectors, scepticism priors. Cost is linear in acts for records and in subscribed readers for deliveries — not acts × readers, since unsubscribed readers evaluate on query.

## Progression and endings (as systems)
What grows: the log; each legend's record-set depth, which is also its credibility and credit limit; the hunters' dossiers; each service's scepticism; the player's competence, visible as a thinner dossier. What decays: witness memory, hypothesis weight without fresh records, hunter budgets without results, dormant dossiers — shelved, not deleted, waking on a linkage or a world-clock change. What accumulates: links between names, alliance copies, archive reviews. Endings are read from the record: capture yields the autopsy and continues the life (interrogation, remand, expulsion, exchange, a sentence that spends years); a burned legend ends a name, not the game; retirement holds only for a name no dossier contains, so file thickness is the retirement score (C11); turning hunter is C05's inverse mode; succession hands on a two-subject file.

## Risks and mitigations
- *"A substrate with few verbs of its own"* (P04, rank 10; P07: "infrastructure rather than a defining concept"; P06: "says less about what the spy does all day"). True of the store, not of the game on it: the verbs are sign, pay, cable, cross, clean, forge, steal the file, lay the decoy, read the windows, and every verb of C02, C04 and C07 resolves into records here. Mitigation is build order: C17 is the engine's first deliverable because it is also save, replay and the debugger (P01: "save, replay and dossier at once"); the career comes from C01 and C18, the clock from C07.
- Thin on economy and politics alone (P10, P02, P03: "the player still needs a career to run on it"). Agreed; they are readers and writers of the store, not its loops.
- Spreadsheet feel: the own-trail view is a map of records with lag timers, never a table; heat is a derived summary only.
- Tuning: persistence and lag between evaporating and inescapable hunts; the autopsy is the balancing tool, and redacted autopsies after near-misses teach without failure.
- Legibility discipline: if the self-forecast leaks what was read, dread dies; the own-record/window boundary (A-P01-2, A-P08-1) is enforced in code.
- Cheap decoys: bounded by the scepticism learner, accomplices' own trails and the timetable check; the learner's adaptations are shown in the autopsy, so adaptation never reads as cheating.
- Table volume: minor jurisdictions take regional defaults; readers are gated by C10's layer, not authored per pair.
- Ignoring paper until punished: the first autopsy is the tutorial; the inverse mode lets players read a file from the hunter's chair first.
